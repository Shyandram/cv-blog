## What you'll learn

- How the probability chain rule turns "model a whole image" into a sequence of "predict the next piece" problems, and how to measure the result in bits per dimension.
- Why training can run in parallel even though sampling is sequential, and how causal masks and masked convolutions keep the answer hidden from its own predictor.
- How PixelRNN and PixelCNN model raw pixels, and why their sequence length eventually pushed the field toward learned tokens.
- How VQ-VAE and VQGAN tokenizers turn an image into a short grid of integers, including the codebook and commitment losses and the straight-through gradient.
- How GPT-style token transformers (Image GPT, Parti, LlamaGen), next-scale prediction (VAR), masked generative models (MaskGIT, Muse) and continuous-token models (MAR) change the unit of prediction.
- How sampling controls (temperature, top-$k$, top-$p$, classifier-free guidance, KV caching) work, and how autoregressive generators compare with diffusion models.

## The big picture

An **autoregressive (AR)** generator builds a sample piece by piece. At each step it looks at everything it has produced so far, predicts a probability distribution for the next piece, draws from it, and appends the result. The "piece" can be a single pixel value, an integer token from a learned codebook, a vector of continuous features, or an entire map at the next resolution.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Imagine drawing a comic one panel at a time. Each new panel must fit the panels already drawn. When you practice, you copy real comics, so you always know what the earlier panels look like. When you invent a new comic, you have to live with your own earlier panels, even the slightly wobbly ones. An autoregressive image model works the same way: practice on real images, then create new ones one piece at a time.
</Callout>

Why does this matter? Autoregression is the same recipe that powers large language models: next-token prediction with a transformer. Bringing images into that recipe lets one architecture read and write text and pictures, reuse the engineering of language-model serving, and measure progress with an exact likelihood. The price is that images are not naturally sequences. Someone has to decide **what a token is** and **in which order tokens are produced**. Most of this chapter is about those two decisions.

Xiong et al.'s survey [1] organizes visual AR models into three families by their prediction unit: **pixel-based**, **token-based** and **scale-based**. We follow that map, then add masked (non-left-to-right) and continuous-token models, which blur the boundary with diffusion. The previous chapters on [generative models](/en/learn/ml/generative-models/) and [diffusion and flow matching](/en/learn/ml/diffusion-flow-matching/) provide the VAE and diffusion background used here; Prince [9] covers the general probabilistic material at textbook level.

## The chain rule needs no independence assumption

**Plain version.** Any joint distribution can be written as a product of "next element given the earlier ones" distributions. That is a fact of probability, not a modeling assumption.

For an ordered sequence $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$,

$$
p_\theta(x)=\prod_{i=1}^N p_\theta(x_i\mid x_{\lt i}),
\qquad
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{NLL}}(\theta)=
-\sum_{i=1}^N\log p_\theta(x_i\mid x_{\lt i}).
$$

Here $x_i$ is the $i$-th element in the chosen order, $x_{\lt i}=(x_1,\ldots,x_{i-1})$ is everything before it, $\theta$ are the network parameters, and $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{NLL}}$ is the negative log-likelihood of one training example (averaged over the dataset in practice).

The chain rule is exact for **any** order. Approximation enters only through the model: a finite network, a limited context window, or a restricted form for each conditional. For discrete values, each conditional is usually a softmax over the possible values; binary pixels can use a Bernoulli distribution; continuous features need a density or another conditional generator (we meet one in the MAR section).

An order is a computational choice, not a claim about physics. **Raster order** (left to right, top to bottom) is convenient but arbitrary for a two-dimensional image: the pixel below you is just as related as the pixel to your right. Different orders lead to different conditionals, and a finite model may find some orders easier to learn than others. Figure 1 shows three prediction units used in this chapter.

### Measuring an AR model: bits per dimension

Because the chain rule gives an exact $\log p_\theta(x)$, AR models are usually compared by **negative log-likelihood in bits per dimension (bpd)**:

$$
\mathrm{bpd}(x)=\frac{-\log_2 p_\theta(x)}{D},
$$

where $D$ is the number of discrete values in the image (for example $D=3\cdot32\cdot32$ for a $32\times32$ RGB image) and $\log_2$ converts nats to bits. A model that assigned every 8-bit value a uniform probability would score exactly 8 bpd; anything lower means the model has learned structure. Bpd is measured on held-out images, so it detects overfitting as well as underfitting.

Two cautions. First, bpd and visual quality can disagree: a model can spend most of its likelihood on imperceptible noise while still making odd global mistakes. Second, for a **token** model the likelihood is over codebook indices, not pixels. A lossy tokenizer throws information away, so a token-level likelihood is not an exact pixel-space likelihood and cannot be compared directly with pixel-model bpd.

## Teacher forcing, causal masks and exposure bias

**Plain version.** During training the model always sees the real history; during sampling it sees its own history. A mask makes sure training never lets a position peek at its own answer.

With **teacher forcing**, every conditional in the training loss is computed using the ground-truth previous elements. Since the whole training image is available, all $N$ conditionals can be evaluated in a single forward pass, as long as position $i$ is prevented from reading positions $\ge i$. A causal transformer enforces this with an attention mask; a PixelCNN enforces it with masked convolution kernels.

Two input conventions are common, and the mask must match the convention:

- In a **shifted-input** decoder, feed `[BOS, x1, x2, ...]` and predict `[x1, x2, x3, ...]`. A lower-triangular mask that *includes* the diagonal is correct, because the input at position $i$ is the *previous* element.
- In a model that receives **unshifted** values, the dependency must be **strictly** earlier. Including the diagonal would let the network copy the value it is supposed to predict.

At inference, earlier sampled mistakes enter the context of later steps. This training/inference mismatch is often called **exposure bias**. It does not make maximum likelihood wrong (the factorization is still exact), but it matters in practice for imperfect models and long sequences: a small early error in the layout of an image can push later steps into contexts that never occurred in training.

## Pixel models: PixelRNN and PixelCNN

**Plain version.** Treat each pixel's intensity as a 256-way classification problem whose input is all pixels above and to the left.

PixelRNN and PixelCNN were introduced together by van den Oord, Kalchbrenner and Kavukcuoglu [2]. Both model each 8-bit value with a **256-way softmax** rather than a Gaussian, so the distribution can be multimodal (a pixel can be "either black or white, but not grey"). Colour is handled by a fixed order inside each pixel, red then green then blue, so that green may depend on red and blue on both. PixelRNN uses two-dimensional LSTM layers (Row LSTM and Diagonal BiLSTM) whose dependency range is potentially unbounded; PixelCNN replaces them with stacked masked convolutions, which have a bounded receptive field but train much faster because all positions are computed in parallel.

### Masked convolutions

A masked convolution multiplies its kernel by a binary mask that zeros the weights at "future" positions. In [2], **mask A** is used only in the first layer: it excludes the centre pixel, so the first layer never sees the value it predicts (and, for colour, only sees channels already predicted). **Mask B** is used in all later layers: it allows the centre position, because after the first layer the centre feature already summarizes only earlier pixels. Formally, for a $k\times k$ kernel with centre $(c,c)$, $c=\lfloor k/2\rfloor$,

$$
M_{uv}=\begin{cases}
1 & u\lt c,\\
1 & u=c\ \text{and}\ v\lt c\ (\text{A})\ \text{or}\ v\le c\ (\text{B}),\\
0 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
$$

where $u$ indexes kernel rows and $v$ kernel columns. The code below builds both masks, stacks five layers, and uses a gradient to find exactly which input pixels can influence the output at the centre of a $9\times9$ image.

```python
import torch
from torch import nn
from torch.nn import functional as F

class MaskedConv2d(nn.Conv2d):
    """PixelCNN-style masked convolution for one channel (raster order)."""
    def __init__(self, cin, cout, k, mask_type):
        super().__init__(cin, cout, k, padding=k//2, bias=False)
        mask = torch.ones(k, k)
        c = k//2
        mask[c, c + (mask_type == "B"):] = 0   # centre (type A) and pixels to its right
        mask[c+1:] = 0                         # all rows below
        self.register_buffer("mask", mask)
    def forward(self, x):
        return F.conv2d(x, self.weight*self.mask, padding=self.padding)

torch.manual_seed(0)
layers = [MaskedConv2d(1, 8, 3, "A"), nn.Tanh()]
for _ in range(4):                            # 5 layers: reach of 5 pixels
    layers += [MaskedConv2d(8, 8, 3, "B"), nn.Tanh()]
net = nn.Sequential(*layers, MaskedConv2d(8, 1, 1, "B"))
print(net[0].mask.int())
x = torch.rand(1, 1, 9, 9, requires_grad=True)
net(x)[0, 0, 4, 4].backward()                 # logit for pixel (row 4, col 4)
dep = (x.grad[0, 0].abs() > 0).int()
print(dep)
later = torch.zeros(9, 9, dtype=torch.bool)
later[4, 4:] = True; later[5:] = True
assert dep[later].sum() == 0                  # never reads itself or later pixels
print("earlier pixels never seen (blind spot):", int(((dep == 0) & ~later).sum()))
```

The assertion passes: no pixel at or after the target influences its logit, so the model is a valid autoregressive factorization. But the printout reveals something else. Six pixels that come *earlier* in raster order, in a staircase above and to the right of the target, have zero gradient. This is the **blind spot** of stacked masked convolutions (Figure 2): each layer's receptive field grows up and to the left, but the region up and to the right is never reached. The model is still correct, just less informed than it could be.

<Figure src={maskedConvFig} alt="Two 5 by 5 binary masks labeled type A and type B, and a 9 by 9 dependency map showing visible earlier pixels in blue and a staircase-shaped blind spot in orange." caption="Figure 2 — Left and centre: mask A excludes the centre weight; mask B keeps it. Right: input pixels that influence the prediction at the dark cell after five stacked 3×3 masked layers, measured with a gradient. Orange cells come earlier in raster order but are never seen: the blind spot." />

The Gated PixelCNN of van den Oord et al. [3] removed the blind spot by splitting the network into two stacks: a **vertical stack** that sees all rows above the current one without restriction and a **horizontal stack** that sees the current row up to the current pixel, combined after each layer. It also replaced ReLU with a gated unit $y=\tanh(W_f\ast x)\odot\sigma(W_g\ast x)$, where $\ast$ is convolution, $\sigma$ the logistic sigmoid and $\odot$ elementwise multiplication, and showed how to condition the model on class labels or embeddings. With these changes PixelCNN matched PixelRNN's likelihood on ImageNet [23] at much lower cost [3].

### Code: a minimal autoregressive model

Masked convolutions are one way to enforce causality; a transformer is not required either. The following complete CPU experiment generates $4\times4$ binary images whose rows all repeat the same four random bits. A single masked linear layer, whose strictly lower-triangular mask excludes the current pixel and all later ones, learns the conditionals directly.

```python
import torch
from torch import nn
from torch.nn import functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
class BinaryAR(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n=16):
        super().__init__()
        self.weight = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(n, n))
        self.bias = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(n))
        self.register_buffer("mask", torch.tril(torch.ones(n, n), diagonal=-1))
    def forward(self, x):
        return F.linear(x, self.weight*self.mask, self.bias)

model = BinaryAR()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=.04)
for step in range(600):
    first_row = torch.randint(2, (128, 4)).float()
    x = first_row.repeat(1, 4)
    loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(model(x), x)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():
    a = torch.rand(2, 16)
    b = a.clone(); b[:, 8:] = torch.rand(2, 8)
    assert torch.allclose(model(a)[:, :9], model(b)[:, :9])
    samples = torch.zeros(8, 16)
    for i in range(16):
        p = model(samples)[:, i].sigmoid()
        samples[:, i] = torch.bernoulli(p)
    rows = samples.reshape(-1, 4, 4)
    print("one generated image:\n", rows[0].int())
    print("repeat agreement:", (rows[:, 1:] == rows[:, :1]).float().mean().item())
```

The assertion checks causality: changing positions 8 onward cannot alter the logits for positions 0 through 8, because each of those outputs reads only earlier inputs. Training then drives the agreement between later rows and the first row to 1.0, while the first row stays random (the model must not memorize one particular row).

The true distribution has 16 equally likely images, so its entropy is $\log_2 16=4$ bits per image, or 0.25 bits per pixel. Later rows contain no new uncertainty. A model that treated all 16 pixels as independent fair coins would need 16 bits per image. The gap between 16 and 4 bits is exactly the structure an autoregressive model captures and an independent-pixel model cannot.

### Why pixel models stopped scaling

A $256\times256$ RGB image has $3\cdot256\cdot256=196{,}608$ values, so pixel-level sampling needs about two hundred thousand sequential network evaluations. Most of the likelihood is also spent on fine, locally predictable detail, while the global layout that people notice is a small fraction of the bits. Image GPT [11] showed that a plain sequence transformer trained to predict pixels autoregressively on low-resolution images learns features that transfer well under linear probing, but the pixel route stays expensive at higher resolutions. The next step was to learn a shorter, more meaningful sequence.

## Discrete visual tokens: VQ-VAE and VQGAN

**Plain version.** Train a separate network that compresses an image into a small grid of "words" from a learned dictionary, then do autoregression over the words instead of the pixels.

### VQ-VAE

VQ-VAE [4] has an encoder that produces a feature vector $z_e(x)\in\mathbb R^d$ at each position of a downsampled grid, a **codebook** $\{e_1,\ldots,e_K\}\subset\mathbb R^d$ of $K$ learned vectors, and a decoder. Each feature is replaced by its nearest code:

$$
k^*=\arg\min_k\|z_e(x)-e_k\|_2^2,\qquad z_q(x)=e_{k^*}.
$$

The image is now represented by the integer grid of $k^*$ values. Training minimizes

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{VQ}}=
\underbrace{\|x-D(z_q)\|^2}_{\text{reconstruction}}
+\underbrace{\|\mathrm{sg}[z_e]-e_{k^*}\|^2}_{\text{codebook}}
+\beta\underbrace{\|z_e-\mathrm{sg}[e_{k^*}]\|^2}_{\text{commitment}},
$$

where $D$ is the decoder, $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ is the **stop-gradient** operator (identity in the forward pass, zero gradient in the backward pass), and $\beta$ is a small weight on the commitment term (the snippet below uses 0.25). The reconstruction term is written as squared error here; any reconstruction likelihood can take its place.

The three terms do three jobs. The **codebook** term moves the chosen code toward the encoder output, like a k-means update. The **commitment** term pulls the encoder toward its code so that encoder outputs do not drift far from the codebook. The `argmin` has no useful gradient, so the reconstruction gradient reaches the encoder through the **straight-through estimator**: in the forward pass use $z_q$, in the backward pass copy the gradient from $z_q$ to $z_e$ as if quantization were the identity. In code this is the one-liner `z_q = z_e + (e - z_e).detach()`.

The toy below trains a 2-D "encoder", codebook and decoder on four clusters, with eight codes available:

```python
import torch
from torch import nn
from torch.nn import functional as F

def quantize(z_e, codebook, beta=0.25):
    """z_e: (n, d) encoder outputs; codebook: (K, d). Returns z_q with straight-through gradient."""
    dist = torch.cdist(z_e, codebook)            # (n, K) Euclidean distances
    idx = dist.argmin(1)                         # nearest code index per vector
    e = codebook[idx]
    codebook_loss = F.mse_loss(e, z_e.detach())  # moves codes toward encoder outputs
    commit_loss = F.mse_loss(z_e, e.detach())    # keeps the encoder near its code
    z_q = z_e + (e - z_e).detach()               # forward: e; backward: identity
    return z_q, idx, codebook_loss + beta*commit_loss

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
centres = torch.tensor([[2., 0.], [-2., 0.], [0., 2.], [0., -2.]])
encoder = nn.Linear(2, 2)                        # toy "encoder" and "decoder"
decoder = nn.Linear(2, 2)
codebook = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(8, 2)*.1)    # K = 8 codes, 4 are enough
opt = torch.optim.Adam([*encoder.parameters(), *decoder.parameters(), codebook], lr=.03)
for step in range(800):
    x = centres[torch.randint(4, (256,))] + .1*torch.randn(256, 2)
    z_q, idx, vq_loss = quantize(encoder(x), codebook)
    loss = F.mse_loss(decoder(z_q), x) + vq_loss
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():
    x = centres.repeat_interleave(100, 0) + .1*torch.randn(400, 2)
    z_q, idx, _ = quantize(encoder(x), codebook)
    print("reconstruction MSE:", round(F.mse_loss(decoder(z_q), x).item(), 4))
    print("codes used:", idx.unique().numel(), "of", len(codebook))
    print("codes per cluster:", [idx[i*100:(i+1)*100].unique().tolist() for i in range(4)])
z = torch.randn(3, 2, requires_grad=True)
z_q, _, _ = quantize(z, codebook.detach())
z_q.sum().backward()
print("straight-through gradient:", z.grad[0].tolist())
```

It prints a reconstruction MSE of about 0.0096, uses **4 of the 8 codes** (one per cluster), and shows a straight-through gradient of `[1.0, 1.0]`. Two lessons hide in those numbers. The reconstruction error is almost exactly the within-cluster noise variance $0.1^2=0.01$: quantization has kept *which cluster* and discarded *where inside the cluster*. And half the codebook is **dead**: codes that are never selected receive no codebook gradient and never move (Figure 3). In real tokenizers, low codebook usage wastes capacity and is a standard failure to monitor.

### Two stages: a tokenizer, then a prior

A VQ-VAE alone is an autoencoder; it does not by itself say which token grids are likely. Generation therefore has **two stages** (Figure 4). Stage 1 trains the tokenizer. Stage 2 freezes it, converts every training image into its index grid, and trains an autoregressive model $p_\psi(k_1,\ldots,k_M)$ over the $M$ indices. To sample, draw an index grid from the prior and decode it. In [4] the prior was a PixelCNN over codes; VQ-VAE-2 [10] used a hierarchy of two code maps (a coarse top level and a finer bottom level) with PixelCNN-style priors and produced diverse, high-fidelity ImageNet samples.

<Figure src={vqPipelineFig} alt="Block diagram: image to encoder to 16 by 16 feature grid to nearest-code lookup to decoder to reconstruction, with a branch from the lookup to 256 integer indices feeding a stage-2 transformer prior." caption="Figure 4 — Two-stage generation. The tokenizer (top row) is trained for reconstruction; the prior (bottom right) only ever sees integer indices. With factor-16 downsampling, a 256×256 image becomes 256 tokens instead of 65,536 pixel positions." />

The arithmetic explains the appeal. If a tokenizer downsamples a $256\times256$ image by 16 in each direction, the prior sees $16\times16=256$ tokens rather than 65,536 pixel positions (or 196,608 colour values). Self-attention cost grows with the square of sequence length, so this is the difference between feasible and infeasible.

### VQGAN: perceptual tokens

Esser, Rombach and Ommer's VQGAN [5] kept the two-stage design but trained the tokenizer with a **perceptual loss** and a **patch-based adversarial loss** in addition to reconstruction, and modelled the token grid with a transformer. The adversarial and perceptual terms let the decoder produce sharp, realistic texture from very few tokens, because the tokens no longer need to encode every pixel exactly; they need to encode enough for the decoder to produce *a* plausible version. This is why VQGAN-style tokenizers became the default front end for token-based generation. Note the division of labour: the **tokenizer** uses adversarial training, but the **prior** is an ordinary autoregressive model trained by maximum likelihood.

### Codebook problems and alternatives

Real tokenizers face three recurring problems: dead codes (as in the toy), a gap between what the tokenizer reconstructs and what the prior can model, and loss of small details such as text and thin lines. Several designs respond to them:

- **ViT-VQGAN** [12] uses a Vision Transformer encoder/decoder, looks codes up in a low-dimensional **factorized** space and **$\ell_2$-normalizes** codes and features, which improved codebook usage and reconstruction.
- **RQ-VAE** [15] uses **residual quantization**: each position is represented by a short stack of codes, each one quantizing what the previous ones missed. This allows a coarser spatial grid (shorter sequence) at similar fidelity.
- **FSQ** [16] (finite scalar quantization) projects to a handful of dimensions and rounds each to a few fixed levels. The implicit codebook is the product of the levels; there is no learned codebook to collapse and no commitment loss.

Compression always creates a bottleneck. Measure codebook usage, reconstruction quality and the generator's quality **separately**: a generator cannot produce detail its tokenizer cannot represent.

## Autoregressive transformers over tokens

**Plain version.** Once an image is a sequence of integers, generating it is the same problem as generating text: a decoder-only transformer predicts the next token.

### From pixels to tokens

Image GPT [11] applied a GPT-style transformer directly to sequences of low-resolution pixels. Its main finding was about **representation learning**: features from a model trained only to predict the next pixel transfer well when a linear classifier is trained on top. Working on tokens instead of pixels shortens sequences enough to generate at higher resolution. The VQGAN transformer [5] is the classic example: a GPT-like model over VQGAN indices in raster order, which let a transformer synthesize high-resolution images that would be far too long as pixel sequences.

### Text-to-image as translation: Parti

Parti [13] treats text-to-image generation like machine translation. A transformer **encoder** reads the text prompt; a transformer **decoder** autoregressively generates ViT-VQGAN image tokens [12] conditioned on it. The report scales this sequence-to-sequence model up to 20 billion parameters and introduces PartiPrompts, a benchmark of more than 1,600 English prompts spanning many categories of content.

### Language-model architecture, unchanged: LlamaGen

LlamaGen [14] asks whether a vanilla language-model architecture, with no image-specific inductive biases, can generate images well when scaled. It uses a Llama-style [24] decoder (RMSNorm pre-normalization, SwiGLU feed-forward layers, 2-D rotary position embeddings) over the tokens of a factor-16 VQ tokenizer with $K=16{,}384$ codes of dimension 8. The class label or projected text embedding is supplied as a **prefix** token, and generation proceeds in raster order. The paper releases class-conditional models from 111M to 3.1B parameters and reports that standard LLM serving software (vLLM) speeds up inference. Its broader message is architectural reuse: an image generator can share almost everything with a text model except the tokenizer.

### Classifier-free guidance for autoregressive models

Classifier-free guidance (CFG) [18] was introduced for diffusion models, but it transfers directly to AR logits. During training, the condition $c$ is randomly replaced by a learned **null** embedding so that one network learns both conditional and unconditional predictions. At each sampling step, compute both sets of logits and combine them:

$$
\ell_{\mathrm{g}}=\ell_{\mathrm u}+s\,(\ell_{\mathrm c}-\ell_{\mathrm u}),
$$

where $\ell_{\mathrm c}$ are the next-token logits with the condition, $\ell_{\mathrm u}$ the logits with the null condition, and $s$ the guidance scale; $s=1$ recovers the ordinary conditional model. This is the form LlamaGen uses [14]. Larger $s$ usually improves prompt or class adherence and sample sharpness at the cost of diversity. It also doubles the per-step compute unless the two passes are batched, so step counts alone understate cost.

## Next-scale prediction (VAR)

**Plain version.** Instead of predicting the next token in a fixed reading order, predict the next, sharper version of the whole image, conditioned on all coarser versions.

Raster order forces a strange dependency: the top-left of an image is generated without any knowledge of the bottom-right. VAR (Visual AutoRegressive modeling) [6] argues that this one-directional order conflicts with the two-directional correlations of image tokens and with spatial locality, and replaces it with a **coarse-to-fine** order over token maps $r_1,\ldots,r_K$ of increasing resolution:

$$
p(r_1,\ldots,r_K)=\prod_{k=1}^K p(r_k\mid r_1,\ldots,r_{k-1}),
$$

where $r_k$ is an $h_k\times w_k$ grid of token indices, $r_1$ is $1\times1$ and $r_K$ has the full latent resolution. All tokens of $r_k$ are predicted **in parallel** given the coarser maps, so the number of sequential steps equals the number of scales, not the number of tokens.

The tokenizer is part of the method. VAR's multi-scale VQ encoder works **residually**: starting from the encoder feature map $f$, for $k=1,\ldots,K$ it downsamples $f$ to $h_k\times w_k$, quantizes it to get $r_k$ (with one codebook shared across scales), upsamples the looked-up codes back to full resolution, passes them through a small convolution $\phi_k$, and subtracts the result from $f$ before moving to the next scale [6]. Each scale therefore encodes what the coarser scales have not explained. Simply resizing ordinary token maps would not reproduce this.

During training, a **block-wise causal** attention mask lets every token of $r_k$ attend to all tokens of $r_{\le k}$ but none of the finer scales; at inference a KV cache stores the coarser scales. VAR uses CFG as well. On class-conditional ImageNet $256\times256$ the paper reports that VAR improves its AR baseline's FID from 18.65 to 1.73, with faster inference, and that its models follow power-law scaling trends similar to those of language models [6]. VAR received a NeurIPS 2024 Best Paper award. As always, those numbers apply to the paper's data, resolution and training protocol.

Parallel prediction within a scale is itself an assumption: tokens in $r_k$ are sampled independently given $r_{\lt k}$. The masked-model section shows what that assumption can cost on a toy problem.

## Masked generative models (MaskGIT and Muse)

**Plain version.** Start with a grid of blanks. At each round, guess every blank at once, keep only the guesses you are most confident about, and blank out the rest again.

MaskGIT [8] uses the same VQ tokens as an AR model, but a **bidirectional** transformer that sees every position. Training is a masked-token objective: choose a mask ratio, replace that fraction of tokens with a special `[MASK]` token, and minimize cross-entropy on the masked positions,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{mask}}=-\mathbb E\Big[\sum_{i\in\mathcal M}\log p_\theta(y_i\mid y_{\bar{\mathcal M}})\Big],
$$

where $y$ is the token grid, $\mathcal M$ is the set of masked positions and $y_{\bar{\mathcal M}}$ the unmasked tokens. The mask ratio is drawn as $\gamma(r)$ with $r\sim\mathcal U(0,1)$ and a **mask schedule** $\gamma$ that decreases from 1 to 0.

Generation runs for $T$ iterations. At iteration $t$ the model predicts all masked tokens in parallel and samples them; each sample's probability serves as its **confidence**. The number of tokens that remain masked after the iteration is

$$
n_t=\big\lceil\gamma(t/T)\,N\big\rceil,
$$

with $N$ the total number of tokens, so the $n_t$ least confident new tokens are re-masked and everything else is fixed. MaskGIT compares several schedules and selects the **cosine** schedule $\gamma(r)=\cos(\pi r/2)$, which fixes few tokens early (when the model knows little) and many late; it generates $256\times256$ images in 8 iterations with a 16×16 token grid and a 1,024-entry codebook [8]. Muse [17] scales the same idea to text-to-image generation, conditioning the masked-token transformer on text embeddings from a pre-trained large language model.

MaskGIT is **not** ordinary left-to-right chain-rule autoregression: the order is chosen on the fly by confidence, and several tokens are sampled in the same step. Those simultaneous tokens are drawn **independently** given the current context. The toy below shows when that matters. It trains a small bidirectional network on the repeated-row $4\times4$ images from earlier and decodes with the cosine schedule.

```python
import math
import torch
from torch import nn
from torch.nn import functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
N, MASK = 16, 2                                   # 16 binary tokens; state 2 = [MASK]
gamma = lambda r: math.cos(math.pi*r/2)          # cosine schedule: fraction still masked
net = nn.Sequential(nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(3*N, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, N))

def data(n):                                      # 4x4 images whose rows repeat
    return torch.randint(2, (n, 4)).repeat(1, 4)

def logits(tokens):                               # sees every position: bidirectional
    return net(F.one_hot(tokens, 3).float())

opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for step in range(1500):
    x = data(256)
    ratio = torch.tensor([gamma(r) for r in torch.rand(256).tolist()])
    n_mask = (ratio*N).ceil().clamp(min=1)
    masked = torch.rand(256, N).argsort(1) < n_mask[:, None]   # random positions
    inp = torch.where(masked, MASK, x)
    loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits(inp)[masked], x[masked].float())
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

@torch.no_grad()
def generate(n, T=4):
    tokens = torch.full((n, N), MASK)
    for t in range(1, T+1):
        p = logits(tokens).sigmoid()
        sample = torch.bernoulli(p).long()
        conf = torch.where(sample == 1, p, 1-p)               # prob. of the drawn value
        still = tokens == MASK
        conf = torch.where(still, conf, torch.inf)            # never re-mask fixed tokens
        proposal = torch.where(still, sample, tokens)
        n_keep_masked = math.floor(gamma(t/T)*N)              # 0 after the last step
        order = conf.argsort(1)                               # least confident first
        remask = torch.zeros_like(still)
        remask.scatter_(1, order[:, :n_keep_masked], True)
        tokens = torch.where(remask, MASK, proposal)
    return tokens

for T in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16):
    rows = generate(1000, T).reshape(-1, 4, 4)
    agree = (rows[:, 1:] == rows[:, :1]).float().mean().item()
    valid = (rows == rows[:, :1]).all(2).all(1).float().mean().item()
    print(f"T={T:2d}  repeat agreement={agree:.3f}  valid images={valid:.3f}")
```

(The code uses `floor` rather than the paper's ceiling so that the count reaches exactly zero at $t=T$ in this tiny example.) The output is:

```text
T= 1  repeat agreement=0.511  valid images=0.000
T= 2  repeat agreement=0.812  valid images=0.100
T= 4  repeat agreement=0.908  valid images=0.360
T= 8  repeat agreement=0.998  valid images=0.982
T=16  repeat agreement=1.000  valid images=0.999
```

With $T=1$ every token is sampled independently from the all-mask context, so rows almost never agree. With more iterations, the first few tokens are fixed early and later ones can condition on them; by $T=8$ nearly every sample is valid. On natural images the dependence between tokens decoded in the same round is weaker, which is why a handful of iterations works, but the trade-off between **fewer steps** and **more independence assumptions** is real (Figure 5).

Right: fraction of valid toy samples rising from 0 at T=1 to nearly 1 at T=8 and 16." caption="Figure 5 — Left: candidate mask schedules γ(r). Centre: with the cosine schedule, 256 tokens and T = 8, few tokens are fixed early and many late. ## Continuous tokens and diffusion conditionals (MAR)

**Plain version.** Autoregression decides *what comes next*; a tiny diffusion model can decide *how to sample* it, so the tokens no longer need to be integers.

Vector quantization is lossy and awkward to train. Li et al. [7] observe that autoregression needs a way to model $p(x_i\mid x_{\lt i})$, not necessarily a categorical distribution. They let the AR (or masked AR) transformer output a conditioning vector $z_i$ for each continuous-valued token $x_i$, and train a small MLP denoiser $\epsilon_\theta$ with a **diffusion loss**:

$$
\mathcal L(z_i,x_i)=\mathbb E_{\epsilon,t}\big\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_{i,t}\mid t,z_i)\big\|^2,
\qquad x_{i,t}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_i+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,
$$

where $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, $t$ is a diffusion time step and $\bar\alpha_t$ the noise schedule from [ML 08](/en/learn/ml/diffusion-flow-matching/). The gradient also flows into the transformer through $z_i$. To sample a token, run the reverse diffusion process of the small denoiser conditioned on $z_i$; a temperature scales the injected noise. The paper's main variant, **MAR** (masked autoregressive), uses a random order and predicts a set of tokens per step, combining the outer structure of MaskGIT with an inner continuous sampler [7].

Consequently, "AR versus diffusion" is not an either/or choice. A system can decide the order and grouping of tokens autoregressively and sample each group by diffusion. Likewise, a language decoder can autoregressively generate *text about* an image without generating any pixels; the unifying idea is factorization, not a particular output type.

## Sampling, temperature and caching

**Plain version.** The trained model gives a distribution at every step; the sampler decides how adventurous to be when drawing from it, and the cache avoids recomputing the past.

For logits $\ell_k$ over $K$ possible values, **temperature** $T$ rescales the distribution:

$$
p_T(k)=\frac{\exp(\ell_k/T)}{\sum_{j=1}^K\exp(\ell_j/T)}.
$$

Lower $T$ concentrates probability on the top values; larger $T$ spreads it (Figure 6). **Top-$k$** sampling keeps only the $k$ highest logits; **top-$p$** (nucleus) sampling keeps the smallest set of values whose probability mass reaches $p$. Both renormalize what remains.

```python
import torch

def filtered_probs(logits, temperature=1.0, top_k=None, top_p=None):
    """Return the (renormalized) distribution actually sampled from."""
    logits = logits / temperature
    if top_k is not None:                               # keep the k largest logits
        kth = logits.topk(top_k).values[..., -1, None]
        logits = logits.masked_fill(logits < kth, -torch.inf)
    if top_p is not None:                               # smallest set with mass >= p
        sorted_logits, order = logits.sort(descending=True)
        cum = sorted_logits.softmax(-1).cumsum(-1)
        drop = cum - sorted_logits.softmax(-1) >= top_p  # mass before this token already >= p
        sorted_logits = sorted_logits.masked_fill(drop, -torch.inf)
        logits = torch.empty_like(logits).scatter(-1, order, sorted_logits)
    return logits.softmax(-1)

logits = torch.tensor([2.0, 1.0, 0.0, -1.0])
for name, kw in [("T=1", {}), ("T=0.5", {"temperature": .5}), ("T=2", {"temperature": 2.}),
                 ("top-k=2", {"top_k": 2}), ("top-p=0.8", {"top_p": .8})]:
    p = filtered_probs(logits, **kw)
    entropy = -(p[p > 0]*p[p > 0].log2()).sum().item()
    print(f"{name:10s}", [round(v, 3) for v in p.tolist()], f"entropy={entropy:.2f} bits")
```

The model's own distribution ($T=1$) has 1.37 bits of entropy; $T=0.5$ lowers it to 0.66 bits and $T=2$ raises it to 1.80 bits. Top-$k=2$ and top-$p=0.8$ both keep the two most likely values here (the first value has mass 0.644, which is below 0.8, so the second is needed). Every one of these changes the distribution you sample from, so a sampler's visual quality should not be confused with the model's likelihood.

### The KV cache

In a causal transformer, the keys and values of earlier tokens do not change when a new token is appended. A **KV cache** stores them, so each new step computes the query, key and value of the newest token only and attends to the cache:

```python
import torch
from torch.nn import functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
d, n = 8, 6
Wq, Wk, Wv = (torch.randn(d, d) for _ in range(3))
x = torch.randn(n, d)                                 # embeddings of n tokens

# Full causal attention over the whole sequence (what training computes).
q, k, v = x@Wq, x@Wk, x@Wv
mask = torch.ones(n, n).tril().bool()
scores = (q@k.T/d**.5).masked_fill(~mask, -torch.inf)
full = scores.softmax(-1)@v

# Incremental decoding: each step computes q, k, v for the newest token only.
K_cache, V_cache, outputs = [], [], []
for i in range(n):
    K_cache.append(x[i]@Wk); V_cache.append(x[i]@Wv)
    K, V = torch.stack(K_cache), torch.stack(V_cache)
    att = F.softmax((x[i]@Wq)@K.T/d**.5, -1)        # attends to tokens 0..i
    outputs.append(att@V)
assert torch.allclose(full, torch.stack(outputs), atol=1e-5)
print("cached decoding matches full causal attention")
print("dot products per step:", [i+1 for i in range(n)], "total:", n*(n+1)//2)
```

The assertion confirms that cached decoding gives exactly the training-time causal outputs (for a single layer here; real models cache every layer and head). The cache removes recomputation but not the sequential dependency, and it grows linearly with sequence length. Step $i$ still attends to $i$ cached keys, so total attention work over a sequence of length $n$ is $n(n+1)/2$, quadratic in $n$. This is why shorter sequences (better tokenizers) and fewer sequential steps (next-scale or masked decoding) matter so much.

For video, tokens can be ordered over space and time or grouped into chunks. Caching, compression and temporal ordering then dominate cost, and object consistency and long-duration drift need their own evaluation.

## Autoregressive versus diffusion

**Plain version.** Both families turn one hard generation problem into many easier ones. AR splits the *image* into pieces; diffusion splits the *noise level* into steps.

| Aspect | Autoregressive (token / scale) | Masked generative | Diffusion / flow ([ML 08](/en/learn/ml/diffusion-flow-matching/)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is generated per step | One token (or one scale) | A confidence-chosen set of tokens | A refinement of the whole sample |
| Sequential steps | Tokens or scales | Fixed, typically few ($T$) | Solver steps (NFE) |
| Typical representation | Discrete codes (or continuous with a diffusion head [7]) | Discrete codes | Continuous pixels or latents |
| Training objective | Exact NLL (cross-entropy) | Masked cross-entropy | Denoising / velocity regression (ELBO-related) |
| Likelihood | Exact over tokens | Not a single exact chain-rule likelihood | Bound or ODE-based estimate |
| Natural strengths | Shares architecture and serving with LLMs; mixes with text | Few steps; easy inpainting and editing | Strong image quality; flexible guidance |
| Typical weaknesses | Long sequential decoding; tokenizer bottleneck; error accumulation | Independence within a step | Many network evaluations unless distilled |

Three points keep this table honest. First, the families mix: MAR [7] puts a diffusion model inside an AR loop, and latent diffusion also depends on a learned tokenizer-like autoencoder. Second, claims that one family "beats" the other (as in LlamaGen's title [14] or VAR's comparison with DiT [6]) hold for a stated benchmark, tokenizer, model size and guidance setting, not universally. Third, cost should be compared in network evaluations, guidance passes, sequence length and actual latency, not in "steps" alone.

## Modern view

The field has moved from raw pixels to learned representations, from fixed raster order to flexible prediction units, and from separate image generators to models that read and write both text and images. Several surveys map this territory:

- **Xiong et al. [1]** (TMLR) is the most direct survey of visual autoregression. Its taxonomy by representation (pixel-, token- and scale-based models) is the one used in this chapter, and it covers applications in image, video, 3D and multimodal generation as well as embodied and medical settings.
- **Bond-Taylor et al. [19]** (TPAMI 2022) compare VAEs, GANs, normalizing flows, energy-based and autoregressive models side by side, including the trade-offs in run time, sample diversity and architectural constraints. It places AR models in the wider generative-model landscape: exact likelihood and stable training, paid for with slow sequential sampling.
- **Li et al. [20]** (TPAMI 2026) survey discrete tokenization for multimodal LLMs, organizing vector-quantization variants and their use in classical, single-modality LLM and multimodal LLM systems, and discussing codebook collapse and unstable gradient estimation.
- **Jia et al. [22]** survey discrete tokenizers across generation, comprehension, recommendation and retrieval, dissecting tokenizers into their sub-modules and mechanisms before organizing methods by application, and listing limitations and future directions.
- **Zhao et al. [21]** survey unified multimodal understanding-and-generation models, grouping them into diffusion-based, autoregressive-based and hybrid AR–diffusion designs, which is where most 2025–2026 activity sits.

**State of the art (2023–2026).** Three lines stand out in the verified literature. (i) *Scaling plain AR transformers over better tokenizers*: LlamaGen [14] shows that a language-model architecture with almost no image-specific design is competitive on class-conditional ImageNet when the tokenizer and training are good. (ii) *Changing the prediction unit*: VAR's next-scale prediction [6] and masked decoding in MaskGIT and Muse [8], [17] reduce the number of sequential steps; MAR [7] removes vector quantization by using a diffusion head per token. (iii) *Unified multimodal models*: the same next-token machinery is used to generate text and image tokens in one model, with tokenizers designed to serve both understanding and generation [20], [21]. Our interpretation is that choosing the **representation and prediction unit** now matters at least as much as choosing the backbone.

**Open problems.** The surveys repeatedly point to the following:

- *Tokenizer quality.* Reconstruction caps generation quality; small text, faces and fine geometry are the usual casualties. Better codebook usage and semantic structure without losing detail remain active topics [20], [22].
- *Discrete versus continuous tokens.* Discrete tokens fit LLM machinery; continuous tokens reconstruct better but need a continuous conditional model such as a diffusion head [7].
- *Speed.* Sequential decoding is latency-bound. Next-scale, masked and parallel decoding trade steps for independence assumptions.
- *Evaluation.* Token likelihood, FID-type metrics, prompt adherence and human preference measure different things; a lossy tokenizer's token likelihood is not a pixel likelihood.
- *Video and long contexts.* Token counts explode over time, and temporal consistency, object permanence and drift need dedicated evaluation [1].

## Key takeaways

- Autoregression is a factorization, not a synonym for "transformer": the chain rule is exact for any order, and the model's capacity and context decide how good each conditional is.
- Training is parallel under teacher forcing; sampling is sequential. Shifted inputs and causal masks (attention masks or PixelCNN mask A/B) must be designed together.
- Pixel models are exact but long; learned tokenizers (VQ-VAE, VQGAN, RQ-VAE, FSQ) shorten the sequence at the price of a reconstruction bottleneck that the generator cannot exceed.
- Token transformers (Image GPT, VQGAN transformer, Parti, LlamaGen) reuse language-model machinery, including classifier-free guidance on logits and KV caching.
- Next-scale (VAR), masked (MaskGIT, Muse) and continuous-token (MAR) models change the **unit** of prediction; fewer sequential steps usually mean more conditional-independence assumptions.
- Temperature, top-$k$ and top-$p$ reshape the sampling distribution; they trade diversity for fidelity but cannot add information lost by the tokenizer.
- AR and diffusion are complementary decompositions and are increasingly combined; compare them on matched data, tokenizers, guidance and compute.
