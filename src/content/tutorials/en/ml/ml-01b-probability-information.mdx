## The big picture

A neural network never sees the whole world. It sees a finite training set, measured by imperfect sensors, labelled by busy people, and then it is asked about images it has never seen. Every step of that story involves **uncertainty**. Probability theory is the mathematics of uncertainty: it tells us how to describe what we do not know, how to combine evidence, and how to make predictions that admit doubt. Information theory, its close relative, measures *how much* uncertainty there is and how costly it is to use the wrong distribution. Together they explain almost every loss function in this site's ML and DL tracks.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Imagine a bag of marbles you cannot see into. **Probability** is your best guess about what you will pull out next: "probably red, maybe blue, almost never green." Every time you pull a marble, you learn a little more about the bag and update your guess — that updating is **Bayes' rule**. **Entropy** is how surprised you expect to be: a bag of all-red marbles never surprises you; a bag with ten colours in equal amounts surprises you a lot. A machine learning model is a very fancy guesser, and training means making its guesses less surprised by the real marbles.
</Callout>

## Why probability?

**Plain version.** Computer science usually deals with certain facts: a bit is 0 or 1, a program either halts or does not. Machine learning cannot work that way because the data, the world and the model are all imperfect.

Goodfellow et al. [1] list three sources of uncertainty:

1. **Inherent stochasticity.** Some systems are genuinely random — photon arrival at a camera sensor (shot noise), the roll of a die, or the exact moment a pedestrian steps off a curb.
2. **Incomplete observability.** Even a deterministic system looks random if we cannot see all of its variables. A single photo of a car does not show whether its brake lights are about to turn on.
3. **Incomplete modelling.** A model must discard information to be useful. A classifier that sees $32\times32$ thumbnails cannot recover details that were lost when the image was downsampled, so its predictions about fine-grained classes must remain uncertain.

It is often more practical to use a simple rule with uncertainty ("most birds fly") than a complex rule that is always true ("birds fly, except penguins, ostriches, injured birds, …").

### Two readings of a probability

A probability can be read in two ways, and both obey the same mathematical rules.

- **Frequentist probability** describes long-run frequencies of repeatable events: "if we toss this coin many times, about 70% of tosses land heads".
- **Bayesian probability** describes a *degree of belief* about something that might not be repeatable: "given this X-ray, I am 40% confident the patient has the disease".

If degrees of belief are required to satisfy a few common-sense consistency properties, they turn out to obey exactly the same rules as frequencies, which is why one calculus serves both readings [1], [3]. In practice, deep learning mixes the two: the data are treated as samples from a distribution (frequentist), while the model's output is often read as a belief about the label (Bayesian).

## Random variables and probability distributions

**Plain version.** A random variable is a quantity whose value is not known in advance. A probability distribution says how likely each value is.

We write random variables in roman letters (for example $\mathrm x$ in [1]); in this tutorial we use $X$ for the variable and $x$ for one of its values. A random variable can be **discrete** (a finite or countable set of values, such as a class label in $\{1,\dots,K\}$) or **continuous** (real values, such as a pixel intensity treated as a real number).

### Probability mass functions

A discrete variable has a **probability mass function** (PMF) $P(X=x)$, often shortened to $P(x)$. It must satisfy

$$
0\le P(x)\le 1\ \text{for every }x,\qquad \sum_{x}P(x)=1.
$$

Here the sum runs over every possible value $x$ of $X$. The first condition says no event is more than certain; the second says that *something* always happens. A joint PMF $P(X=x,Y=y)$ gives the probability that both events happen together.

### Probability density functions

A continuous variable has a **probability density function** (PDF) $p(x)$, which must satisfy

$$
p(x)\ge 0,\qquad \int p(x)\,dx = 1 .
$$

A density is *not* a probability. The probability that $X$ falls in an interval $[a,b]$ is the area under the density:

$$
P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b p(x)\,dx .
$$

The probability of any single exact value is zero. Because only areas must be at most one, a density can exceed one: a narrow Gaussian with standard deviation $0.25$ peaks at about $1.6$ (Figure 1, top right). A common mistake is to report "probability 1.6" for a density value.

### Marginal probability

**Plain version.** If you know how two things vary together, you can recover how one of them varies on its own by "summing out" the other.

Given a joint distribution over $X$ and $Y$, the **marginal** distribution of $X$ is

$$
P(x)=\sum_y P(x,y)\quad\text{(discrete)},\qquad p(x)=\int p(x,y)\,dy\quad\text{(continuous)}.
$$

This is the **sum rule**. If the joint is written as a table with rows for $x$ and columns for $y$, the marginal of $x$ is the row sums — historically written in the margin of the page, hence the name.

### Conditional probability

**Plain version.** A conditional probability answers "how likely is this, *given that* I already know that?"

$$
P(y\mid x)=\frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)},\qquad P(x)>0 .
$$

$P(y\mid x)$ is the probability of $Y=y$ after we have observed $X=x$. It is only defined when the condition can actually happen. Rearranging gives the **product rule** $P(x,y)=P(y\mid x)P(x)$.

Do not confuse conditioning with intervention. "The probability that the street is wet given that people carry umbrellas" is high, but *forcing* people to carry umbrellas will not wet the street. Questions about interventions belong to causal inference, which needs more than a joint distribution.

### The chain rule of probability

Applying the product rule repeatedly factorizes any joint distribution over $n$ variables:

$$
P\big(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(n)}\big)=P\big(x^{(1)}\big)\prod_{i=2}^{n}P\big(x^{(i)}\mid x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(i-1)}\big).
$$

Here $x^{(i)}$ is the value of the $i$-th variable. This identity is *exact* — no assumption is involved. It is the backbone of autoregressive models: a language model or an autoregressive image model predicts one token or pixel at a time given all previous ones ([ML 09](/en/learn/ml/autoregressive-vision/), [NLP 01](/en/learn/nlp/introduction/)). The modelling choice lies in *how* each conditional is parameterized, not in the factorization itself.

### Independence and conditional independence

Two variables are **independent**, written $X\perp Y$, if

$$
p(x,y)=p(x)\,p(y)\quad\text{for all }x,y .
$$

They are **conditionally independent** given $Z$, written $X\perp Y\mid Z$, if

$$
p(x,y\mid z)=p(x\mid z)\,p(y\mid z)\quad\text{for all }x,y,z .
$$

Ice-cream sales and sunburn are dependent, but roughly independent once you condition on the weather. Conditional independence is the main tool for making high-dimensional models tractable — naive Bayes assumes the features are independent given the class; a hidden Markov model assumes the future is independent of the past given the present state. These are *assumptions* [3]: they should be justified by the problem, not adopted only because they make the algebra easy.

### Code: a joint table, marginals and conditionals

The following example builds a small joint table for weather and umbrella use, computes marginals and conditionals, and tests independence. It also shows a case where two variables are clearly dependent yet have (almost) zero covariance.

```python
import numpy as np

# Joint table P(weather, umbrella): rows = sunny/rainy, columns = no/yes
P = np.array([[0.56, 0.14],
              [0.06, 0.24]])
assert np.isclose(P.sum(), 1)

p_weather = P.sum(axis=1)            # marginal over umbrella
p_umbrella = P.sum(axis=0)           # marginal over weather
p_umb_given_w = P / p_weather[:, None]   # each row sums to 1
print("P(weather):", p_weather)
print("P(umbrella):", p_umbrella)
print("P(umbrella | weather):\n", p_umb_given_w)
print("independent?", np.allclose(P, np.outer(p_weather, p_umbrella)))

# Zero covariance does not imply independence
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, 200_000)
y = x**2                              # y is a deterministic function of x
print("cov(x, y) ~", np.cov(x, y)[0, 1].round(4))
```

Output: the marginals are $[0.7, 0.3]$ and $[0.62, 0.38]$; the conditional table is $[[0.8, 0.2],[0.2, 0.8]]$; the variables are not independent; and the sample covariance of $x$ and $x^2$ is about $-0.0007$, essentially zero, even though $y$ is completely determined by $x$.

## Expectation, variance and covariance

**Plain version.** The expectation is the long-run average; the variance is how far values typically spread from that average; the covariance says whether two quantities tend to go up and down together.

The **expectation** of a function $f$ under a distribution is

$$
\mathbb E_{x\sim P}[f(x)]=\sum_x P(x)\,f(x),\qquad
\mathbb E_{x\sim p}[f(x)]=\int p(x)\,f(x)\,dx .
$$

The subscript $x\sim P$ names the distribution we average over; we drop it when it is obvious. Expectation is **linear**: $\mathbb E[\alpha f(x)+\beta g(x)]=\alpha\mathbb E[f(x)]+\beta\mathbb E[g(x)]$ for constants $\alpha,\beta$. Almost every training loss is an expectation over the data distribution, estimated by an average over a mini-batch.

The **variance** measures spread around the mean $\mu=\mathbb E[f(x)]$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(f(x))=\mathbb E\big[(f(x)-\mu)^2\big]=\mathbb E[f(x)^2]-\mu^2 .
$$

Its square root is the **standard deviation**, which has the same units as $f(x)$.

The **covariance** of two quantities measures how they vary together:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(f(x),g(y))=\mathbb E\big[(f(x)-\mathbb E[f(x)])\,(g(y)-\mathbb E[g(y)])\big].
$$

A positive covariance means both tend to be above (or below) their means at the same time; a negative covariance means one tends to be high when the other is low. Its magnitude depends on the units, so we often normalize it into the **correlation**

$$
\rho=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\operatorname{Var}(Y)}}\in[-1,1].
$$

Independence implies zero covariance, but **zero covariance does not imply independence**. Covariance measures only *linear* dependence. In the code above, $y=x^2$ with $x$ uniform on $[-1,1]$: knowing $x$ tells you $y$ exactly, but the covariance is zero because the relationship is symmetric and nonlinear. Mutual information, introduced later, captures every kind of dependence.

### The covariance matrix

For a random vector $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^n$ with mean $\boldsymbol\mu=\mathbb E[\mathbf x]$, the **covariance matrix** is

$$
\Sigma=\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf x)=\mathbb E\big[(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\big],\qquad \Sigma_{ij}=\operatorname{Cov}(x_i,x_j).
$$

Its diagonal holds the variances $\Sigma_{ii}=\operatorname{Var}(x_i)$. $\Sigma$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite, so (by [ML 01A](/en/learn/ml/linear-algebra/)) it has real nonnegative eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. The eigenvectors are the principal axes of the data cloud and the eigenvalues are the variances along them — the red arrows in Figure 1 — which is exactly what PCA computes. For an affine map $\mathbf y=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b$, $\mathbb E[\mathbf y]=A\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf y)=A\Sigma A^\top$.

## Common probability distributions

**Plain version.** A handful of distributions appear over and over. Choosing one is choosing an assumption about your data or your target.

### Bernoulli

A single binary variable $X\in\{0,1\}$ with parameter $\phi\in[0,1]$:

$$
P(X=1)=\phi,\quad P(X=0)=1-\phi,\quad P(X=x)=\phi^{x}(1-\phi)^{1-x}.
$$

Its mean is $\phi$ and its variance $\phi(1-\phi)$. A binary classifier with a sigmoid output predicts the parameter $\phi$ of a Bernoulli distribution over the label.

### Categorical (multinoulli)

A single discrete variable with $K$ states, parameterized by a probability vector $\mathbf p\in[0,1]^K$ with $\sum_k p_k=1$:

$$
P(X=k)=p_k .
$$

If the label is encoded one-hot as $\mathbf y\in\{0,1\}^K$, then $P(\mathbf y)=\prod_k p_k^{y_k}$. A softmax classifier outputs exactly such a vector. (The *multinomial* distribution counts outcomes over several draws; for one draw it reduces to the categorical, which Goodfellow et al. call "multinoulli" [1].)

### Gaussian (normal)

The most widely used continuous distribution:

$$
\mathcal N(x;\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big).
$$

$\mu$ is the mean (and the peak), $\sigma^2$ the variance. Sometimes we use the **precision** $\beta=1/\sigma^2$ instead, which is cheaper to evaluate when the variance changes often.

Two reasons make the Gaussian the default. The **central limit theorem** says that the sum of many independent variables with finite variance tends towards a Gaussian, so measurement noise built from many small effects is often near-Gaussian. And among all distributions with a given mean and variance on the real line, the Gaussian has the **maximum entropy**: it builds in the least extra structure.

In $n$ dimensions,

$$
\mathcal N(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^n\det\Sigma}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\Big),
$$

where $\boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb R^n$ is the mean vector and $\Sigma\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ is a symmetric positive-definite covariance matrix. The **precision matrix** $\Lambda=\Sigma^{-1}$ has a neat meaning: $\Lambda_{ij}=0$ exactly when $x_i$ and $x_j$ are conditionally independent given all other coordinates. An **isotropic** Gaussian has $\Sigma=\sigma^2 I$ — the noise distribution used by diffusion models ([ML 08](/en/learn/ml/diffusion-flow-matching/)).

A fundamental link to the losses you will use: if a regression network predicts the mean of a Gaussian with fixed variance, maximizing the likelihood is the same as minimizing the **mean squared error**.

### Exponential and Laplace

Sometimes we want a sharp point at a specific location.

$$
p(x;\lambda)=\lambda\,\mathbf 1_{x\ge0}\,e^{-\lambda x},\qquad
\operatorname{Laplace}(x;\mu,\gamma)=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{|x-\mu|}{\gamma}\Big).
$$

$\lambda>0$ is the rate, $\mathbf 1_{x\ge0}$ is the indicator that is 1 when $x\ge0$ and 0 otherwise, $\mu$ is the location and $\gamma>0$ the scale. Compared with the Gaussian, the Laplace density has a sharper peak and heavier tails (Figure 1, bottom left). Predicting the location of a Laplace distribution by maximum likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the **absolute error** ($L_1$ loss), which is why $L_1$ is more tolerant of outliers than $L_2$; a Laplace *prior* on weights gives $L_1$ regularization (sparsity).

### Dirac and empirical distributions

The **Dirac delta** $\delta(x-\mu)$ puts all of its mass at the point $\mu$: it is zero everywhere else yet integrates to one. It is a generalized function, defined by what it does inside integrals: $\int \delta(x-\mu)f(x)\,dx=f(\mu)$.

A training set $\{\mathbf x^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf x^{(m)}\}$ defines the **empirical distribution**

$$
\hat p(\mathbf x)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta\big(\mathbf x-\mathbf x^{(i)}\big),
$$

which puts mass $1/m$ on each of the $m$ examples. Training a model by averaging a loss over the dataset is the same as taking an expectation under $\hat p$. It is also the distribution that maximizes the likelihood of the training data — which is precisely why minimizing training loss alone overfits.

### Mixtures and latent variables

**Plain version.** A mixture first picks a "type" at random, then draws a sample from the distribution belonging to that type.

$$
P(\mathbf x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(z=k)\,P(\mathbf x\mid z=k).
$$

$z$ is a categorical **latent variable** — a variable we never observe directly — chosen with probabilities $\pi_k=P(z=k)$, and $P(\mathbf x\mid z=k)$ is the $k$-th component. A **Gaussian mixture model** (GMM) uses Gaussian components $\mathcal N(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$; with enough components it can approximate any smooth density to any desired nonzero error [1]. Given an observation $\mathbf x$, Bayes' rule gives the **posterior responsibility** $P(z=k\mid\mathbf x)$: how likely it is that component $k$ generated that point. Fitting a GMM with expectation–maximization is the subject of [PR 07](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/clustering-mixtures/).

<Figure src={mixtureFig} alt="Left: a histogram of 2,000 samples with three dashed weighted Gaussian components and their solid sum. Right: points from a two-dimensional mixture coloured by which hidden component produced them." caption="Figure 2 — A Gaussian mixture. Left: the black density is the weighted sum of three components; the grey histogram is 2,000 samples drawn by first picking a component and then sampling from it. Right: in two dimensions, colour shows the hidden component z, which a real dataset would not reveal." />

Latent variables are central to modern generative models. A VAE ([ML 07](/en/learn/ml/generative-models/)) is, informally, a mixture with a *continuous* latent $\mathbf z$ and a neural network for $p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)$ [8]; the marginal $p(\mathbf x)=\int p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)p(\mathbf z)\,d\mathbf z$ is then intractable and must be approximated.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
pi = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])      # mixing weights P(z = k)
mu = np.array([-2.0, 0.5, 3.0])     # component means
sd = np.array([0.6, 0.8, 0.5])      # component standard deviations

# Ancestral sampling: first the hidden component z, then x given z
z = rng.choice(3, size=100_000, p=pi)
x = rng.normal(mu[z], sd[z])

def log_normal(x, m, s):
    return -0.5*((x - m)/s)**2 - np.log(s) - 0.5*np.log(2*np.pi)

def mixture_logpdf(x):
    # log sum_k pi_k N(x; mu_k, sd_k^2), computed with log-sum-exp
    a = np.log(pi) + log_normal(x[:, None], mu, sd)
    m = a.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(a - m).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))).ravel()

grid = np.linspace(-6, 6, 4001)
print("density integrates to", (np.exp(mixture_logpdf(grid)).sum()*(grid[1]-grid[0])).round(4))
print("sample mean", x.mean().round(3), "vs exact", (pi*mu).sum().round(3))
# Posterior responsibility P(z | x) for one observation
a = np.log(pi) + log_normal(np.array([0.0])[:, None], mu, sd)
resp = np.exp(a - a.max()); resp /= resp.sum()
print("P(z | x=0):", resp.round(3))
```

The density integrates to 1.0, the sample mean (−0.255) matches the exact mean $\sum_k\pi_k\mu_k=-0.25$, and the point $x=0$ is assigned to the middle component with probability 0.99. The log-sum-exp trick used here is explained in [ML 01C](/en/learn/ml/numerical-computation/).

## Useful functions: sigmoid and softplus

**Plain version.** The sigmoid squashes any real number into a probability between 0 and 1; the softplus is a smooth version of $\max(0,x)$ that is always positive.

$$
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}},\qquad \zeta(x)=\log\big(1+e^{x}\big).
$$

The **logistic sigmoid** $\sigma$ produces the parameter $\phi$ of a Bernoulli distribution; it **saturates** (becomes flat) for large $|x|$, which makes gradients small there. The **softplus** $\zeta$ produces a positive number, such as a standard deviation $\sigma$ or a precision $\beta$; its name comes from being a softened "positive part" $x^+=\max(0,x)$.

<Figure src={entropySigmoidFig} alt="Left: the binary entropy curve, zero at probability 0 and 1 and peaking at one bit at probability 0.5. Right: the sigmoid curve rising from 0 to 1, and the softplus curve that follows max(0, x) smoothly." caption="Figure 3 — Left: entropy of a Bernoulli variable, maximal (1 bit) at φ = 0.5 and zero when the outcome is certain. Right: the logistic sigmoid σ(x) and the softplus ζ(x), a smooth version of max(0, x)." />

These identities are worth memorizing, because they appear every time a loss is simplified [1]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1-\sigma(x)&=\sigma(-x), &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sigma(x)&=\sigma(x)\big(1-\sigma(x)\big),\\
\log\sigma(x)&=-\zeta(-x), &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\zeta(x)&=\sigma(x),\\
\sigma^{-1}(p)&=\log\frac{p}{1-p}, &\qquad \zeta(x)-\zeta(-x)&=x .
\end{aligned}
$$

$\sigma^{-1}$ is the **logit** (log-odds) function. The identity $\log\sigma(x)=-\zeta(-x)$ shows why binary cross-entropy should be computed from logits: $-\log\sigma(x)=\zeta(-x)$, and softplus can be evaluated without overflow.

```python
import numpy as np

def sigmoid(x):
    return 1/(1 + np.exp(-x))

def softplus(x):
    return np.logaddexp(0, x)          # log(1 + exp(x)) without overflow

x = np.linspace(-30, 30, 13)
assert np.allclose(1 - sigmoid(x), sigmoid(-x))
assert np.allclose(np.log(sigmoid(x)), -softplus(-x))
assert np.allclose(softplus(x) - softplus(-x), x)
h = 1e-6
assert np.allclose((softplus(x+h) - softplus(x-h))/(2*h), sigmoid(x), atol=1e-6)
p = sigmoid(x[4:9])
assert np.allclose(np.log(p/(1-p)), x[4:9])   # logit inverts sigmoid
print("naive log(1+exp(1000)):", np.log1p(np.exp(np.float64(1000))))
print("stable softplus(1000):", softplus(1000.0))
```

All identities pass. The naive expression returns `inf` (with an overflow warning) while `np.logaddexp` returns 1000.0.

## Bayes' rule

**Plain version.** Bayes' rule turns "how likely is the evidence if the hypothesis is true?" into "how likely is the hypothesis now that I have seen the evidence?" — and it insists that you account for how common the hypothesis was in the first place.

From the product rule written both ways, $P(a,b)=P(a\mid b)P(b)=P(b\mid a)P(a)$, so

$$
P(a\mid b)=\frac{P(b\mid a)\,P(a)}{P(b)},\qquad P(b)=\sum_{a'}P(b\mid a')\,P(a').
$$

- $P(a)$ is the **prior**: how plausible $a$ is before seeing the evidence.
- $P(b\mid a)$ is the **likelihood** of the evidence $b$ under hypothesis $a$.
- $P(a\mid b)$ is the **posterior**: the updated plausibility.
- $P(b)$ is the **evidence** (or marginal likelihood), the normalizer obtained by summing over all hypotheses $a'$.

### A worked example: the base-rate effect

Consider a hypothetical defect detector with **sensitivity** $P(+\mid\text{defect})=0.9$, **false-positive rate** $P(+\mid\text{no defect})=0.05$, and a **prevalence** (base rate) $P(\text{defect})=0.01$. The probability that an item flagged as positive really is defective is

$$
P(\mathrm{defect}\mid +)
=\frac{0.9\cdot0.01}{0.9\cdot0.01+0.05\cdot0.99}
\approx 0.154 .
$$

Only about 15% of alarms are real, even though the detector catches 90% of defects. Counting makes this obvious: among 10,000 items, 100 are defective and 90 of them raise an alarm, but 9,900 are fine and 495 of them also raise an alarm (Figure 4, right). The same detector at 10% prevalence gives $0.09/(0.09+0.045)=2/3$. This is a mathematical illustration, not a performance claim about any real system. Its lesson carries over to every deployed classifier: **sensitivity and false-positive rate alone do not determine how trustworthy a positive prediction is; the deployment prevalence matters.** [ML 11](/en/learn/ml/evaluation-deployment/) returns to this when discussing precision under distribution shift.

<Figure src={baseRateFig} alt="Left: a curve of the probability of a defect given a positive result versus prevalence on a log scale, rising from near 0 to 1, with points marked at 1% (0.154) and 10% (0.667). Right: a bar chart showing 90 true positives and 495 false positives among 10,000 items." caption="Figure 4 — The base-rate effect for a hypothetical detector with sensitivity 0.9 and false-positive rate 0.05. Left: the posterior P(defect | +) depends strongly on prevalence. Right: at 1% prevalence, false alarms outnumber true alarms more than five to one." />

```python
import numpy as np

def posterior_positive(prevalence, sensitivity=0.9, false_pos=0.05):
    evidence = sensitivity*prevalence + false_pos*(1 - prevalence)   # P(+)
    return sensitivity*prevalence / evidence                          # P(defect | +)

for prev in [0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5]:
    print(f"prevalence {prev:>5}: P(defect | +) = {posterior_positive(prev):.3f}")

# Simulation check with 1,000,000 synthetic items
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
defect = rng.random(1_000_000) < 0.01
alarm = np.where(defect, rng.random(defect.size) < 0.9, rng.random(defect.size) < 0.05)
print("simulated:", defect[alarm].mean().round(3))
```

The formula gives 0.018, 0.154, 0.667 and 0.947 for the four prevalences, and a one-million-item simulation reproduces 0.154.

Bayes' rule also underlies the **Bayes classifier**: predicting the class $k$ with the largest posterior $P(y=k\mid\mathbf x)\propto p(\mathbf x\mid y=k)P(y=k)$ minimizes the expected error rate. [PR 01](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/bayes-decisions/) develops this decision theory in depth.

## Change of variables for densities

**Plain version.** If you stretch a rubber sheet, the ink on it gets thinner. When a random variable is transformed, its density must be rescaled by how much the transformation stretches space.

A tempting mistake: if $y=g(x)$ and $x$ has density $p_x$, then $p_y(y)=p_x(g^{-1}(y))$. This is wrong, because it does not integrate to one. The correct rule for an invertible, differentiable $g$ in one dimension is

$$
p_y(y)=p_x\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\,\left|\frac{d\,g^{-1}(y)}{dy}\right| .
$$

The probability in a small interval must be preserved: $|p_y(y)\,dy|=|p_x(x)\,dx|$. In $n$ dimensions, with $\mathbf y=g(\mathbf x)$,

$$
p_x(\mathbf x)=p_y\big(g(\mathbf x)\big)\,\left|\det\frac{\partial g(\mathbf x)}{\partial\mathbf x}\right| ,
$$

where $\partial g/\partial\mathbf x$ is the $n\times n$ **Jacobian matrix** of $g$ (entry $(i,j)$ is $\partial y_i/\partial x_j$) and $|\det(\cdot)|$ is the absolute value of its determinant — the factor by which $g$ scales a small volume (see [ML 01A](/en/learn/ml/linear-algebra/) for determinants). This formula is the entire foundation of **normalizing flows**, which stack invertible networks with cheap Jacobian determinants to obtain an exact likelihood [9].

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(0, 1, 500_000)        # x ~ N(0, 1)
y = np.exp(x)                        # y = g(x), invertible on (0, inf)

def p_x(x):
    return np.exp(-0.5*x**2)/np.sqrt(2*np.pi)

def p_y(y):
    # p_y(y) = p_x(g^{-1}(y)) * |d g^{-1}(y) / dy| = p_x(log y) / y
    return p_x(np.log(y))/y

counts, edges = np.histogram(y, bins=np.linspace(0.05, 4, 80), density=False)
centers = 0.5*(edges[:-1] + edges[1:])
empirical = counts/(y.size*np.diff(edges))
print("max |histogram - formula|:", np.abs(empirical - p_y(centers)).max().round(3))
print("wrong formula (no Jacobian) error:", np.abs(empirical - p_x(np.log(centers))).max().round(3))
```

With the Jacobian factor $1/y$ the histogram matches the formula to within 0.011 (sampling noise); without it the error is 0.46.

## Likelihood, maximum likelihood and MAP

**Plain version.** Hold the observed data fixed and ask which parameter values make that data most plausible. If you also have prior knowledge about the parameters, weigh that in too.

Suppose a model $p_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf x)$ has parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$, and we observe a dataset $\mathcal D=\{\mathbf x^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf x^{(m)}\}$ of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) examples. The **likelihood** is

$$
L(\boldsymbol\theta)=p_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathcal D)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{\boldsymbol\theta}\big(\mathbf x^{(i)}\big).
$$

As a function of $\boldsymbol\theta$, this is *not* a probability distribution over parameters: it need not integrate to one over $\boldsymbol\theta$. The **maximum likelihood estimate** (MLE) is

$$
\boldsymbol\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p_{\boldsymbol\theta}\big(\mathbf x^{(i)}\big)
=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ \mathbb E_{\mathbf x\sim\hat p}\big[-\log p_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf x)\big].
$$

We take logarithms because a product of many numbers below one underflows, while a sum of logs is numerically safe and has the same maximizer. The right-hand form says MLE **minimizes the average negative log-likelihood** (NLL) under the empirical distribution $\hat p$. For a classifier $p_{\boldsymbol\theta}(y\mid\mathbf x)$ the same idea gives the conditional NLL $-\log p_{\boldsymbol\theta}(y\mid\mathbf x)$, which is the cross-entropy loss we meet below.

Two classic results: for a Bernoulli coin with $k$ heads in $n$ flips, $\phi_{\mathrm{ML}}=k/n$; for a Gaussian, $\mu_{\mathrm{ML}}$ is the sample mean and $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}=\frac1m\sum_i(x^{(i)}-\mu_{\mathrm{ML}})^2$ — a slightly *biased* estimate (dividing by $m-1$ removes the bias).

### MAP estimation

**Bayesian** inference treats $\boldsymbol\theta$ itself as a random variable with a **prior** $p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ and computes the **posterior**

$$
p(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathcal D)=\frac{p(\mathcal D\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\,p(\boldsymbol\theta)}{p(\mathcal D)}\ \propto\ p(\mathcal D\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\,p(\boldsymbol\theta).
$$

The full posterior is often intractable, so a common shortcut is its mode, the **maximum a posteriori** (MAP) estimate:

$$
\boldsymbol\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\big[\log p(\mathcal D\mid\boldsymbol\theta)+\log p(\boldsymbol\theta)\big].
$$

With a Gaussian prior $p(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\tfrac1\lambda I)$, the term $-\log p(\boldsymbol\theta)$ equals $\tfrac\lambda2\|\boldsymbol\theta\|_2^2$ plus a constant: **weight decay is MAP with a Gaussian prior**. A Laplace prior gives $L_1$ regularization. This links probabilistic assumptions to the regularizers in [ML 02](/en/learn/ml/neural-networks/). But a MAP point estimate is still a single parameter vector; it does not tell you how uncertain the parameters are. Approximating the whole posterior is the topic of [PR 08](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/approximate-inference/) and of variational inference [10].

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
flips = rng.random(10) < 0.7          # 10 coin flips, true P(heads) = 0.7
k, n = flips.sum(), flips.size

phi = np.linspace(1e-3, 1 - 1e-3, 999)
log_lik = k*np.log(phi) + (n - k)*np.log(1 - phi)
a, b = 5.0, 5.0                        # Beta(5, 5) prior, centered on 0.5
log_prior = (a - 1)*np.log(phi) + (b - 1)*np.log(1 - phi)

print(f"{k} heads in {n} flips")
print("MLE  grid:", phi[log_lik.argmax()].round(3), " closed form:", k/n)
print("MAP  grid:", phi[(log_lik + log_prior).argmax()].round(3),
      " closed form:", round((k + a - 1)/(n + a + b - 2), 3))

# Gaussian MLE: sample mean and the 1/m (biased) variance
x = rng.normal(2.0, 3.0, 50)
print("Gaussian MLE mean, var:", x.mean().round(3), x.var().round(3),
      " unbiased var:", x.var(ddof=1).round(3))
```

With this seed, 6 heads appear in 10 flips. The MLE is 0.6; the Beta(5, 5) prior pulls the MAP estimate towards 0.5, giving 0.556, matching the closed form $(k+a-1)/(n+a+b-2)$. With more data the likelihood dominates and the two estimates converge. The Gaussian MLE variance (7.71) is smaller than the unbiased estimate (7.87), as expected.

## Information theory

**Plain version.** Information theory measures surprise. A rare event carries a lot of information when it happens; a certain event carries none. Averaging surprise gives entropy, and the extra surprise from using the wrong distribution gives KL divergence.

Claude Shannon founded the field to study how many bits are needed to send messages over a noisy channel [5]; the textbook treatment is Cover and Thomas [7]. For machine learning, the useful idea is to quantify how different two distributions are.

### Self-information and entropy

The **self-information** of an outcome $x$ is

$$
I(x)=-\log P(x).
$$

With the natural logarithm the unit is the **nat**; with $\log_2$ it is the **bit** (one nat $=1/\ln2\approx1.443$ bits). Certain events ($P=1$) carry zero information; unlikely events carry a lot; and information from independent events adds, because logs turn products into sums.

The **Shannon entropy** is the expected self-information:

$$
H(P)=\mathbb E_{x\sim P}[I(x)]=-\sum_x P(x)\log P(x).
$$

We use the convention $0\log0=0$, justified by the limit $p\log p\to0$ as $p\to0$. Entropy is the minimum average number of bits (or nats) needed to encode outcomes drawn from $P$. It is zero for a deterministic outcome and maximal, $\log K$, for a uniform distribution over $K$ outcomes. For a Bernoulli variable with parameter $\phi$, $H=-\phi\log\phi-(1-\phi)\log(1-\phi)$, which peaks at one bit when $\phi=0.5$ (Figure 3, left).

For continuous variables, $h(p)=-\int p(x)\log p(x)\,dx$ is the **differential entropy**. Treat it carefully: it can be negative (a narrow uniform distribution on $[0,\tfrac12]$ has $h=-\log2$) and it changes when you rescale the coordinates. It is not a limiting version of the discrete count of bits.

### KL divergence

The **Kullback–Leibler divergence** [6] measures how different a distribution $Q$ is from a reference $P$:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)=\mathbb E_{x\sim P}\Big[\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\Big]=\sum_x P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} .
$$

In coding terms, it is the *extra* number of nats needed to encode samples from $P$ when the code was designed for $Q$. Its key properties:

- **Nonnegative**: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\ge0$, with equality if and only if $P=Q$ (for discrete variables; "almost everywhere" for continuous ones). This follows from Jensen's inequality, because $-\log$ is convex.
- **Infinite under unsupported mass**: if $Q(x)=0$ for some $x$ with $P(x)>0$, the divergence is infinite.
- **Asymmetric**: in general $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|P)$, and it does not satisfy the triangle inequality. KL is therefore *not* a distance metric, even though it is often described as "the distance between distributions".

### Forward versus reverse KL

The asymmetry has visible consequences when the approximating family is too simple. Figure 5 fits a single Gaussian $q$ to a target $p$ with two well-separated modes.

- Minimizing **forward KL** $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ heavily penalizes $q$ for being small where $p$ has mass. The best Gaussian therefore **covers both modes**, placing its mean between them ($\mu=0$, $\sigma\approx2.62$) and putting a lot of mass in the empty valley. For Gaussian $q$, the forward-KL optimum simply matches the mean and variance of $p$.
- Minimizing **reverse KL** $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ heavily penalizes $q$ for being large where $p$ is small. The best Gaussian therefore **locks onto one mode** ($\mu\approx2.49$, $\sigma\approx0.81$) and ignores the other.

Right: a narrow orange dashed Gaussian sitting on the right-hand peak only." caption="Figure 5 — KL is asymmetric. Fitting one Gaussian q to a two-mode target p: forward KL(p‖q) gives a mass-covering fit centred between the modes; reverse KL(q‖p) gives a mode-seeking fit on one peak (the other peak gives an equally good mirror solution)." />

Which direction you minimize is a design choice. **Maximum likelihood minimizes forward KL** from the data distribution to the model, so likelihood-trained models (autoregressive models, flows, VAEs) tend to spread probability over all the data, sometimes producing blurry samples. **Variational inference usually minimizes reverse KL** from the approximation to the true posterior [10], and so tends to underestimate posterior uncertainty.

### Cross-entropy

The **cross-entropy** of $Q$ relative to $P$ is

$$
H(P,Q)=-\mathbb E_{x\sim P}\big[\log Q(x)\big]=H(P)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q).
$$

Because $H(P)$ does not depend on $Q$, minimizing cross-entropy with respect to $Q$ is the same as minimizing forward KL. In classification, $P$ is the empirical label distribution — a one-hot vector for each example — and $Q$ is the model's softmax output $\hat{\mathbf y}$. For one example with true class $y$,

$$
H(\mathbf e_y,\hat{\mathbf y})=-\sum_k [\mathbf e_y]_k\log\hat y_k=-\log\hat y_{y},
$$

where $\mathbf e_y$ is the one-hot vector for class $y$. Averaged over a dataset, this is exactly the conditional negative log-likelihood. So "cross-entropy loss", "log loss" and "negative log-likelihood of a categorical model" are three names for the same objective. Any loss consisting of a negative log-likelihood is a cross-entropy between the empirical distribution and the model — which is why the mean squared error is also "the cross-entropy" for a Gaussian model [1].

### Mutual information

**Mutual information** measures how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another:

$$
I(X;Y)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\big)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y).
$$

Here $H(X\mid Y)=-\mathbb E_{x,y}[\log p(x\mid y)]$ is the conditional entropy and $H(X,Y)$ the joint entropy. $I(X;Y)\ge0$, with equality if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent — unlike covariance, it detects any form of dependence. It is symmetric in $X$ and $Y$.

### Code: information quantities

```python
import numpy as np

p = np.array([.7, .2, .1])
q = np.array([.5, .3, .2])
entropy = -(p*np.log(p)).sum()
cross_entropy = -(p*np.log(q)).sum()
kl = (p*np.log(p/q)).sum()
reverse_kl = (q*np.log(q/p)).sum()
assert np.allclose(cross_entropy - entropy, kl) and kl >= 0
print("H(p), H(p,q), KL(p||q), KL(q||p) in nats:",
      np.round([entropy, cross_entropy, kl, reverse_kl], 4))
print("H(p) in bits:", (entropy/np.log(2)).round(4))

# Mutual information from a joint table (same weather/umbrella table)
P = np.array([[0.56, 0.14],
              [0.06, 0.24]])
px, py = P.sum(1), P.sum(0)
mi = (P*np.log(P/np.outer(px, py))).sum()
H = lambda t: -(t[t > 0]*np.log(t[t > 0])).sum()
assert np.isclose(mi, H(px) + H(py) - H(P.ravel()))
print("I(weather; umbrella) =", mi.round(4), "nats")

# Cross-entropy loss on one-hot labels equals mean -log q(y)
probs = np.array([[0.7, 0.2, 0.1], [0.1, 0.8, 0.1], [0.3, 0.3, 0.4]])
labels = np.array([0, 1, 2])
print("mean cross-entropy:", (-np.log(probs[np.arange(3), labels])).mean().round(4))
```

Output: $H(p)=0.8018$, $H(p,q)=0.8869$, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=0.0851$ and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)=0.0920$ nats — the two directions differ. $H(p)$ is 1.1568 bits. The weather–umbrella mutual information is 0.1637 nats, and both formulas for it agree. The mean cross-entropy of the three predictions is 0.4987 nats. In real training code, compute the cross-entropy from logits with a fused function rather than from probabilities (see [ML 01C](/en/learn/ml/numerical-computation/)).

## Structured probabilistic models

**Plain version.** A joint distribution over many variables is far too big to write down as a table. Structured models draw a graph saying which variables directly influence which, and then the joint splits into small pieces.

A joint table over 100 binary variables would need $2^{100}-1$ numbers. **Structured probabilistic models**, also called **graphical models**, exploit conditional independence to factorize the joint into small factors [1], [3]. There are two main kinds.

**Directed models** (Bayesian networks) use a directed acyclic graph $\mathcal G$. Each variable depends only on its parents $Pa_{\mathcal G}(x_i)$:

$$
p(\mathbf x)=\prod_i p\big(x_i\mid Pa_{\mathcal G}(x_i)\big).
$$

For example, with $a\to b\to c$ and $a\to c$ we have $p(a,b,c)=p(a)\,p(b\mid a)\,p(c\mid a,b)$; deleting the edge $a\to c$ asserts $c\perp a\mid b$ and shortens the last factor to $p(c\mid b)$. Directed graphs are natural for generative stories ("first choose the class, then the pixels"), and a mixture model is the simplest example: $z\to\mathbf x$.

**Undirected models** (Markov random fields) use an undirected graph. Each **clique** $\mathcal C^{(i)}$ (a set of mutually connected nodes) has a nonnegative **factor** $\phi^{(i)}$, and

$$
p(\mathbf x)=\frac1Z\prod_i\phi^{(i)}\big(\mathcal C^{(i)}\big),\qquad Z=\sum_{\mathbf x}\prod_i\phi^{(i)}\big(\mathcal C^{(i)}\big).
$$

The **partition function** $Z$ normalizes the product; computing it is often the hard part. Undirected models suit symmetric interactions, like neighbouring pixels in an image prior or a conditional random field for segmentation.

A graph is a *description* of a distribution, not a different kind of distribution: any distribution can be written either way, although one may be much more compact. Deep learning often uses these ideas implicitly — an autoregressive model is a fully connected directed model, and a VAE is a two-node directed model $\mathbf z\to\mathbf x$ whose conditional is a neural network [8].

## Modern view

The probability and information theory in this chapter is classical. What has changed in the deep-learning era is *how* it is used and what goes wrong in practice. This section surveys the review literature and recent directions; it summarizes their framing rather than reproducing benchmark numbers.

### Calibration: are predicted probabilities honest?

A classifier is **calibrated** if, among all inputs for which it predicts confidence 0.8, about 80% are correct. Guo et al. [11] (ICML 2017) found that modern deep networks are often **overconfident**, and identified depth, width, weight decay and batch normalization as factors that affect calibration. Their main recommendation, **temperature scaling** — dividing all logits by a single scalar $T$ fitted on a validation set — is a one-parameter variant of Platt scaling and remains a strong baseline because it changes confidence without changing the predicted class. Wang's survey [12] organizes calibration methods into four families: post-hoc calibration, regularization during training, uncertainty estimation, and compositions of these; its revised version also covers calibration of large language models. The probabilistic lesson: a softmax output is a model of $P(y\mid\mathbf x)$, and like any model it can be wrong in its uncertainty, not only in its argmax.

### Uncertainty quantification

Two major reviews map the field of uncertainty in deep learning. Abdar et al. [13] (*Information Fusion*, 2021) review Bayesian approximations, ensembles and their applications across many domains. Gawlikowski et al. [14] (*Artificial Intelligence Review*, 2023) distinguish **reducible model (epistemic) uncertainty** from **irreducible data (aleatoric) uncertainty** — roughly the third and first sources of uncertainty from the start of this chapter — and compare deterministic single networks, Bayesian methods, ensembles and test-time augmentation, together with calibration measures.

Two primary studies are especially influential. **Deep ensembles** [15] train several networks from different random initializations and average their predictive distributions; this simple, parallel method was competitive with approximate Bayesian neural networks and expressed higher uncertainty on out-of-distribution inputs. Ovadia et al. [16] (NeurIPS 2019) benchmarked uncertainty methods under **dataset shift** and found that post-hoc calibration on in-distribution validation data did not carry over well, while methods that marginalize over models (such as ensembles) held up better.

A complementary, assumption-light approach is **conformal prediction**. Angelopoulos and Bates [17] give an accessible introduction: using a held-out calibration set, conformal methods turn any pre-trained model's scores into prediction *sets* that contain the true label with a user-chosen probability, assuming the calibration and test data are exchangeable. It does not require the model's probabilities to be calibrated, which makes it attractive for vision and language systems that are used as black boxes.

### Mutual information in representation learning

Mutual information became a popular objective for self-supervised learning ([ML 06](/en/learn/ml/representation-learning/)). Contrastive Predictive Coding [18] introduced the **InfoNCE** loss, which classifies a positive pair among negatives and can be shown to maximize a lower bound on mutual information. Poole et al. [19] (ICML 2019) unified many variational MI bounds and showed that they degrade when the true MI is large, trading high bias against high variance. Tschannen et al. [20] (ICLR 2020) argued that the success of these methods "cannot be attributed to the properties of MI alone": the encoder architecture and the parameterization of the estimator matter at least as much. The modern consensus is to treat InfoNCE-style objectives as well-behaved contrastive losses with an information-theoretic motivation, not as precise MI estimators.

### Divergences and latent variables in generative models

Generative modelling is applied probability. VAEs [8] maximize an evidence lower bound that combines a reconstruction likelihood with a KL term; Blei et al. [10] review variational inference, which approximates intractable posteriors by minimizing (reverse) KL within a tractable family. Normalizing flows [9] are built entirely on the change-of-variables formula. Diffusion and flow-matching models ([ML 08](/en/learn/ml/diffusion-flow-matching/)) combine Gaussian noise, conditional expectations and likelihood bounds. The choice of divergence (forward KL, reverse KL, or something else) shapes whether a model covers all the data or concentrates on its modes — the same picture as Figure 5, at scale.

### Open problems

- **Calibration under shift.** Calibration that holds on a validation set can fail on new cameras, hospitals or prompts [16]; detecting when this happens is an open question.
- **Separating uncertainty types.** Decomposing predictive uncertainty into epistemic and aleatoric parts is conceptually clear but hard to do reliably for large networks [14].
- **Estimating information in high dimensions.** Variational MI bounds have fundamental bias–variance limits [19], so information-theoretic claims about deep representations need care.
- **Uncertainty for foundation models.** Applying calibration and conformal methods to large vision–language and language models, whose outputs are open-ended, is an active research area [12], [17].

## Key takeaways

- Probability describes uncertainty from randomness, partial observation and simplified models; frequentist and Bayesian readings share the same rules.
- A PMF assigns probabilities; a PDF assigns densities, which can exceed one. Probabilities of continuous events are areas.
- The sum rule (marginalization), product rule (conditioning) and chain rule are exact; independence and conditional independence are modelling assumptions.
- Covariance measures linear co-variation only — zero covariance does not imply independence; mutual information detects any dependence.
- Choosing a distribution is choosing a loss: Bernoulli/categorical give cross-entropy, Gaussian gives squared error, Laplace gives absolute error; Gaussian and Laplace priors give $L_2$ and $L_1$ regularization.
- Bayes' rule combines evidence with base rates; the same detector can be trustworthy at one prevalence and misleading at another.
- Transforming a random variable rescales its density by the absolute Jacobian determinant.
- Cross-entropy equals entropy plus KL; minimizing it is maximum likelihood. KL is asymmetric: forward KL covers modes, reverse KL seeks one.
