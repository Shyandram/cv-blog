## What you'll learn

- Distinguish morphological properties, part-of-speech tags, phrase structure and dependencies.
- Explain why independent word labels can disagree with a valid sentence-level analysis.
- Derive hidden Markov sequence scores and maximum-path decoding.
- Specify a context-free grammar and implement a bottom-up chart parser.
- Preserve and evaluate structural ambiguity rather than forcing one unexplained tree.
- Connect explicit syntax with contextual encoders and current language-model evaluation.

## The big picture

Language has a linear surface and a structured interpretation. A sentence arrives as an ordered sequence of words, but readers group those words and identify relations among them. “We saw robots with cameras” might describe camera-carrying robots or the equipment used to see them. The word list alone does not choose one interpretation.

McRoy's Chapters 3 and 4 introduce grammatical categories and syntactic processing, with a separate chart-parsing appendix [1]. Hagiwara's Chapter 5 develops sequential labeling, including part-of-speech tagging [2]. This chapter combines those views. A tagger attaches categories to positions; a parser searches for a structure linking positions. Both are structured prediction tasks when choices at one position constrain choices elsewhere.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
A sentence is like a construction kit. The words are pieces, and grammar describes ways to connect them. Tagging labels the pieces; parsing shows the connections. Sometimes the same pieces make two sensible constructions. A good explanation shows both, then asks which fits the situation.
</Callout>

Syntax is useful for extracting who did what, locating modifiers, identifying argument boundaries and creating tests that distinguish language fluency from structural understanding. It does not settle every semantic question. A grammatically valid sentence may be false, and a misspelled or fragmentary message may still communicate clearly. We therefore treat syntax as an explicit representation with a known scope, not as a universal definition of understanding.

## Words, morphology and grammatical categories

**Plain version.** A word's category describes how it behaves in a sentence. Its spelling can suggest a category, but context often decides which use is intended.

Morphology concerns structure within words. A plural ending, tense marker or derivational prefix can supply useful evidence, but not every language expresses these properties with the same pieces or word boundaries. Subword tokens learned for efficient modeling also need not correspond to linguistic morphemes. Keep the model's tokenization separate from the grammatical units used in annotation.

Part of speech describes a grammatical category, such as noun or verb. A noun commonly heads a noun phrase; a verb commonly heads a clause's predicate. These are distributional descriptions rather than rules that every noun must name a physical object. “Decision” and “arrival” are nouns although they refer to abstract content and events.

The same surface word can have several categories. In the invented pair “the pilots can land” and “the metal can fell,” the word “can” has different roles. The second sentence also demonstrates why a familiar local phrase can be misleading when the remainder of the sentence changes. A tagger must consider the permitted context and its annotation scheme.

Represent a tagged sentence as:

$$
\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_T),
\qquad
\mathbf y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T),
\qquad
y_t\in\mathcal Y.
$$

Here $\mathbf x$ is the input word sequence, $x_t$ is the word at position $t$, $T$ is sequence length, $\mathbf y$ is the tag sequence, $y_t$ is the tag for word $x_t$ and $\mathcal Y$ is the finite tag inventory. The symbols $t$ and $T$ refer to linguistic word positions, not automatically to model subword positions.

Annotation needs additional rules: contractions, punctuation, multiword expressions and words with several plausible analyses. When a model uses subwords, define how its outputs return to words. Using only the first subword's representation is one possible convention; pooling all subwords is another. Whatever the choice, gold labels and decoded output must use the same units.

## Local classification versus sequence prediction

**Plain version.** Choosing the best tag for every word separately can miss a better joint interpretation. A sequence model makes the relationship among tags explicit.

A local classifier may predict:

$$
\hat y_t=\operatorname*{argmax}_{k\in\mathcal Y}
p_\theta(y_t=k\mid\mathbf x).
$$

Here $\hat y_t$ is the selected tag at position $t$, $k$ ranges over tag inventory $\mathcal Y$, $p_\theta$ is a model with parameters $\theta$ and $\mathbf x$ is the whole visible input sequence. A local output head can still read contextual input: “independent” describes the output decision rule, not necessarily the encoder.

A structured decoder instead chooses:

$$
\hat{\mathbf y}
=\operatorname*{argmax}_{\mathbf y\in\mathcal A(\mathbf x)}
S_\theta(\mathbf x,\mathbf y).
$$

Here $\hat{\mathbf y}$ is the selected complete tag sequence, $\mathcal A(\mathbf x)$ is the set of permitted analyses for input $\mathbf x$, and $S_\theta$ is a score assigned by parameters $\theta$ to an input–analysis pair. The permitted set can enforce an annotation constraint, while the score can reward plausible neighboring labels.

This distinction appears in many NLP tasks. Entity labels must form valid spans; a dependency tree needs one parent per non-root word; a generated structured record must satisfy a schema. A stronger encoder does not automatically guarantee a valid output. The decoder or a separate validator must enforce constraints that the application requires.

Structured decoding also has a tradeoff. An incorrect hard constraint can exclude a legitimate construction. If the grammar says every sentence must contain an overt subject, it will reject many commands and fragments. Document the constraint and its intended input domain rather than treating constrained output as evidence that the constraint was linguistically universal.

## Hidden Markov models for tags

**Plain version.** Imagine that tags form a hidden sequence and each tag generates a word. Neighboring tags and word compatibility then jointly determine the score.

A first-order hidden Markov model (HMM), reviewed by Rabiner [3], uses:

$$
p(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)
=\pi_{y_1}b_{y_1}(x_1)
\prod_{t=2}^{T}a_{y_{t-1},y_t}b_{y_t}(x_t).
$$

Here $p(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)$ is joint probability of words and tags, $\pi_k$ is the probability of initial tag $k$, $a_{j,k}$ is the transition probability from tag $j$ to tag $k$, $b_k(w)$ is the probability that tag $k$ emits word $w$, $x_t$ and $y_t$ are the word and tag at position $t$, and $T$ is sequence length. This example conditions on a fixed length; an explicit end-state factor can be added when modeling termination.

Initial probabilities sum to one. Each transition row sums to one, and each emission distribution sums to one over the observation vocabulary. These normalization conditions make the expression a probability model rather than an arbitrary product of useful scores.

The assumptions are restrictive: the next tag depends only on the previous tag, and an emitted word depends only on the current tag. A real sentence has long dependencies, lexical preferences and discourse context. The value of this model here is that the assumptions and exact decoder are visible. It provides a small laboratory for distinguishing model capacity from search.

With fully tagged training examples, count initial states, transitions and emissions, then normalize with a stated smoothing rule. With words alone, hidden-state estimation is a different problem; a state learned without supervision is not automatically a named grammatical category. The example below uses manually specified probabilities so that training uncertainty does not obscure decoding.

## Maximum-path decoding

**Plain version.** Keep the best partial path ending in each tag. Extending those paths is enough to find the best complete sequence without listing every possibility.

Use log probabilities to turn products into sums:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_1(k)&=\log\pi_k+\log b_k(x_1),\\
D_t(k)&=\log b_k(x_t)+
\max_{j\in\mathcal Y}\bigl[D_{t-1}(j)+\log a_{j,k}\bigr].
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $D_t(k)$ is the log score of the best path covering positions $1$ through $t$ and ending with tag $k$, $\pi_k$ is the initial probability, $b_k(x_t)$ is the emission probability, $a_{j,k}$ is the transition probability and $\mathcal Y$ is the tag inventory. The indices $j$ and $k$ identify the previous and current tags. A zero probability contributes $-\infty$.

The recurrence is maximum-path dynamic programming, commonly called Viterbi decoding [3]. For each cell, store the previous tag attaining the maximum. After selecting the best final tag, follow these backpointers in reverse. Keeping only the score loses the actual sequence.

The decoder considers $T$ positions and all pairs of adjacent tags, so its time complexity is $O(TK^2)$ for $K=|\mathcal Y|$ tags. The score table uses $O(TK)$ space if all backpointers are retained. By contrast, enumerating tag sequences considers $K^T$ possibilities. This is a computational saving under a specific factorization, not an approximate search result.

The following standalone example verifies the dynamic-programming answer against exhaustive enumeration. The toy observation “fish fish” has an intentionally ambiguous word. Independent emission preferences select nouns at both positions; the joint model can favor a noun followed by a verb.

```python
import itertools
import numpy as np

tags = ["N", "V"]
vocabulary = ["fish", "swim"]
initial = np.array([0.8, 0.2])
transition = np.array([[0.1, 0.9], [0.8, 0.2]])
emission = np.array([[0.7, 0.3], [0.3, 0.7]])
words = ["fish", "fish"]
observation = [vocabulary.index(word) for word in words]
assert np.allclose(transition.sum(axis=1), 1)
assert np.allclose(emission.sum(axis=1), 1)

scores = np.empty((len(words), len(tags)))
back = np.zeros_like(scores, dtype=int)
scores[0] = np.log(initial) + np.log(emission[:, observation[0]])
for t in range(1, len(words)):
    candidates = scores[t - 1, :, None] + np.log(transition)
    back[t] = candidates.argmax(axis=0)
    scores[t] = candidates.max(axis=0) + np.log(emission[:, observation[t]])
path = [int(scores[-1].argmax())]
for t in range(len(words) - 1, 0, -1):
    path.append(int(back[t, path[-1]]))
path.reverse()

def joint_score(sequence):
    value = np.log(initial[sequence[0]])
    value += np.log(emission[sequence[0], observation[0]])
    for t in range(1, len(words)):
        value += np.log(transition[sequence[t - 1], sequence[t]])
        value += np.log(emission[sequence[t], observation[t]])
    return value

all_paths = list(itertools.product(range(len(tags)), repeat=len(words)))
brute = max(all_paths, key=joint_score)
assert tuple(path) == brute
print("local emission choices:", [tags[emission[:, w].argmax()] for w in observation])
print("best joint path:", [tags[index] for index in path])
print("joint log score:", round(joint_score(path), 3))
```

Changing transition probabilities changes the chosen sequence even when every word-level preference stays fixed. This does not prove the new choice is correct in real language. It proves that the implemented model and decoder express the intended dependence.

## Constituents and context-free grammars

**Plain version.** Phrase structure groups words into units that can combine recursively. A grammar is a specification of permitted combinations, while a parse is one derivation for a particular sentence.

A context-free grammar can be specified as:

$$
G=(\mathcal N,\Sigma,\mathcal R,S).
$$

Here $G$ is the grammar, $\mathcal N$ is the set of nonterminal categories, $\Sigma$ is the terminal vocabulary, $\mathcal R$ is the set of rewrite rules and $S\in\mathcal N$ is the start category. Nonterminals represent structures such as a noun phrase; terminals are the observed words. A context-free rule replaces one nonterminal without inspecting its surrounding symbols.

For an original toy grammar, use rules such as $S\rightarrow NP\ VP$, $VP\rightarrow V\ NP$, $PP\rightarrow P\ NP$ and $NP\rightarrow NP\ PP$. Here $S$ denotes a sentence, $NP$ a noun phrase, $VP$ a verb phrase, $PP$ a prepositional phrase, $V$ a verb category and $P$ a preposition category. These are notation choices for the toy grammar, not a complete grammar of English.

Recursive rules can generate unbounded families from finite specifications. The same recursion can also make naive search revisit work or loop indefinitely. A parser therefore needs a search strategy, termination rules and a compact representation of partial analyses.

Distinguish coverage from preference. A rule system can permit a sentence while assigning several analyses. Conversely, it can fail to parse a perfectly reasonable sentence because a lexical entry or production is missing. A parsing failure says that the implemented grammar and search did not produce an analysis; it does not establish that the user wrote nonsense.

McRoy discusses grammars, search and charts as explicit computational representations [1]. In an application, the important benefit is inspectability: one can show which rule connected spans and where ambiguity arose. This is especially useful before feeding a structure to an extraction system that would otherwise silently select the wrong modifier.

## Chart parsing by spans

### Top-down hypotheses and bottom-up evidence

**Plain version.** A parser can start from what a sentence is expected to contain, or from what the observed words already support. Both directions need a way to avoid repeating the same work.

A top-down search begins with the start category and expands a production into expected children. It can reject lexical alternatives that do not fit the current expectation, but a poorly chosen expansion can send it down a long unsuccessful path. A recursive production that expands its first child into the same category can revisit the same unresolved expectation before consuming any input.

A bottom-up search begins with observed words and constructs categories that their spans support. It can create many phrases that never contribute to a complete sentence. This is wasted work from the viewpoint of a particular final derivation, but the intermediate results can be shared by several successful analyses.

The important design question is therefore not simply which direction is “better.” Ask how partial work is represented, which constraints are applied, and when two partial analyses count as the same search state. A chart can store completed spans and, in more general implementations, partially completed rule applications. A state that already exists need not be recomputed merely because another path reaches it.

Memoization alone does not settle ambiguity. Two derivations can share a span and category yet differ internally. If the task is only recognition, merging them into one Boolean state is enough. If the task requires the preferred parse, the state needs a score and backpointer. If the task requires all alternatives, it needs a forest that retains distinct derivation links.

These distinctions also explain why adding recursion to a grammar requires care in an implementation. A grammar can be mathematically well-defined while a naive recursive parser fails to terminate. Conversely, a parser can terminate quickly by pruning away an analysis that the grammar permits. State clearly whether the algorithm is exhaustive for the implemented grammar or uses a bounded search.

For the next executable example, bottom-up binary spans make the dependency order especially simple: every parent depends only on shorter children. That limited grammar is an educational choice. Supporting unary productions, empty spans or a wider grammar requires a corresponding algorithmic extension, not an extra dictionary entry pasted into the same loops.

**Plain version.** Store what each contiguous span can be. Build longer spans by joining shorter ones and share their results across all complete parses.

Use half-open boundaries: the span $[i,j)$ contains tokens at positions $i$ through $j-1$. For a grammar with binary rules and lexical rules, recognition follows:

$$
C[i,j,A]=
\bigvee_{\substack{A\rightarrow BC\in\mathcal R\\i<k<j}}
\left(C[i,k,B]\land C[k,j,C]\right).
$$

Here $C[i,j,A]$ is a Boolean statement that category $A$ covers span $[i,j)$, $\mathcal R$ is the binary rule set, $A\rightarrow BC$ combines left category $B$ with right category $C$, $k$ is an interior split boundary, $\land$ means both children must exist and $\bigvee$ means at least one rule-and-split combination succeeds. The letter $C$ is used both for the chart notation and for a right-child category; its role is identified by brackets versus a category argument.

Initialize length-one cells from lexical rules. Then iterate over span lengths, start positions, split positions and compatible rules. Because children are shorter than their parent, they have already been computed. A unary rule or empty production needs additional handling; this lesson's executable grammar deliberately excludes both.

This algorithm recognizes an input, but recognition alone cannot explain its trees. Store successful combinations as backpointers or a packed forest. Several parents can share a child span rather than duplicating its full tree. Expanding all complete trees can still cost exponential time when a sentence is highly ambiguous, even if recognition is polynomial.

For fixed binary grammar rules, the nested span-and-split loops have cubic dependence on token length. The chart has quadratically many spans, each with possible categories. These costs concern the specified algorithm, not every modern parser. Neural or transition-based methods use different representations and search choices.

## A parser that preserves two analyses

**Plain version.** When the grammar permits two attachments, report the alternatives. A parser should not discard ambiguity merely because one option was inserted first.

The following standalone parser stores complete small trees in chart cells. This is intentionally simpler than a packed-forest implementation. It is suitable for the five-word demonstration, while the preceding section explains why explicit enumeration is unsuitable for large ambiguous inputs.

```python
from collections import defaultdict

words = "we saw robots with cameras".split()
lexical = {
    "we": ["NP"], "saw": ["V"], "robots": ["NP"],
    "with": ["P"], "cameras": ["NP"],
}
rules = [
    ("S", "NP", "VP"),
    ("VP", "V", "NP"),
    ("VP", "VP", "PP"),
    ("NP", "NP", "PP"),
    ("PP", "P", "NP"),
]
chart = defaultdict(lambda: defaultdict(list))
for i, word in enumerate(words):
    for category in lexical.get(word, []):
        chart[i, i + 1][category].append((category, word))
for length in range(2, len(words) + 1):
    for start in range(len(words) - length + 1):
        end = start + length
        for split in range(start + 1, end):
            for parent, left, right in rules:
                for left_tree in chart[start, split].get(left, []):
                    for right_tree in chart[split, end].get(right, []):
                        tree = (parent, left_tree, right_tree)
                        if tree not in chart[start, end][parent]:
                            chart[start, end][parent].append(tree)

def bracket(tree):
    if len(tree) == 2:
        return f"({tree[0]} {tree[1]})"
    return f"({tree[0]} {bracket(tree[1])} {bracket(tree[2])})"

parses = chart[0, len(words)].get("S", [])
assert len(parses) == 2
for tree in parses:
    print(bracket(tree))
print("complete parses:", len(parses))
```

In one tree, the prepositional phrase belongs inside the object noun phrase. In the other, it modifies the verb phrase. Both use every word in order and satisfy the grammar. Choosing between them may require the surrounding scene, prior discourse or lexical preferences beyond these rules.

Add an unknown word and the chart may become empty at a lexical cell. The correct response is a coverage diagnosis. A production service might use a fallback tagger, allow an explicit unknown category or ask for clarification, but each fallback changes the recognized language. It should be documented and tested.

## Probabilities over structures

**Plain version.** A grammar says which analyses exist. A probability model can prefer some analyses and summarize uncertainty across them.

A probabilistic context-free grammar assigns a probability to each production, normalized among rules with the same parent. A derivation has:

$$
p(\mathcal T)=\prod_{r\in\mathcal T}p(r),
\qquad
\hat{\mathcal T}=\operatorname*{argmax}_{\mathcal T\in\mathcal P(\mathbf x)}
p(\mathcal T).
$$

Here $\mathcal T$ is a complete derivation tree, $r$ indexes each production occurrence in that tree, $p(r)$ is its production probability, $\mathcal P(\mathbf x)$ is the set of permitted parses whose terminal yield is the input $\mathbf x$, and $\hat{\mathcal T}$ is the highest-probability parse. The product includes repeated uses of a rule separately.

Replacing chart alternatives with sums computes total probability mass over derivations. Replacing them with maxima computes the best derivation. These answer different questions: a sentence with many moderately probable parses can have greater total probability than a sentence with one strong parse. The best tree need not explain most of the posterior mass.

Rule probabilities estimated from a treebank depend on the annotation convention and domain. A news-style treebank and a support-message corpus can have different fragment patterns and attachment preferences. Probabilistic modeling supplies a preference mechanism, but it does not remove domain dependence.

One can also use a contextual encoder to score spans, rules or dependencies. Then the syntax representation and the feature computation are separate: the output remains a tree even if its scores come from a large neural network. Compare output validity and parsing accuracy explicitly rather than inferring structural competence from the encoder's size.

## Dependency structure and grammatical roles

**Plain version.** Dependency analysis links words directly. It highlights which word depends on which other word and labels their grammatical relationship.

A basic dependency tree can be represented by head indices:

$$
h_t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}\setminus\{t\},
\qquad
r_t\in\mathcal D.
$$

Here $h_t$ is the head index of word $t$, $0$ denotes an artificial root, $T$ is sentence length, $r_t$ is the relation label for that word and $\mathcal D$ is the relation inventory. A valid basic tree additionally requires a specified root convention, connectivity and absence of directed cycles.

Independent head choices can violate those global requirements. Two words might select each other, forming a cycle. Several disconnected components might attach without a unique sentence root. A tree decoder must enforce the intended structure, or the service must validate and reject an invalid analysis.

Universal Dependencies provides a cross-linguistic annotation framework for morphology, part of speech and grammatical dependencies [4]. Its categories aim for useful comparability across languages while retaining language-specific distinctions. This is an annotation framework, not a claim that every language has identical word order or overt grammatical markers.

Phrase structure and dependencies emphasize different information. A constituent tree makes span grouping explicit. A dependency tree makes lexical heads and relations explicit. Converting between them requires conventions for heads, punctuation and function words; the conversion is not merely a drawing change.

For extraction, grammatical roles are useful evidence but not interchangeable with semantic roles. A passive sentence changes grammatical subject placement while preserving who acted on whom. The next lesson develops that distinction and explains why a dependency arrow does not by itself establish a real-world relation.

## Evaluation and practical failure modes

**Plain version.** Compare the representation that the parser promises to produce, and inspect mistakes where tokenization, annotation or context changes the answer.

Tag accuracy compares predicted and reference labels at aligned word positions. Constituency evaluation often compares labeled spans after stating whether lexical preterminals, punctuation and the root are included. A dependency evaluation can compare heads alone or heads together with relation labels. Different inclusion rules can change the denominator; publish the convention alongside the score.

For labeled dependency attachment:

$$
\operatorname{LAS}=
\frac1M\sum_{t\in\mathcal I}
\mathbf1[(\hat h_t,\hat r_t)=(h_t,r_t)].
$$

Here $\operatorname{LAS}$ is labeled attachment score, $\mathcal I$ is the set of evaluated word positions, $M=|\mathcal I|$ is its size, $\hat h_t$ and $\hat r_t$ are predicted head and relation, $h_t$ and $r_t$ are reference head and relation, and $\mathbf1[\cdot]$ is the indicator function. The Universal Dependencies framework defines the structures to which such comparisons apply [4]. Token alignment must be fixed before applying this formula.

Separate a parser's structural errors from input segmentation errors. If the gold annotation has two words where the tokenizer emits one, an attachment comparison cannot be interpreted normally. Report the segmentation mismatch and specify how alignment is handled. Do not silently delete difficult examples.

Build small diagnostic pairs. Move a modifier while keeping vocabulary similar; add a nested clause; replace an active construction with a passive one; include a fragment or a code-switched phrase. These tests reveal the conditions under which a model's structure changes, even when an aggregate score appears stable.

Avoid treating one gold tree as certainty when the sentence is genuinely ambiguous. A corpus needs a chosen convention or adjudicated analysis, but another analysis may be linguistically reasonable. For an application, ask whether the disagreement changes the extracted fact or action. That connects a parsing error to its practical consequence.

## Modern view

**Survey perspective.** Zhao et al.'s 2023–2026 LLM survey distinguishes model training, adaptation, utilization and capacity evaluation [5]. Syntax can play several roles within that taxonomy: a supervised output, an internal representational probe, a constraint on generation or a diagnostic challenge. Chang et al.'s evaluation survey further separates the tasks being measured from the benchmarks and protocols used [6]. Neither fluent generation nor a general evaluation score substitutes for checking a requested tree representation.

**Recent original work.** Tian, Xia and Song's 2024 study examines language-model constituency parsing and methods to obtain more faithful structural output [7]. Hale and Stanojević's 2024 targeted study tests a syntactic universal across languages [8]. The latter's results differ by language, illustrating why an English-focused structural test cannot establish uniform multilingual competence. These studies address different questions: generating an annotated parse and exhibiting a particular syntactic generalization.

**Practice in 2023–2026.** Contextual encoders can provide rich evidence for tags and trees, while large language models can be prompted to return structures [5], [7]. An output parser and structural validator remain necessary. A bracketed string that looks plausible can omit a token, reorder words or fail to form a connected tree. Validate yield, category inventory and global constraints before consuming it.

**Open problems.** Annotation schemes differ; low-resource languages lack enough independently checked trees; segmentation interacts with syntax; spontaneous dialogue contains fragments and repairs; and a system can learn benchmark-specific conventions without robust compositional behavior. Preserving alternative analyses, testing structural minimal pairs and measuring downstream consequences offer concrete ways to study those limits.

## Key takeaways

- Linguistic word units and model subword units need an explicit alignment.
- Contextual features and structured output constraints are separate design choices.
- Maximum-path decoding is exact under the stated factorization and assumptions.
- A grammar's failure to parse is a coverage result, not a judgment of a speaker.
- Charts share spans; enumerating all trees can still grow exponentially.
- Phrase structure and dependencies represent different aspects of syntax.
- Validate structural outputs and state evaluation denominators clearly.
- Syntax contributes evidence for meaning but does not determine truth or intent.
