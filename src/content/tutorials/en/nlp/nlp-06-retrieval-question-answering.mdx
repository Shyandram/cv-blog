## The big picture

A language model can continue a question without searching a current document collection. A retrieval system instead selects information from an explicit corpus. Question answering adds another decision: whether the selected information supports an answer, and which words express it. Putting these components together creates a useful pipeline, but no stage automatically certifies the next.

McRoy's Chapter 8 connects question answering, text retrieval, information extraction and argumentation [1]. Hagiwara's Chapters 3 and 9 provide the representation and pretrained-model background, while Chapters 10–11 address application development and deployment [2]. We supplement those readings with classical information retrieval and current retrieval-augmented generation research. The example stays small enough to inspect every document and decision.

## Corpus design before model choice

**Plain version.** A retriever searches what you give it. Document identity, freshness and permissions are part of the system, not optional metadata added after ranking.

Represent a searchable passage as:

$$
d=(u,v,b,e,\mathbf x,a).
$$

Here $d$ is a passage record; $u$ is the parent document identifier; $v$ is its version; $b$ and $e$ are inclusive-start and exclusive-end offsets in the original document; $\mathbf x$ is passage text; and $a$ describes its access policy. The offset unit must be declared. This is our application record, rather than a universal storage standard.

A passage may be a paragraph, a sentence group or a section selected by document structure. Small passages can isolate an answer but lose its qualifications. Large passages retain context but introduce unrelated text and consume more generation budget. Choose chunk boundaries based on the task, then evaluate whether the supporting facts and their conditions remain together.

For example, a heading may specify “Outdoor equipment only,” followed by a paragraph containing a temperature limit. If the paragraph is indexed alone, it may appear to apply to indoor equipment. Retaining the heading or a structured scope field changes the answerability contract. A fixed character window is easy to implement, but simplicity does not establish that it preserves meaning.

Versions also matter. An old policy can be relevant yet inapplicable. Keep effective dates and replacement links when the application needs current answers. Do not rely on a generator to infer the current policy merely because the newest passage appears last in the prompt. Filter or rank by declared applicability rules, and make conflicts visible when the corpus does not identify a single authoritative source.

Apply permissions before returning passage text to an unauthorized component or user. Filtering the final visible answer is too late if private text was already included in a model request or log. The retriever's corpus view should match the current user's permitted view. Measure retrieval quality under that restricted view; global relevance scores can otherwise evaluate documents the system may never show.

## Lexical retrieval and sparse vectors

**Plain version.** Lexical retrieval matches words and weights informative terms. It is inspectable and often useful when exact names, identifiers and technical phrases matter.

A simple term-frequency and inverse-document-frequency weight is:

$$
w_{t,d}=\operatorname{tf}(t,d)\log\frac{N}{\operatorname{df}(t)}.
$$

Here $w_{t,d}$ is the weight of term $t$ in document $d$; $\operatorname{tf}(t,d)$ is its frequency under a chosen convention; $N$ is the number of indexed documents; and $\operatorname{df}(t)$ is the count of documents containing the term. A zero document frequency requires special handling for query-only terms. Implementations often smooth the formula or transform term frequency; record the actual convention [3, 4].

With normalized document and query vectors:

$$
s(q,d)=\frac{\mathbf v_q^\top\mathbf v_d}
{\|\mathbf v_q\|_2\|\mathbf v_d\|_2}.
$$

Here $q$ is a query; $d$ is a candidate document; $\mathbf v_q,\mathbf v_d$ are their vectors; $s$ is cosine similarity; and $\|\cdot\|_2$ is Euclidean norm. Define the score as zero when either vector is zero. The code uses normalized vectors from scikit-learn, so their dot product implements cosine similarity for nonzero rows [4].

An inverted index stores, for each term, the documents where it occurs. That allows a search engine to consider matching candidates without comparing a query with every vocabulary entry in every document [3]. Our small code computes all document scores because the corpus contains only four passages. The mathematical representation and the efficient indexing strategy are separate choices.

Preprocessing directly affects retrieval. A tokenizer that discards one-character tokens can remove the A in Pump A. Lowercasing may be helpful for ordinary words but can erase distinctions in case-sensitive identifiers. Phrase features help distinguish adjacent words, but do not capture arbitrary syntax or guarantee semantic equivalence. Inspect the vocabulary before blaming the ranking model for a missed result.

Lexical systems can miss paraphrases. “Device ceased operating” and “machine shutdown” may share little wording. A synonym or query-expansion rule can help, but changes the matching policy and may add ambiguity. Evaluate expansion on held-out queries, especially exact-identifier cases. A broad expansion that improves recall can also retrieve a wrong device with similar terminology.

## Saturation and document length

**Plain version.** Repeating a query word many times should not always keep increasing relevance. Longer documents also have more opportunities to match by chance.

One common BM25 form is:

$$
s(q,d)=\sum_{t\in q}
\operatorname{idf}(t)
\frac{f_{t,d}(k_1+1)}
{f_{t,d}+k_1\left(1-b+b\,|d|/\overline L\right)}.
$$

Here $q$ is the query-term set in this simplified form; $d$ is the candidate document; $f_{t,d}$ is term frequency; $\operatorname{idf}(t)$ is a chosen inverse-document-frequency weight; $|d|$ is document length in tokens; $\overline L$ is average document length; $k_1$ is a positive saturation parameter; and $b\in[0,1]$ controls length normalization. Different implementations use different IDF and query-frequency conventions. The information-retrieval textbook discusses this family and its assumptions [3].

The numerator and denominator make the term-frequency contribution approach a limit as repetition grows. The length term changes how much frequency is expected in a longer document. These are ranking adjustments, not a probability that the document contains a true answer. A passage can rank highly because it repeatedly discusses a limit while never stating the value.

Tune length normalization on development queries that reflect the deployment corpus. If every useful answer is in a short reference entry while irrelevant documents are long narratives, a particular setting can look very strong. Changing to long technical manuals may alter that behavior. Keep passage construction fixed when comparing ranking settings, or report that both the corpus representation and score changed.

## Dense retrieval and joint reranking

**Plain version.** A learned encoder can match related expressions without exact word overlap. Its success depends on the training task and the examples used as contrasts.

A dual-encoder score is:

$$
s_\theta(q,d)=f_\theta(q)^\top g_\theta(d).
$$

Here $f_\theta$ maps queries to vectors; $g_\theta$ maps passages to vectors; $\theta$ denotes learned parameters; and $s_\theta$ is their dot-product score. The encoders may share parameters or use distinct ones. Dense Passage Retrieval provides an original question-answering example of this design [5].

Passage vectors can be computed ahead of time. A query then searches those vectors for high scores. Approximate search trades some exact nearest-neighbor fidelity for speed; if used, evaluate both the representation and the search approximation. Our chapter does not implement such an index, because an exact small corpus is better for learning the contract.

A contrastive training loss can compare one positive passage with several negative passages:

$$
\mathcal L=-\log
\frac{\exp(s_\theta(q,d^+)/\tau)}
{\exp(s_\theta(q,d^+)/\tau)+\sum_j\exp(s_\theta(q,d_j^-)/\tau)}.
$$

Here $\mathcal L$ is the loss for one query; $d^+$ is an annotated positive passage; $d_j^-$ is negative candidate $j$; $\tau$ is a positive temperature; and $s_\theta$ is the learned score. Negatives may include difficult passages with similar wording. A passage labeled negative merely because it was not annotated can be a false negative if it also contains a valid answer.

A joint reranker reads a query and candidate passage together, which permits richer token interactions than comparing two fixed vectors. It is typically applied after a cheaper candidate retrieval stage. This adds computation and another selection procedure. Report candidate recall before reranking: a reranker cannot recover the missing correct passage if it never receives it.

Dense and lexical retrieval can be complementary. Dense representations may help paraphrases; lexical matching can preserve exact codes and rare names. Combining score scales requires care because a lexical score and a learned dot product do not have the same units. Select combination settings on development data, and inspect whether the combined system introduces wrong-entity results.

## Ranking evaluation

**Plain version.** Retrieval evaluation needs queries with relevance judgments. A document being selected does not establish that it was the right one.

For query $q$ with relevant-document set $R_q$ and top-$k$ result set $T_k(q)$:

$$
\operatorname{Recall@}k(q)=\frac{|R_q\cap T_k(q)|}{|R_q|}.
$$

Here $k$ is the cutoff; $R_q$ contains the annotated relevant documents; $T_k(q)$ contains the retrieved documents up to that cutoff; and $|\cdot|$ denotes set size. We evaluate this expression only for queries with at least one relevant document. Queries with no answer belong in a separate answerability evaluation, rather than receiving an arbitrary recall value.

If the first relevant result has rank $r_q$:

$$
\operatorname{RR}(q)=\frac{1}{r_q},\qquad
\operatorname{MRR}=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\operatorname{RR}(q).
$$

Here $\operatorname{RR}$ is reciprocal rank; $\operatorname{MRR}$ is its mean; $Q$ is the number of evaluated queries; and $r_q$ starts at one. Define reciprocal rank as zero when no relevant result is returned. These ranking diagnostics appear in the current retrieval-augmented evaluation discussion [6]. Reciprocal rank emphasizes the first successful hit and ignores later relevant results.

Annotation completeness matters. If only one passage is judged relevant when several support the answer, a valid retrieval can be scored as wrong. Record whether relevance means topic match, answer-containing evidence or complete support. Use multiple judgments when an ambiguity is consequential, and adjudicate according to a written guide. The dataset should expose the task definition, not merely list a favorite passage.

Keep query families and document derivatives separate across development and test. Rephrasing a development query into a nearly identical test query gives weak evidence of generalization. Likewise, splitting chunks from one report across document partitions can leak facts and wording. Choose a split reflecting the deployment question: unseen users, unseen documents, future versions or new domains.

## A local evidence-backed answerer

**Plain version.** We can demonstrate retrieval and support checks without a large generator. The answerer handles one explicit question family and returns a source interval.

The standalone code uses four synthetic passages. It preserves one-character words so that the device identifier A remains a feature. It measures ranking on three annotated queries, then answers only a narrow temperature-limit question for Pump A. The function is not a general classifier for question intent; its caller must route the declared question family to it.

```python
import re
import numpy as np
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

corpus = {
    'temperature-v2': 'Pump A temperature limit is 80 C. This policy is current.',
    'shutdown-v1': 'Pump A shutdown follows a vibration alarm.',
    'label-v1': 'A technician replaced the cabinet label.',
    'sensor-v1': 'Sensor B reports humidity once per hour.',
}
ids = list(corpus)
vectorizer = TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), token_pattern=r"(?u)\b\w+\b")
D = vectorizer.fit_transform([corpus[key] for key in ids])
def rank(query):
    score = (D @ vectorizer.transform([query]).T).toarray().ravel()
    order = sorted(range(len(ids)), key=lambda i: (-score[i], ids[i]))
    return [(ids[i], float(score[i])) for i in order]
queries = [
    ('Pump A temperature limit', {'temperature-v2'}),
    ('vibration shutdown alarm', {'shutdown-v1'}),
    ('humidity report', {'sensor-v1'}),
]
recalls, reciprocal = [], []
for query, relevant in queries:
    ranking = rank(query)
    recalls.append(len({key for key, _ in ranking[:2]} & relevant) / len(relevant))
    first = next((i + 1 for i, (key, _) in enumerate(ranking) if key in relevant), None)
    reciprocal.append(1 / first if first else 0.0)
    print(query, '->', ranking[0][0])
print('mean Recall@2:', np.mean(recalls))
print('mean reciprocal rank:', np.mean(reciprocal))

def answer_temperature(query):
    if not re.search(r'\bpump\s+a\b', query, flags=re.I):
        return {'answer': None, 'reason': 'unsupported entity'}
    key, score = rank(query)[0]
    text = corpus[key]
    pattern = r'Pump A temperature limit is (\d+) C\.'
    match = re.search(pattern, text)
    if not match:
        return {'answer': None, 'reason': 'no matching evidence'}
    evidence = text[match.start():match.end()]
    result = {'answer': f'{match.group(1)} C', 'document': key,
              'start': match.start(), 'end': match.end(), 'evidence': evidence}
    assert text[result['start']:result['end']] == result['evidence']
    return result
print(answer_temperature('What is the Pump A temperature limit?'))
print(answer_temperature('What is the Pump Z temperature limit?'))
```

The three top results match their single annotated relevant passages, producing mean Recall@2 and mean reciprocal rank of 1.0 in this small run. That is a fixture result, not a real retrieval benchmark. The supported Pump A answer returns 80 C, its document version and a character interval. A Pump Z question returns no answer even though temperature-related passages exist.

The explicit entity check prevents a high lexical score from transferring Pump A's limit to an unsupported device. The evidence expression also requires the source to state the expected relation, not merely contain a number. A more flexible system needs a richer entity and relation check, including scope, units and applicable dates. Expand those capabilities with labeled tests rather than weakening the check until every question receives an answer.

The answerer searches only the top passage. It can therefore abstain when the correct evidence appears lower in the ranking. That limitation is intentional and visible. A later version could inspect several candidates under a fixed budget, while recording which candidate supplied the final answer. Measure the effect on both supported answers and false acceptance.

The function stores an evidence slice and verifies the offsets. This catches serialization mistakes, but not every semantic mistake. A quoted sentence can still describe the wrong entity, an outdated policy or a hypothetical case. Exact quotation is an important mechanical check; the support relation remains a separate part of the contract.

## Relevance is not answerability

**Plain version.** A passage can discuss the topic and still lack the requested fact. A system should have a meaningful way to return insufficient evidence.

<Figure src={supportFig} alt="Three synthetic passages distinguish a topical mention, a limit for the wrong pump and complete evidence for Pump A" caption="Figure 2 — Topic overlap, wrong-entity evidence and complete support require different decisions. Scores alone do not define answerability." />

For a narrow question, define the required fields before evaluating a system. The temperature example needs device identity, a limit relation, a value and a unit. It may also need an effective date or operating condition. Missing any required field can make the available evidence insufficient even if the passage ranks first.

An abstention policy decides when to withhold an answer or ask for clarification. If confidence scores are used, select their threshold on development data with the intended costs of a wrong answer and unnecessary review. A raw similarity score is not a calibrated correctness probability. Its scale can change when the corpus, tokenizer or embedding model changes.

Report both coverage and quality among accepted answers:

$$
\mathrm{coverage}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{accepted}}}{N_{\mathrm{all}}},
\qquad
\mathrm{accepted\ accuracy}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{correct,\ accepted}}}{N_{\mathrm{accepted}}}.
$$

Here $N_{\mathrm{all}}$ is the number of evaluated questions; $N_{\mathrm{accepted}}$ is the count receiving an answer; and $N_{\mathrm{correct,\ accepted}}$ counts accepted answers judged correct under the task contract. Accepted accuracy is undefined when no answer is accepted, so report that condition rather than presenting zero acceptance as a successful accuracy result.

Coverage must include questions without available answers. Otherwise a system that always answers can look good on a collection designed to contain an answer everywhere. Include wrong entities, missing values, conflicting sources and unsupported time scopes. These cases test whether the model recognizes the limits of evidence instead of merely generating a likely response.

## Retrieval-augmented generation

**Plain version.** A generator can condition its answer on retrieved passages. Retrieval supplies external context, but the generator still needs to use it correctly.

The original retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, paper combines a learned retriever with conditional sequence generation for knowledge-intensive tasks [7]. One sequence-level form is:

$$
p(y\mid x)\approx\sum_{z\in Z_k(x)}
p_\eta(z\mid x)\,p_\theta(y\mid x,z).
$$

Here $x$ is the input question; $y$ is the output sequence; $z$ is a retrieved passage; $Z_k(x)$ is the selected top-$k$ candidate set; $\eta$ parameterizes the retriever; and $\theta$ parameterizes the generator. The approximation reflects restricting retrieval to candidates. This is a model-specific probabilistic formulation, not a definition that every contemporary RAG application implements.

Many practical systems retrieve passages, assemble a prompt and run a separately trained generator. That pipeline can be useful without optimizing the original marginal objective. State which design you use. Otherwise the name RAG can hide whether retrieval is learned, whether candidates are reranked and whether the generator is trained to use the selected sources.

Grounding means relating claims to external evidence under a declared support rule. A generated answer with citations may be traceable, but traceability is weaker than support. A valid URL can point to a document that contradicts the answer. Evaluate whether each consequential claim follows from its cited evidence, and whether the answer includes necessary qualifications.

Evidence may also conflict. A generator should not silently combine mutually inconsistent policies into a single confident answer. An application can prioritize an authoritative version, display a conflict or request clarification according to its contract. The decision should come from metadata and task rules, rather than which sentence happens to be most fluent.

## Boundaries around retrieved content

**Plain version.** Retrieved text is evidence to inspect, not an instruction source with permission to change the application.

A passage can contain quoted commands, misleading text or an adversarial instruction. Prompt injection describes attempts to make a model follow instructions that conflict with the intended application boundary; OWASP documents this risk category [8]. For retrieval systems, the important boundary is between user/application instructions and untrusted source material.

Keep source text delimited and labeled with document identity. Do not allow it to redefine the current user's permissions, request secret data or authorize a tool action. If tools are available, validate actions against the user's task and the application policy outside the retrieved passage. A textual delimiter helps structure input, but is not a complete enforcement mechanism.

The same principle applies to logs and caches. A cached result produced under one user's access policy must not be reused for another user without checking permissions. Evidence spans must refer to the same document version used for the answer. When a document is removed or replaced, refresh the index and consider invalidating derived answers.

These checks also support ordinary correctness. A passage that says “Ignore the old limit; the new policy is below” can be legitimate document content that needs interpretation, while “Ignore your application instructions” is not a new authority for the system. Store both as source material, then apply the task's evidence and permission rules. Do not make the retriever a route for changing the governing contract.

## Diagnose failures with controlled substitutions

**Plain version.** Replace one imperfect stage with a known-correct input to discover where the final answer fails. Keep these diagnostic results separate from end-to-end deployment scores.

First provide the answerer with the annotated supporting passage directly. If it still produces the wrong value, retrieval is not the immediate cause. Inspect entity matching, unit handling, scope and realization. If it succeeds with gold evidence but fails with retrieved evidence, inspect candidate recall and the selected passage order. This comparison localizes a bottleneck without pretending that perfect evidence will be available at deployment.

Next preserve the correct passage but add irrelevant or conflicting passages. A system that fails only after extra context arrives has a context-selection or evidence-integration problem. Vary one property at a time: a different device identifier, an obsolete version, a quoted allegation or an unsupported command. Label the intended behavior before running the experiment so the interpretation does not shift to excuse the output.

Also test the evidence serializer independently. A correct answer paired with offsets into a different document version is a traceability failure. An exact quotation paired with an incorrect value is a support failure. A correct value copied from an unauthorized document is an access failure. A single answer-accuracy score cannot explain these distinctions, even when the visible response looks identical.

Finally retain realistic query distributions. A diagnostic suite deliberately overrepresents difficult cases, while a representative held-out set estimates ordinary behavior. Report both with their purposes stated. Do not mix the two into one aggregate number and claim it estimates production traffic. Controlled examples teach why the system fails; representative examples estimate how often the failure matters under the chosen deployment conditions.

## Modern view

**Plain version.** Current retrieval-augmented research studies retrieval, context construction and generation together, while increasingly evaluating their failures separately.

Gao and colleagues' survey organizes RAG into naive, advanced and modular paradigms, and examines retrieval, augmentation and generation [6]. It also separates context relevance, answer relevance and answer faithfulness. This taxonomy helps locate a change: query reformulation changes the retrieval input; reranking changes candidate order; context compression changes what the generator receives. Each operation can improve one case while removing a critical qualification in another.

The original DPR and RAG papers provide concrete designs behind the broad terminology [5, 7]. Zhao and colleagues' survey, updated through 2026, places retrieval-assisted use within a wider account of large-model utilization and capability evaluation [9]. These sources support a system-level view; they do not establish that adding retrieval always increases accuracy for every corpus and question family.

Open questions include robust retrieval across languages and domains, reliable abstention, version-aware answers, contradictory evidence, privacy-preserving access and support evaluation for multi-passage reasoning. Long context changes the cost tradeoff, but a larger prompt does not automatically resolve relevance or permissions. A system still needs to identify which evidence applies to the user's question.

An effective study sequence is to build a lexical baseline, label query relevance and answerability, inspect failures, and then introduce one additional component at a time. Compare retrieval before and after the change, supported-answer quality, abstention and resource cost. Keep final test queries separate from prompt and threshold selection. This produces an application whose improvements can be attributed to measured behavior rather than to a more fashionable component name.

## Key takeaways

- Searchable passages need document identity, version, offsets and access policy.
- Lexical and dense retrieval offer different evidence and failure modes.
- Candidate recall limits what a later reranker or answerer can recover.
- Ranking scores are not correctness probabilities.
- Relevance, answerability, citation validity and claim support are separate conditions.
- Report coverage alongside accepted-answer quality, including unanswerable cases.
- Retrieved content cannot authorize actions or redefine application permissions.
