## What you'll learn

- Separate lexical meaning, predicate arguments, sentence scope and real-world truth.
- Define an entity schema with reproducible character offsets and annotation rules.
- Understand sequence labels, span predictions and overlapping mentions.
- Evaluate exact entity matches instead of relying on token accuracy.
- Extract relations and events while preserving attribution, negation and evidence.
- Compare specialized extractors with current generative extraction approaches.

## The big picture

Syntax describes how words connect. Semantics asks what those connections express. Information extraction turns selected parts of that meaning into records that an application can inspect. These are related activities, but the output contract matters: a customer-support system might need a product name and a complaint event, whereas a historical archive needs people, organizations, dates and attributed statements.

McRoy's Chapter 5 develops semantic representations and interpretation; Chapter 8 connects language processing with information extraction and argumentation [1]. Hagiwara's Chapter 5 develops named entity recognition as sequence labeling, and Chapter 9 discusses pretrained models that can support inference tasks [2]. We follow the linguistic questions first, then design a narrow executable extraction contract. A model architecture cannot fix an unclear definition of the desired record.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Imagine turning a story into a set of labeled cards. One card says who appeared, another says what happened, and a third shows the exact line that supports it. “Mina did not buy the robot” must produce different cards from “Mina bought the robot.” A tidy card is useful only when it preserves what the story actually says.
</Callout>

The practical risk is losing distinctions during conversion. “The supplier said the device passed” does not establish that the device passed. “The device may fail” does not describe a completed failure. A system can identify the correct names and still build a false record by dropping attribution or modality. We therefore evaluate both surface extraction and interpretation, rather than treating well-formed JSON as evidence of correctness.

## Meaning at several levels

**Plain version.** A word's meaning depends on its use, and sentence meaning depends on how those uses combine. A representation should expose the distinctions needed by the application.

Lexical semantics concerns meanings of words and expressions. “Bank” can refer to a financial institution or the edge of a river; a contextual representation may distinguish these uses without providing a human-readable dictionary sense. Word-sense annotation requires a sense inventory and examples, not just a collection of embeddings. If two annotators choose different inventories, their labels may disagree even when their reading of the sentence is similar.

Compositional semantics describes how smaller meanings combine. In “Mina repaired the sensor,” the predicate connects a repairer with a repaired object. “The sensor repaired Mina” contains the same content words but exchanges their roles. A bag of words hides this distinction, and a sentence similarity score can remain high despite the reversal. The useful question is whether a representation preserves the distinctions required for the decision, rather than whether it produces a visually pleasing cluster.

Pragmatic interpretation adds context about speakers, goals and shared knowledge. “Can you open the window?” can function as a request, although its surface form asks about ability. We defer sustained discourse and dialogue context to [NLP 07](/en/learn/nlp/nlp-07-discourse-dialogue/). Here, make the permitted context explicit: a sentence, its enclosing paragraph, the whole document or a conversation prefix. Different context budgets define different tasks.

A small semantic record for an asserted event can be written as:

$$
e=(p,\mathbf a,t,m,s).
$$

Here $e$ is an event record; $p$ is its predicate or event type; $\mathbf a$ is a mapping from argument roles to participants; $t$ is a time value or an unknown marker; $m$ records polarity and modality; and $s$ identifies the source evidence. The tuple is our application design, not a claim that every linguistic theory uses the same representation. An unknown value must remain distinct from a guessed value.

Consider “According to Mina, the sensor might fail tomorrow.” A useful record could identify a possible failure event, the sensor as the affected object, tomorrow as a relative time and Mina as the attributed source. Resolving tomorrow requires the document's creation date and time convention. Replacing it with a calendar date without that metadata silently introduces an assumption. Keeping the relative expression is often the more accurate output.

## Predicates, arguments and grammatical alternations

**Plain version.** The same event can be expressed with different grammar. Extract the roles in the event instead of assuming the first name is always the actor.

For a simple two-participant predicate, write:

$$
\operatorname{repair}(\text{Mina},\text{sensor}).
$$

Here $\operatorname{repair}$ names the relation; the first argument is the repairer; and the second argument is the repaired object. “Mina repaired the sensor” and “The sensor was repaired by Mina” can share this representation. Their surface subjects differ. The grammar from the previous chapter supplies clues, but a role interpretation must also account for the predicate and construction [1].

Not every verb permits every alternation, and not every argument is overt. “The sensor was repaired” leaves the repairer unspecified. A system that fills in the nearest previously mentioned person has made a contextual inference, not a direct extraction. That inference may be reasonable, but it needs a separate confidence policy and evidence contract. Preserve an explicit unknown role when the application does not authorize that inference.

Arguments and modifiers also differ. In “Mina repaired the sensor in Taipei on Tuesday,” the repairer and repaired object participate in the core event; location and time modify it. An event schema may include all four fields, but it should say whether each is required. Optional fields prevent an extractor from inventing details merely to satisfy an overly strict schema.

<Figure src={rolesFig} alt="Active and passive sentences point to the same repairer and repaired-object record" caption="Figure 1 — Surface subjects can change while event roles stay the same. The examples and record are synthetic." />

Semantic roles provide a bridge across constructions, not a guarantee of universal agreement. Different resources can label the same participant with predicate-specific or more general roles. Before combining training corpora, compare their definitions and examples. A mechanical label rename can conceal differences about instruments, causes, beneficiaries and implicit participants.

This distinction matters in application design. A maintenance log might need the actor who performed an intervention; an incident report might need the cause of a failure. Those fields are not interchangeable. A sentence can mention a technician, a manufacturer and a damaged component without identifying who caused the damage. The extraction interface should allow “not stated,” rather than forcing a name into every slot.

## Quantifiers, negation and scope

**Plain version.** Small words such as “every,” “some” and “not” can change a statement more than the names do. Their scope tells us which part of the meaning they modify.

Two readings of “Every technician checked a sensor” are:

$$
\forall x\,[T(x)\Rightarrow\exists y\,(S(y)\land C(x,y))]
$$

and:

$$
\exists y\,[S(y)\land\forall x\,(T(x)\Rightarrow C(x,y))].
$$

Here $x$ ranges over possible technicians; $y$ ranges over possible sensors; $T(x)$ means that $x$ is a technician; $S(y)$ means that $y$ is a sensor; and $C(x,y)$ means that $x$ checked $y$. The symbols $\forall$ and $\exists$ denote universal and existential quantification; $\Rightarrow$ means implication; and $\land$ means conjunction. The first formula permits a different sensor for each technician. The second requires one sensor checked by all technicians.

These formulas are deliberately small. A realistic interpretation also needs tense, the relevant domain of people and devices, and whether the statement is reported or asserted. Formalizing the sentence does not supply those missing assumptions. It makes them visible. For example, universal statements can hold vacuously when the relevant technician set is empty; an application that assumes at least one technician must state that additional premise.

Negation creates a related issue. “Mina did not inspect every sensor” does not normally mean “Mina inspected no sensors.” The first allows some inspections while denying completeness. Likewise, “No evidence of failure was found” describes an observation or search result. It should not automatically become “failure is impossible.” Losing these distinctions can make a document database appear more certain than its sources.

In a restricted extractor, unsupported scope constructions should trigger abstention or review. A narrow rule that correctly handles explicit “did not repair” can still misread “did not say that Mina repaired.” Adding every occurrence of “not” as a negated event is not enough: the system must determine what is negated. Test nested clauses and reporting verbs as separate error categories.

McRoy's semantics chapter provides a foundation for these distinctions [1]. We use explicit predicates to teach their consequences, rather than suggesting that a production system must convert every sentence into a complete logical theory. The required representation depends on the questions the system must answer and the cost of accepting an unsupported conclusion.

## Entity recognition starts with a schema

**Plain version.** An entity recognizer locates mentions and assigns types. It does not automatically identify the unique real-world object behind each mention.

Represent an entity mention as:

$$
r=(d,b,e,c).
$$

Here $r$ is a mention record; $d$ is the document identifier; $b$ is its inclusive starting character offset; $e$ is its exclusive ending offset; and $c$ is its category. The surface text is the original document slice from $b$ to $e$. These are Python string offsets in our example; another interface may use bytes or another indexing convention. Declare the convention because Unicode characters and encoded bytes do not have identical boundaries.

An annotation guideline must decide whether titles belong to a person's span, whether an organization suffix is included, and how to handle ambiguous uses. “Taipei” can name a place or stand for a government in context. A location-only schema and a context-sensitive organization schema can legitimately produce different annotations. Evaluation becomes meaningful only after the schema and guidelines are frozen.

Named entity recognition, or NER, commonly distinguishes people, organizations and locations; the CoNLL-2003 shared-task paper describes one established benchmark setting [3]. We mention that resource to identify the evaluation tradition, not to imply that its categories fit every application. Product identifiers, legal provisions or equipment parts may be more useful in another domain.

Entity linking is a separate decision: map a mention to a record in a knowledge base, or choose that no suitable record exists. Two mentions with the same spelling can refer to different organizations; two different spellings can refer to the same one. A recognizer that marks “Orion” as an organization has not solved this identity problem. Linking requires a candidate inventory, context and an explicit unknown-entity policy.

Keep the original text and offsets even when you store a normalized name. Lowercasing, accent removal and whitespace collapse can change strings and boundaries. One reliable design extracts from the original text and stores normalization as an additional field. If preprocessing is necessary, retain an alignment map so the predicted span can be projected back without guessing.

## Sequence labels and span prediction

**Plain version.** Entity mentions can be expressed as labels on tokens or as explicit spans. The representation determines which overlaps the model can describe.

In a BIO scheme, a token label begins with B for the beginning of a mention, I for its continuation or O for outside. An example is “Mina works at Orion Labs,” with person labels on Mina and organization labels on Orion and Labs. Hagiwara's sequence-labeling chapter and the NER survey give context for token-level extraction models [2, 4].

For an independent token classifier:

$$
p_\theta(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)=\prod_{i=1}^{T}p_\theta(y_i\mid\mathbf x).
$$

Here $\mathbf x$ is the input token sequence; $\mathbf y$ is the label sequence; $T$ is the number of tokens; $i$ indexes positions; and $\theta$ denotes learned parameters. Each prediction may use a contextual representation of the whole permitted input, although the output labels are independent under this factorization. Input context and output dependence are different ideas.

Independent decisions can produce invalid transitions, such as an I-PER label after O. A deterministic repair rule is possible, but its behavior becomes part of the evaluation contract. Alternatively, a structured decoder searches only valid paths while scoring the whole sequence. The dynamic-programming idea from [NLP 03](/en/learn/nlp/nlp-03-syntax-parsing/) applies when the score decomposes locally. More powerful encoders do not remove the need to define valid outputs.

A flat token-label sequence cannot represent arbitrary nested mentions. “Orion Labs Research Division” could contain an organization mention and a nested division mention under a particular schema. A span model instead scores candidate boundaries and types:

$$
s_\theta(b,e,c\mid\mathbf x).
$$

Here $s_\theta$ is a learned score; $b$ and $e$ are candidate span boundaries; $c$ is the candidate category; and $\mathbf x$ is the input. The score need not be a calibrated probability. A selection policy decides which candidates to keep, whether overlap is allowed and how conflicts are resolved.

The candidate count grows with sentence length, so practical designs may bound maximum span length or prune candidates. Every such restriction changes which mentions can be recovered. If very long names matter, measure their recall separately. A fast system can otherwise achieve a reasonable average score while never representing the outputs needed by a small but consequential group of users.

## Exact span evaluation

**Plain version.** Correctly labeling common background tokens can hide missed entities. Evaluate complete mentions, including their boundaries and types.

For sets of predicted records $P$ and gold records $G$:

$$
\mathrm{precision}=\frac{|P\cap G|}{|P|},\qquad
\mathrm{recall}=\frac{|P\cap G|}{|G|},\qquad
F_1=\frac{2|P\cap G|}{|P|+|G|}.
$$

Here $P$ and $G$ contain document identifier, start offset, end offset and category tuples; $|\cdot|$ denotes set size; and $P\cap G$ contains exact matches. Precision measures the share of predictions that match; recall measures the share of annotations recovered; and $F_1$ combines them. Our code reports zero when a displayed denominator is zero; another evaluation convention should be documented explicitly.

Exact matching is strict. Predicting “Orion” when the annotation says “Orion Labs” creates both a false positive and a false negative. That is appropriate when downstream code depends on a complete name. For another application, partial overlap may deserve a secondary diagnostic. Report its matching rule separately; do not rename a forgiving overlap score as the established exact-match measure [3].

<Figure src={spansFig} alt="Gold and predicted entity spans show how truncation and wrong types produce exact-match errors" caption="Figure 2 — A boundary error and a type error remain errors even when most words are labeled correctly. This is a synthetic diagnostic example." />

A single aggregate score still hides the mechanism. Break errors into missed mention, spurious mention, boundary mismatch, category mismatch and linking mismatch. Also report performance by category and document source. When a category is rare, retain its example count so the reader can assess uncertainty. A large weighted average can otherwise bury a category with nearly no successful predictions.

## A complete CPU extraction experiment

**Plain version.** A small deterministic extractor makes the contract easy to inspect. It is a teaching baseline with visible blind spots, not a general language-understanding system.

The following standalone example recognizes an invented name inventory, extracts one tightly constrained active repair construction and evaluates exact entity records. It downloads nothing. The inventory is fixed before evaluation, and the gold records are written explicitly. A lexicon is an allowed input here; if a real evaluation forbids external name inventories, the same code would violate that contract.

```python
import re

documents = {
    "a": "Mina repaired Sensor A in Taipei.",
    "b": "Orion Labs said Sensor B might fail.",
    "c": "Leo did not repair Sensor B.",
    "d": "The sensor was repaired by Mina.",
}
inventory = {
    "Mina": "PER", "Leo": "PER", "Orion Labs": "ORG",
    "Sensor A": "DEVICE", "Sensor B": "DEVICE", "Taipei": "LOC",
}
gold = {
    ("a", 0, 4, "PER"), ("a", 14, 22, "DEVICE"), ("a", 26, 32, "LOC"),
    ("b", 0, 10, "ORG"), ("b", 16, 24, "DEVICE"),
    ("c", 0, 3, "PER"), ("c", 19, 27, "DEVICE"), ("d", 27, 31, "PER"),
}
predicted = set()
for doc_id, text in documents.items():
    for name, category in inventory.items():
        pattern = r"(?<!\w)" + re.escape(name) + r"(?!\w)"
        for match in re.finditer(pattern, text):
            predicted.add((doc_id, match.start(), match.end(), category))
tp = len(predicted & gold)
precision = tp / len(predicted) if predicted else 0.0
recall = tp / len(gold) if gold else 0.0
f1 = 2 * tp / (len(predicted) + len(gold)) if predicted or gold else 0.0
assert predicted == gold
print("entity precision/recall/F1:", precision, recall, f1)

repair = re.compile(r"^(Mina|Leo) repaired (Sensor [AB])(?=[ .])")
events = []
for doc_id, text in documents.items():
    match = repair.search(text)
    if match:
        events.append({
            "document": doc_id, "predicate": "repair",
            "actor": match.group(1), "object": match.group(2),
            "polarity": "positive", "modality": "asserted",
            "evidence": [match.start(), match.end()],
        })
assert len(events) == 1 and events[0]["document"] == "a"
for event in events:
    start, end = event["evidence"]
    print(event, "source:", documents[event["document"]][start:end])
print("unsupported repair constructions:", ["c", "d"])
```

The exact entity score is 1.0 on these hand-built documents, because all mention spellings are in the inventory. That result does not establish generalization. The event rule returns only document a. It excludes the explicit negation in c but also misses the valid passive construction in d. These are different reasons for returning no event, and a production interface should distinguish rejected negative claims from unsupported syntax.

Document b contains recognized entities but no asserted repair event. It also contains an attributed, possible failure. This example illustrates why entity recognition and event extraction need separate evaluation. A system can obtain every name correctly while omitting the task's most relevant event. Expanding the event grammar requires additional positive and negative fixtures before accepting the new behavior.

The evidence interval covers only the matched clause, not the trailing location. That is adequate for the narrow repair record, which has no location field. If the record later includes location, expand the evidence contract and its tests. Treat schema changes as changes to the task, rather than adding a field that is populated opportunistically from unrelated nearby words.

## Relations, events and provenance

**Plain version.** A relation links entities; an event organizes participants around an occurrence. Both need evidence, and both can be wrong even when every entity span is right.

A binary relation record can be written:

$$
q=(r,h,t,s).
$$

Here $q$ is the record; $r$ is the relation type; $h$ is the head entity or mention; $t$ is the tail entity or mention; and $s$ identifies supporting evidence. The letters head and tail define an ordering convention, not a linguistic claim. For an employment relation, reversing the employee and employer changes the record. Define direction in the schema and evaluate it.

Event extraction adds a trigger or event type with multiple roles, such as buyer, seller, item, amount and time. A transaction sentence may mention several prices, only one of which is the actual amount paid. Jointly selecting role assignments can prevent some contradictions, but it cannot resolve an ambiguous source without additional context. Preserve alternatives or route ambiguity to review when the application permits it.

Provenance should include document identity, source version and supporting span. Without a version, offsets can point into a later edited document. Without attribution, a quoted allegation can become an asserted fact. Without access controls, a correct extraction can expose information from a document the current user is not permitted to read. These are properties of the record pipeline, rather than failures that a larger encoder automatically fixes.

Decompose evaluation by stage. Measure mention detection, category prediction, candidate relation selection and the final supported record. An end-to-end score captures the user's result, while stage scores explain which component needs work. Evaluate relations against predicted mentions as well as gold mentions: giving an extractor perfect input mentions during evaluation can conceal the failure rate of the deployed pipeline.

## Inference and argumentation

**Plain version.** A text can support a conclusion without proving that conclusion true in the world. Keep textual entailment and evidence quality separate.

Natural language inference classifies a premise–hypothesis pair as entailment, contradiction or neutral under the annotation convention. The SNLI paper describes a widely used annotated setting [5]. If the premise is “Mina repaired a sensor,” the hypothesis “Someone repaired something” follows under an ordinary interpretation. “Mina repaired every sensor” does not follow, and “Mina repaired no sensor” conflicts with the premise.

An inference model predicts an annotation relationship. It does not independently verify the premise. If the premise itself is fabricated, an entailed hypothesis can also be fabricated. A retrieval-assisted application therefore needs both source selection and an answer-support check. We develop that distinction in [NLP 06](/en/learn/nlp/nlp-06-retrieval-question-answering/).

Argumentation mining can identify a claim, a proposed supporting statement and an expressed attack relation [1]. “The device is reliable because it passed one test” contains a recognizable argument structure. Identifying that structure does not establish that the test is representative or that the conclusion is valid. The application may store the stated reason while marking its evidential strength as unassessed.

Useful challenge pairs change one crucial distinction at a time: actor reversal, negation, possibility versus assertion, allegation versus fact, or one versus all. These tests target the contract directly. Fluent paraphrases are useful additional variation, but they should preserve the intended labels. A synthetic paraphrase that changes scope or removes attribution must receive a new annotation rather than inherit the original record automatically.

## Modern view

**Plain version.** Current extraction systems combine pretrained representations with explicit task definitions. General language generation increases flexibility, while leaving span fidelity and support as separate problems.

Li and colleagues' NER survey organizes earlier deep systems by input representation, context encoder and tag decoder [4]. This taxonomy is still useful: changing the encoder does not determine whether the output is a flat sequence or overlapping spans. Xu and colleagues' 2024 generative extraction survey adds a task axis—entities, relations, events and unified extraction—and an adaptation axis including prompting, examples, supervised tuning and constrained generation [6]. Read these maps as design dimensions, rather than a ranking of universally superior architectures.

GLiNER is a 2024 original example of label-conditioned span extraction with a bidirectional Transformer [7]. Its design shows that adapting the requested entity categories need not require autoregressively generating a long list of records. A structured span output can retain direct alignment with input text. The relevant comparison for an application includes supported category definitions, nested-span behavior, latency and error review, alongside aggregate accuracy.

A generative extractor instead emits a textual serialization of the requested record. Enforcing a valid schema can prevent missing keys or malformed syntax, but valid serialization does not guarantee a correct name, relation or evidence interval. A practical validation layer checks that quoted evidence exists, offsets match, categories are allowed and entity identities are consistent. These checks eliminate some errors while leaving interpretation errors for evaluation or review.

Open problems include changing category inventories, specialized language, nested entities, cross-document identity and reliable uncertainty under domain shift. Multilingual extraction adds different name boundaries, writing systems and conventions for titles. Treat a language-specific annotation guideline as part of the task definition; translating category names is insufficient. Evaluate documents by source and language while keeping enough examples to make each comparison interpretable.

For a new project, establish a deterministic or compact supervised baseline, freeze an annotation guide and inspect its error types before choosing a larger model. Use a held-out document set that prevents repeated templates or near-duplicate reports crossing the split. Compare complete supported records, not only whether a model returns parseable output. The result is a measurable extraction service whose failures can be traced back to evidence.

## Key takeaways

- Semantic representations expose roles, scope and assumptions that word lists hide.
- Mention recognition and real-world entity linking are different tasks.
- Keep source text, explicit offset conventions and document versions.
- Exact span evaluation checks boundaries and types; token accuracy can hide missed entities.
- Preserve negation, modality and attribution when building event records.
- Valid structured output does not establish that its contents are supported.
- Evaluate complete pipelines and use stage diagnostics to explain their failures.
