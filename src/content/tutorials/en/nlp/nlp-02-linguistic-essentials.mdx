## The big picture

Language lets people describe situations, ask questions, coordinate action and change what others know. The visible sentence is only part of that activity. Its internal structure constrains interpretation; speakers also rely on shared objects, earlier conversation and expectations about why someone spoke.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Imagine someone hands you building blocks and says, “Build that one again.” Knowing the blocks' names does not tell you which building “that one” means, what happened last time, or whether the speaker wants the same colors. Language works similarly: words supply pieces, grammar connects them, and the situation supplies information the speaker leaves unsaid.
</Callout>

Consider “The technician inspected the panel with the camera.” Did the technician use a camera, or inspect a panel that had a camera attached? Knowing every dictionary entry does not settle the question. Consider “Can you inspect it tomorrow?” Now the object, reference date and intended request depend on context. A useful analysis identifies these alternatives before choosing a representation or label.

## 1. Language, modality and units

**Plain version.** Speech, signing and writing are different ways of expressing language, and each records different information. A computer's “token” is yet another unit, chosen for processing.

Language is a system of structured expression and interpretation. Speech and writing are ways to express it; they are not interchangeable containers. Signed languages use systematic visual-manual expression, and tactile signing provides another modality [4]. A written transcript preserves selected information from an interaction, while leaving out timing, gaze, gesture and much of its physical setting.

### Sounds and written symbols answer different questions

**Phonetics** studies the physical production and perception of speech sounds. **Phonology** studies their patterning within a language. A **phoneme** is a contrastive sound unit in a particular linguistic system; different physical realizations can belong to the same phoneme [5]. A **grapheme** is a unit of a writing system. These units need not map one to one.

English “ship” has four letters, yet its initial “sh” represents one consonant sound. Conversely, a letter can contribute differently in different words. Counting letters therefore cannot directly answer a question about sound structure. A speech application and a text application may need different annotations even when their users intend the same words.

Tone and intonation also differ [6]. Mandarin mā, má, mǎ and mà distinguish the ordinary written words 媽, 麻, 馬 and 罵. Their tone patterns contribute to lexical contrasts. The broader contour of an utterance can also convey questioning or emphasis. In English, “Really.” and “Really?” suggest different interpretations in writing, but punctuation only approximates how an actual speaker expresses disbelief, curiosity or confirmation. The written question mark does not recover the original voice.

### Characters, words and tokens

A **word form** is a particular realized form; a **lexeme** groups related forms as one vocabulary item. For a simplified analysis, “repair,” “repairs” and “repaired” are forms of the verb lexeme REPAIR. A **lemma** is a chosen citation form used to index that item. Lemmatization is thus an annotation decision, not a claim that the surface forms are interchangeable in a sentence [2, 3].

The distinctions matter in “The technician repairs cameras” versus “The technician repaired cameras.” Grouping both verbs under REPAIR helps search for the activity, but discarding the forms loses a present-versus-past contrast. An application can preserve both the original span and its lemma rather than deciding that one representation must replace the other.

A computational **token** is an input unit selected by a processing procedure. It might be a word, part of a word, punctuation or a character sequence. Its boundaries need not coincide with phonemes, morphemes or lexemes. Likewise, a Chinese character can participate in a multi-character word: the written sequence 相機 denotes a camera. A character-based system receives two units where a word-oriented analysis may use one. Neither count independently defines the number of meanings in the phrase.

## 2. Morphology: structure inside words

**Plain version.** Words have parts. Some parts carry vocabulary meaning, others carry grammar such as tense or number, and the way the parts are grouped can change the meaning.

Word structure can convey both vocabulary meaning and grammatical information. **Morphology** studies that structure; a **morpheme** is a minimal recurring pairing of form with meaning or grammatical function [7]. “Minimal” does not mean “one letter,” and a meaningful element need not stand alone. Morphological evidence such as a plural ending, tense marker or derivational prefix is useful, but not every language expresses these properties with the same pieces or word boundaries.

### Inflection, derivation and productive formation

An **inflectional** contrast changes a word's grammatical realization, as in repair/repaired. A **derivational** process can create a new vocabulary item, as in repair/repairable. A **compound** combines bases, as in camera mount [3, 7]. These are analytical distinctions: identifying an ending's spelling is not enough to decide its function.

Compare “repairs” in “She repairs the camera” and “The repairs took a day.” In the first sentence, it is a finite verb form; in the second, a plural noun. A rule that strips every final “s” loses both grammatical category and the reason for the ending. It would also mishandle irregular pairs such as mouse/mice. The task is to explain recurring form–function patterns, with exceptions represented explicitly.

**Productivity** means that a pattern can support new combinations, rather than merely describing a memorized list. If a workshop introduces a new “sensor cradle,” listeners can interpret the compound from familiar pieces and the setting. Yet composition does not specify every detail: the cradle might hold a sensor during assembly, transport or operation. Word formation can supply a general relation while context supplies its exact instance.

Chinese also has internal word structure and productive combinations. In 三台相機, 三 expresses the count, 台 is a classifier associated with the counted object, and 相機 names the object. English “three cameras” places plural marking on the noun in this example. The contrast identifies different grammatical choices; it does not establish that Chinese words lack structure or that English nouns always carry an explicit count.

### Stemming versus lemmatization

**Plain version.** A stemmer chops endings off by rule; a lemmatizer looks up the dictionary form, using the word's grammatical category. The stemmer is cheap and crude; the lemmatizer needs more knowledge and makes fewer blunders.

A **stemmer** maps word forms to a shared string, the **stem**, by rewriting suffixes. Porter's algorithm is the classic English example: it applies ordered groups of suffix rules, each guarded by a condition on the remaining stem, and was designed to improve information retrieval by grouping related terms [16]. The stem need not be a real word; it only has to be consistent. A **lemmatizer** returns the lemma, which requires a lexicon and usually the part of speech: “saw” is the lemma of a noun but a form of SEE as a verb.

The toy stripper below uses four ordered rules and a minimum-stem-length guard, in the spirit of (but far simpler than) Porter's algorithm. The lookup table stands in for a dictionary.

```python
# A toy suffix stripper (a few Porter-style rules) versus a lookup lemmatizer.
RULES = [("ies", "y"), ("ing", ""), ("ed", ""), ("s", "")]   # tried in order; first match wins

def stem(word):
    for suffix, replacement in RULES:
        if word.endswith(suffix) and len(word) - len(suffix) >= 3:
            return word[: -len(suffix)] + replacement
    return word

# A lemmatizer needs the part of speech; this tiny table stands in for a dictionary.
LEMMAS = {("repairs", "VERB"): "repair", ("repairs", "NOUN"): "repair",
          ("repaired", "VERB"): "repair", ("mice", "NOUN"): "mouse",
          ("was", "AUX"): "be", ("batteries", "NOUN"): "battery",
          ("news", "NOUN"): "news", ("this", "DET"): "this"}

def lemmatize(word, pos):
    return LEMMAS.get((word, pos), word)

for word, pos in [("repairs", "VERB"), ("repaired", "VERB"), ("batteries", "NOUN"),
                  ("mice", "NOUN"), ("was", "AUX"), ("news", "NOUN"), ("this", "DET")]:
    print(f"{word:10s} stem={stem(word):9s} lemma={lemmatize(word, pos)}")
```

The stripper handles the regular forms (`repairs`, `repaired`, `batteries` all reach a sensible stem) but returns `mice` and `was` unchanged and damages `news` → `new` and `this` → `thi`. The first two failures are irregular morphology; the last two are **over-stemming**, where an ending that only looks like a suffix is removed. The lemmatizer gets all seven right, but only because its table already contains them; an unseen word passes through unchanged. Neither tool decides the grammatical category: the caller supplied `VERB` or `NOUN`, which is the job of the tagger in [NLP 06](/en/learn/nlp/sequence-labeling/).

Which one to use depends on the task. For search, merging `repairs` and `repaired` can raise recall even if a few unrelated words collide. For a status extractor, the tense the stemmer throws away may be exactly the answer. Modern neural pipelines rarely stem their input; they keep the original form and let subword tokens and context carry the morphology, which is one reason the next section matters.

### The same pieces can combine in different orders

An invented description “an unsealable case” permits two useful structural analyses:

- `un + (seal + able)`: a case that cannot be sealed.
- `(un + seal) + able`: a case that can be unsealed.

The first treats “un” as negating an adjective; the second treats it as reversing the sealing action before “able” forms an adjective. The readings have opposite practical implications. Their availability and naturalness depend on context, so the example demonstrates possible analyses rather than claiming that every speaker assigns both equally often.

A bag of these pieces is identical in both analyses. What changes is the intermediate grouping and the kind of object each operation combines with. The same problem will reappear in sentence attachment and logical scope: a representation must retain structure if the distinction matters to the task.

### Chinese segmentation is an interpretation problem

Written Chinese does not mark word boundaries with spaces. The original string 學生會說話 supports “學生 / 會 / 說話” (“students can speak”) and “學生會 / 說話” (“the student council speaks”). The first reading treats 會 as expressing ability. The second treats 學生會 as a compound noun. Spaces inserted after processing therefore record an analysis; the unspaced string does not supply them directly.

The following program enumerates paths through a small, explicit lexicon. It is not a general Chinese segmenter. It shows why choosing the longest matching entry cannot establish the speaker's intended reading.

```python
from functools import lru_cache

text = "學生會說話"
lexicon = ("學生", "學生會", "會", "說話")

@lru_cache(None)
def segment(start):
    if start == len(text):
        return ((),)
    paths = []
    for word in lexicon:
        if text.startswith(word, start):
            for rest in segment(start + len(word)):
                paths.append((word,) + rest)
    return tuple(paths)

for path in segment(0):
    print(" / ".join(path))
assert len(segment(0)) == 2
```

The two printed paths are the readings above. Every recursive step consumes a known word, and the base case accepts only complete coverage. Enlarging the lexicon may add alternatives; it does not add evidence about a particular conversation. A syntax analysis, the surrounding sentence or knowledge that the message concerns a council meeting could favor one path.

Even the gold standard is a choice. The Second International Chinese Word Segmentation Bakeoff evaluated systems on four corpora, two in Traditional characters (from Academia Sinica in Taiwan and City University of Hong Kong) and two in Simplified characters (from Peking University and Microsoft Research), and each provider segmented its data under its own standard [27]. A system can therefore be “wrong” on one corpus and “right” on another for the same split. Report which segmentation standard your labels follow.

## 3. Tokens and subwords

**Plain version.** A model needs a fixed list of units. Whole words leave too many unknown words; single characters make sequences long. Subword methods learn a middle ground from data: frequent strings become single tokens, rare ones are spelled out from smaller pieces.

[NLP 01](/en/learn/nlp/introduction/) defined the unknown-token rate and showed why mapping every unseen word to one marker loses distinctions. A character vocabulary reduces whole-word unknowns if the necessary characters are covered, at the cost of longer sequences. A byte-based encoding can cover arbitrary input bytes with a complete base byte alphabet, but individual units then need not correspond to readable characters. Subword methods trade vocabulary size against sequence length; Mielke and colleagues survey these choices [22]. There is no universal segmentation that is best for every language, task and resource budget.

A learned tokenizer typically fits statistical patterns in a corpus. These patterns can help reuse frequent pieces, but a frequent piece is not necessarily a meaningful morpheme. Conversely, a meaningful inflection can be split across several pieces. This distinction matters when asking what the model can recognize: tokenization supplies an encoding; later computation must learn how the encoded parts combine.

### Work through one subword method: BPE

BPE learns which adjacent pieces are worth joining. It gives frequent combinations their own symbols while leaving other strings as sequences of smaller pieces. Sennrich, Haddow and Birch adapted byte-pair encoding for rare-word translation [17]. The character-based teaching version below works inside words:

1. Represent each training word as characters plus an end marker.
2. Count adjacent symbol pairs, weighted by the word's corpus frequency.
3. Merge the most frequent pair everywhere it appears in those word representations.
4. Save that merge and repeat for a fixed number of steps.

A merge changes subsequent pair counts. After combining two symbols, the new symbol can participate in another pair. The algorithm therefore learns an ordered sequence of operations, rather than a set of pieces that can be applied in any order.

```python
from collections import Counter

corpus = ("low low low low low lower lower newest newest newest newest newest "
          "newest widest widest widest").split()
# Start from characters; "_" marks the end of a word so merges respect boundaries.
words = Counter(tuple(w) + ("_",) for w in corpus)

def pair_counts(words):
    pairs = Counter()
    for symbols, freq in words.items():
        for a, b in zip(symbols, symbols[1:]):
            pairs[a, b] += freq
    return pairs

def merge(words, pair):
    out = Counter()
    for symbols, freq in words.items():
        s, i = [], 0
        while i < len(symbols):
            if i + 1 < len(symbols) and (symbols[i], symbols[i + 1]) == pair:
                s.append(symbols[i] + symbols[i + 1]); i += 2
            else:
                s.append(symbols[i]); i += 1
        out[tuple(s)] += freq
    return out

merges = []
for step in range(10):
    pairs = pair_counts(words)
    best = max(pairs, key=lambda p: (pairs[p], p))   # most frequent; ties broken by symbol order
    merges.append(best)
    words = merge(words, best)
print("merges:", [a + b for a, b in merges])

def tokenize(word):                   # apply the learned merges in order
    symbols = list(word) + ["_"]
    for pair in merges:
        symbols = list(merge(Counter({tuple(symbols): 1}), pair))[0]
    return symbols

for w in ["lowest", "newer", "wider"]:
    print(w, "->", tokenize(w))
```

The output includes `lowest -> ('low', 'est_')`, `newer -> ('ne', 'w', 'e', 'r', '_')` and `wider -> ('wi', 'd', 'e', 'r', '_')`. The first word was not in the training corpus, yet its letters can be recombined using learned pieces. The longer encodings of the other words reflect which combinations acquired merges.

### Why the merge sequence matters

The first merge is `t_`, followed by `st_` and `est_`. Repeated endings in the training words make these combinations frequent. This resembles an English suffix in the example, but the algorithm has no instruction to discover suffixes: frequency drives its decisions. Another corpus can produce a different split of the same spelling.

The merge `low` occurs only after smaller combinations have been formed. Encoding follows the saved order so it reproduces the fitted procedure. A longest-piece heuristic over the final symbols is a different algorithm and need not produce the same segmentation. The tie-breaking rule also matters: this code resolves equal counts through symbol order, making the run reproducible.

The function returns pieces, not numerical IDs. It does not prove coverage for arbitrary unseen characters. The example's successful novel words reuse characters present during training; a word containing a new character would require a declared fallback and a compatible vocabulary lookup. This separates two questions: can a string be segmented, and does every returned piece have an ID that the model understands?

### WordPiece, the unigram model and SentencePiece

BPE is one member of a family. The others differ in how they choose pieces and how they split a new word.

- **WordPiece** originated in Japanese and Korean voice search [18]. Google's neural translation system describes its version as choosing a fixed number of pieces so that the segmented training corpus uses as few pieces as possible, with a boundary marker added at the start of each word so that the original word sequence can be recovered without ambiguity [19]. BERT uses a WordPiece vocabulary [30]; [NLP 10](/en/learn/nlp/pretrained-bert/) builds on it.
- **Unigram language model** segmentation works top-down: start from a large candidate inventory, model a word as a sequence of independently drawn pieces, and prune the pieces that contribute least to the corpus likelihood [20]. Because it is probabilistic, one word can have several scored segmentations; Kudo used this to sample alternative segmentations during training as a regularizer [20].
- **SentencePiece** is a toolkit rather than a new criterion: it trains BPE or unigram models directly from raw sentences and treats the space as an ordinary symbol, so no language-specific pre-tokenizer is required and decoding reverses encoding [21]. That matters for languages such as Chinese and Japanese that do not separate words with spaces.

In all three, the learned pieces are statistical, not morphological. A subword boundary that happens to fall at `est` is evidence about corpus frequency; it is not an analysis that `-est` is a superlative suffix.

### Use a complete tokenizer, not just a merge list

Tokenization software also specifies normalization, pre-tokenization, special markers and decoding [23]. A checkpoint's token IDs refer to its particular vocabulary and conventions. Matching only the tokenizer family name is insufficient: two vocabularies can assign different symbols to the same ID.

For a pretrained model, use the associated tokenizer and configuration. For a tokenizer trained in your experiment, fit it on the permitted training text and freeze its vocabulary and merge rules before evaluation. Inspect examples as pieces, IDs and reconstructed text; each view catches a different failure.

## 4. Parts of speech

**Plain version.** A word's category describes how it behaves in a sentence. Its spelling can suggest a category, but context often decides which use is intended.

Part of speech describes a grammatical category, such as noun or verb. A noun commonly heads a noun phrase; a verb commonly heads a clause's predicate. These are distributional descriptions rather than rules that every noun must name a physical object. “Decision” and “arrival” are nouns although they refer to abstract content and events [2].

The same surface word can have several categories. In the invented pair “the pilots can land” and “the metal can fell,” the word “can” has different roles. The second sentence also demonstrates why a familiar local phrase can be misleading when the remainder of the sentence changes. A tagger must consider the permitted context and its annotation scheme.

### The Universal Dependencies tag set

Tag inventories differ between projects, which makes cross-language comparison hard. **Universal Dependencies** (UD) is a framework for consistent cross-linguistic annotation of parts of speech, morphological features and dependency relations [24]. Its universal part-of-speech (UPOS) inventory has 17 tags [25]:

| Group | Tags |
| --- | --- |
| Open class | `ADJ` adjective, `ADV` adverb, `INTJ` interjection, `NOUN` noun, `PROPN` proper noun, `VERB` verb |
| Closed class | `ADP` adposition, `AUX` auxiliary, `CCONJ` coordinating conjunction, `DET` determiner, `NUM` numeral, `PART` particle, `PRON` pronoun, `SCONJ` subordinating conjunction |
| Other | `PUNCT` punctuation, `SYM` symbol, `X` other |

UD separates the coarse tag from **features** such as `Number=Plur` or `Tense=Past`, so “repairs” as a noun can be `NOUN` with `Number=Plur` while “repaired” is `VERB` with `Tense=Past`. Each language's treebanks add documented conventions for constructions the universal guidelines leave open. The tag set is a shared annotation scheme, not a claim that every language draws category boundaries in the same place.

Represent a tagged sentence as:

$$
\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_T),
\qquad
\mathbf y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T),
\qquad
y_t\in\mathcal Y.
$$

Here $\mathbf x$ is the input word sequence, $x_t$ is the word at position $t$, $T$ is sequence length, $\mathbf y$ is the tag sequence, $y_t$ is the tag for word $x_t$ and $\mathcal Y$ is the finite tag inventory, for example the 17 UPOS tags. The symbols $t$ and $T$ refer to linguistic word positions, not automatically to model subword positions.

Annotation needs additional rules: contractions, punctuation, multiword expressions and words with several plausible analyses. When a model uses subwords, define how its outputs return to words. Using only the first subword's representation is one possible convention; pooling all subwords is another. Whatever the choice, gold labels and decoded output must use the same units. Predicting $\mathbf y$ from $\mathbf x$, with hidden Markov models, Viterbi decoding and recurrent taggers, is the subject of [NLP 06](/en/learn/nlp/sequence-labeling/).

## 5. Syntax: order, grouping and grammatical relations

**Plain version.** Grammar organizes words into groups and links them by relations such as subject and object. The same words in a different structure can describe a different event.

Grammar organizes combinations. Word order, phrase structure and relations such as subject and object constrain who did what to whom [2, 3]. These constraints are richer than a list of adjacent words.

### Why word counts lose role information

“Ada praised Bo” and “Bo praised Ada” contain the same words. Their ordinary active-clause readings reverse the person giving praise and the person receiving it. The Chinese pair 阿達稱讚小博 / 小博稱讚阿達 makes the same contrast in these sentences. A representation that only counts words maps each pair to the same object.

```python
from collections import Counter

first = "Ada praised Bo".split()
second = "Bo praised Ada".split()

def roles(tokens):
    # Only our deliberately fixed three-word active-clause template.
    assert len(tokens) == 3 and tokens[1] == "praised"
    return {"praiser": tokens[0], "praised_person": tokens[2]}

print(Counter(first) == Counter(second))
print(roles(first))
print(roles(second))
assert roles(first) != roles(second)
```

The count comparison prints `True`; the role dictionaries differ. This is an information loss result, not a trained-model benchmark. Once the two inputs have become identical counts, a later deterministic function of those counts alone cannot recover which original order occurred.

Syntactic roles also differ from semantic roles. In “Bo was praised by Ada,” Bo is the grammatical subject, while Ada still performs the praising. Equating the subject with the actor would fail even though the passive sentence describes the same event. An extraction task should state whether it wants grammatical dependencies, event participants or both.

### Constituency and dependency describe different aspects

A **constituent** is a unit that participates in larger structure. In “The label beside the boxes is missing,” the noun phrase “the label beside the boxes” functions as the subject. Its head is “label”; “beside the boxes” modifies that head. Replacing the whole phrase with “it” preserves a simple grammatical frame: “It is missing.” Such tests provide evidence for grouping, rather than proving that every adjacent substring is a phrase [8].

The agreement contrast “The labels beside the box are missing” shows why proximity is insufficient. The verb agrees with the subject's head in these examples, not the nearest noun “box.” A local heuristic could find the closest noun correctly and still select the wrong number.

A **dependency** representation instead records relations between words, such as a verb's subject and object or a noun's modifier [2]. UD uses dependency trees in which every word has exactly one head and a labeled relation to it, such as `nsubj` for a nominal subject [24]. Constituency highlights grouping; dependency highlights linked heads. They can describe related evidence without having identical structures or annotation conventions. The Penn Treebank, a large early English resource, annotated constituency trees [26]; UD annotates dependencies across many languages [24].

In the panel example, “with the camera” can modify the inspecting event or the panel. The simplified diagrams below retain this one attachment distinction and omit determiners and other relations. They are teaching diagrams, not complete parses under a named annotation standard.

Surrounding context can favor either: a technician explaining a recording method versus someone distinguishing equipped and unequipped panels. Grammatical possibility does not determine which situation actually occurred.

## 6. Semantics: meanings and truth conditions

**Plain version.** Semantics asks what an expression means and what the world would have to be like for a sentence to be true. Word meanings matter, but so does how the sentence combines them.

A sentence can be fluent while describing a situation incorrectly. **Semantics** asks how expressions contribute meaning and how a structured sentence determines conditions under which its assertion is true [2, 3]. Separating these conditions from conversational inference makes ambiguity easier to analyze.

### Lexical meaning is contextual and relational

**Polysemy** concerns related senses of an expression; **homonymy** concerns distinct meanings sharing a form [3]. A window can be an opening in a wall or a bounded area on a screen. The relationship helps explain the extension, but replacing “window” with one fixed concrete object would miss the screen reading. A dictionary can list senses; the occurrence still needs interpretation. [NLP 04](/en/learn/nlp/lexical-semantics-word-embeddings/) studies how vectors learned from contexts capture some of these relations.

Chinese 開 appears in 開門 (“open a door”) and 開會 (“hold a meeting”). Translating every occurrence with the same English verb would erase the construction-specific difference. Meaning belongs to the expression in its combination and context, not to a context-free lookup from character to English word.

Even apparently simple adjectives can rely on a comparison class. A small elephant may be larger than a large ant. The expressions compare each animal with an appropriate class, not with one universal size threshold. In a collection containing only toy elephants, the relevant comparison can change again. An annotation called “small” should therefore specify what is being compared.

### Composition depends on structure

**Compositionality** is the idea that the meaning of a complex expression depends on meanings of its parts and how they combine [3]. It does not say that word meanings can simply be added together. “Bo praised Ada” combines participants in a directed relation; a reordered sentence combines the same names differently. Attachment changes which expression a modifier restricts.

An idiomatic expression can also have a conventional meaning not predictable from isolated word entries. A lexicon and a grammar are complementary: the analysis must record both conventional combinations and productive composition. For an application, ask which distinction is needed. Searching for repair instructions may tolerate broad lexical grouping; deciding whether a repair was completed requires event, tense and negation information.

### Quantifier scope changes what is required

“Every technician uses a camera” has a reading in which different technicians may use different cameras. It can also be understood, in a suitable context, as involving one camera shared by all. These are different conditions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &: \forall t\in T,\ \exists c\in C:\ U(t,c),\\
B &: \exists c\in C,\ \forall t\in T:\ U(t,c).
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $T$ is the set of technicians, $C$ the set of cameras, $U(t,c)$ means technician $t$ uses camera $c$, $A$ and $B$ name the two readings, $\forall$ means “for every,” and $\exists$ means “there exists.” Both sets are nonempty in this example. The variable $c$ can depend on $t$ in $A$; in $B$ one $c$ must work for every $t$.

To check $A$, visit each technician and find at least one camera they use. To check $B$, choose a camera first and verify that all technicians use that particular camera. If $B$ holds, $A$ follows: use the shared camera for each person. The reverse implication fails when every person has a camera but no camera is shared by everyone.

```python
technicians = ("Ada", "Bo", "Cy")
cameras = ("A", "B")
worlds = {
    "distributed": {("Ada", "A"), ("Bo", "B"), ("Cy", "A")},
    "shared": {("Ada", "A"), ("Bo", "A"), ("Cy", "A")},
}
for name, uses in worlds.items():
    each_has_some = all(any((t, c) in uses for c in cameras)
                        for t in technicians)
    some_for_everyone = any(all((t, c) in uses for t in technicians)
                           for c in cameras)
    print(name, each_has_some, some_for_everyone)
assert not any(all((t, c) in worlds["distributed"] for t in technicians)
               for c in cameras)
```

The outputs are `distributed True False` and `shared True True`. Each row in Figure 3 represents one person's possibilities. The first reading requires a marked cell in every row; the second requires an entirely marked column. Scope is therefore a constraint difference, not merely a different paraphrase of the same requirement.

### Negation and scope must also be kept apart

“Not every technician approved” allows some approvals and at least one non-approval. “Every technician did not approve,” under a reading with negation inside the universal quantifier, says that nobody approved. Written English can itself leave scope unclear; explicit paraphrases remove the ambiguity.

The Chinese contrast 不是每位技師都批准 / 每位技師都不批准 makes the intended distinction easier to display. The former negates “all approved”; the latter assigns non-approval to each technician. For a nonempty technician set:

$$
\neg\forall t\in T\ P(t)\ \equiv\ \exists t\in T\ \neg P(t),
\qquad
\forall t\in T\ \neg P(t)\ \equiv\ \neg\exists t\in T\ P(t).
$$

Here $T$ is the technician set, $P(t)$ means $t$ approved, $\neg$ is negation, $\forall$ and $\exists$ are universal and existential quantification, and $\equiv$ means logical equivalence. The first condition needs one counterexample to universal approval; the second excludes every approval. If one technician approves and another does not, only the first condition is true.

[NLP 12](/en/learn/nlp/syntactic-semantic-tasks/) develops predicate–argument structure and semantic role labeling, and [NLP 11](/en/learn/nlp/classification-bert/) tests inference between sentences. Before designing a field such as `approved`, decide whose approval, what event and which quantified claim that field represents.

## 7. Pragmatics: interpretation in a situation

**Plain version.** What a speaker means can go beyond what the sentence literally says. Pragmatics studies how the situation fills that gap.

Speakers often communicate more than their sentence literally asserts. **Pragmatics** studies language use in context, including reference, background assumptions and communicative actions [9, 10]. Context supplies evidence; it does not license arbitrary interpretations.

### Reference and the speaker's viewpoint

“I will check that here tomorrow” is structurally simple but under-specified outside its setting. “I” identifies the speaker, “that” selects a referent, “here” uses a spatial viewpoint, and “tomorrow” uses a temporal viewpoint. Such context-dependent expressions involve **deixis** [10]. A forwarded message should not silently replace the original speaker's time and place with the receiver's.

Reference also depends on discourse. “Mei put a camera beside a lamp. It was overheating.” Either object may be the referent of “it” without further evidence. Knowing that cameras and lamps can both become hot leaves the ambiguity intact. In “Mei turned off the lamp. It stopped flickering,” the described event supplies evidence favoring the lamp. Evidence may favor a candidate without turning a grammatical possibility into a universal rule.

A practical analysis records the expression, candidate referents and supporting context. An unresolved referent is a meaningful result when the input does not determine one. Coreference resolution across sentences is developed in [NLP 12](/en/learn/nlp/syntactic-semantic-tasks/), and tracking entities across dialogue turns in [NLP 08](/en/learn/nlp/seq2seq-transformers/).

### Assertion, presupposition and implicature

An **assertion** contributes the claim at issue. A **presupposition** presents a proposition as background rather than as the main update. A **conversational implicature** is an additional inference drawn from the utterance and its use, which can often be cancelled without contradicting the literal assertion [9, 10]. They should not collapse into one “meaning” label.

“Mei stopped checking the sensor” asserts a cessation and normally backgrounds that Mei checked it previously. “Mei did not stop checking the sensor” still typically takes earlier checking for granted. That persistence under negation helps diagnose the background contribution. These are ordinary interpretations, not a proof that the earlier event actually occurred. A listener can challenge it: “She never checked it in the first place.”

“Some panels passed” asserts that at least one panel passed in the relevant collection. In a conversation asking whether the whole collection passed, a listener may infer “not all.” But “Some panels passed; in fact, all of them did” can cancel that inference without denying the first assertion [9]. By contrast, “All panels passed; in fact, one failed” conflicts under the same panel collection, test and time.

This difference can be checked without a language model. Imagine three panels: one, two and three passing panels all satisfy the literal “some.” A “some but not all” interpretation retains only the first two possibilities. The inference narrows the possibilities because of a conversational expectation; that narrowing is not contributed by existential meaning alone. Whether it is justified depends on the question, speaker knowledge and manner of reporting.

### A grammatical question can perform a request

“Can you lower the gain?” has the form of an ability question. During a recording session, it can perform a request to change a setting; during an assessment, it can ask whether the trainee knows how [10]. A **speech act** is the communicative action performed through an utterance, such as requesting, promising, asserting or asking.

“The gain is too high” can likewise function as a report or an indirect request. The physical words do not themselves specify every interpersonal condition. Who controls the equipment? What was the preceding question? Is the speaker diagnosing the recording or coordinating an adjustment? These details support an interpretation of the action.

A useful annotation separates sentence form, asserted content and intended act. Otherwise, every question-shaped request may be labeled as information seeking, or every unfavorable description may be labeled as an instruction. The goal is a defensible reading of this interaction, including alternatives when the setting is missing.

## 8. Discourse: meaning across sentences and turns

**Plain version.** Sentences in a text or a conversation lean on each other. Later sentences refer back, contrast, explain or correct, and a reader keeps track of that shared state.

An utterance can introduce an entity, correct an earlier claim, provide a reason or contrast with an expectation. **Discourse** analysis considers these connections across sentences and conversational turns [2, 10]. Reading each sentence independently discards the shared state that makes later expressions interpretable.

“Mei told Lin that she would calibrate the camera” can refer to either person as the future calibrator. The Chinese 美玲告訴怡君，她會校正相機 permits a comparable ambiguity in this invented setting. A preceding statement “Lin had already volunteered” favors Lin; “Mei was the only available technician” favors Mei. Neither grammatical gender nor mention order alone settles both contexts.

Now compare “The sensor became hot. Its readings stayed stable” with “The sensor became hot, but its readings stayed stable.” The second explicitly marks a contrast with an expectation that heat would disrupt readings. The assertion about stability can be the same while the discourse contribution differs. Conversely, “The readings drifted because the sensor became hot” introduces a causal explanation, not merely two events in sequence.

Conversations also contain repairs. “Move it to Friday.” “The inspection?” “Yes, the inspection.” The middle turn does not advance the scheduling date; it resolves the object. Once resolved, later references can use the shared identification. A dialogue system that summarizes only the first and last words misses why the clarification changed the conversational state.

This suggests a practical representation with distinct records for introduced entities, asserted events, unresolved references and conversational questions. Such a representation can remain partial. A one-sentence input may leave the speaker unknown; an entire exchange may resolve the speaker but still leave the exact camera unspecified. Increasing context length only helps when relevant evidence is actually present.

## 9. Corpora, annotation and Zipf's law

**Plain version.** NLP learns from collections of real text, often with human labels added. How the text was collected and how consistently it was labeled limit what any model can learn from it, and every corpus has a long tail of rare words.

A **corpus** is a collection of text or transcribed speech gathered under stated decisions: which sources, which period, which speakers, which language varieties. An **annotated corpus** adds analyses such as segmentation, tags, trees, entity spans or sentiment labels, produced under written guidelines. Two widely used examples show the range. The Penn Treebank annotated English text with part-of-speech tags and constituency trees [26]. The Universal Dependencies project releases treebanks for many languages under one cross-lingual dependency scheme [24]. The segmentation bakeoff corpora above show that even word boundaries come with a standard attached [27].

### Annotation guidelines and agreement

A label is only as clear as the guideline behind it. Two careful annotators can disagree because a sentence is genuinely ambiguous, because the guideline is silent, or because one of them made an error. Measuring agreement on a shared sample separates these cases before the labels are used for training.

Raw percent agreement overstates reliability when one label is very common, because annotators would agree often even by guessing. Cohen's kappa corrects for that [28]:

$$
\kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e},
\qquad
p_e=\sum_{k\in\mathcal Y}\frac{n_{A,k}}{n}\cdot\frac{n_{B,k}}{n}.
$$

Here $p_o$ is the observed fraction of items on which annotators $A$ and $B$ agree; $p_e$ is the agreement expected if each annotator chose labels independently at their own observed rates; $\mathcal Y$ is the label set; $n_{A,k}$ and $n_{B,k}$ count how often each annotator used label $k$; and $n$ is the number of items. $\kappa=1$ is perfect agreement and $\kappa=0$ is agreement at the chance level; the formula requires $p_e\lt1$.

```python
from collections import Counter
from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score

words = "Mei has to repair the camera mount before the inspection".split()
a = "PROPN VERB PART VERB DET NOUN NOUN ADP DET NOUN".split()   # annotator A
b = "PROPN AUX ADP VERB DET NOUN NOUN ADP DET NOUN".split()     # annotator B
n = len(words)
p_o = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n                     # observed agreement
ca, cb = Counter(a), Counter(b)
p_e = sum(ca[k] * cb[k] for k in ca) / n**2                     # agreement expected by chance
kappa = (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e)
print(f"p_o={p_o:.3f}  p_e={p_e:.3f}  kappa={kappa:.3f}")
assert abs(kappa - cohen_kappa_score(a, b)) < 1e-12
print("disagreements:", [(w, x, y) for w, x, y in zip(words, a, b) if x != y])
```

It prints `p_o=0.800  p_e=0.180  kappa=0.756`, and the two disagreements are `has` (`VERB` versus `AUX`) and `to` (`PART` versus `ADP`). Neither is a careless slip: “has to” sits on the border between a main verb and an auxiliary, and infinitival “to” resembles a preposition. The useful outcome is a guideline decision for that construction, not a higher number. Kappa summarizes reliability; the disagreement list says what to fix.

### Zipf's law: a few words are very common, most are rare

Count every word in a corpus and sort the types by frequency. A handful of function words such as “the” and “of” account for a large share of all tokens, while a huge number of types occur only once or twice. **Zipf's law** describes the relation between a word's frequency rank and its count as approximately a power law [29]:

$$
f(r)\approx \frac{C}{r^{s}},
\qquad
\log f(r)\approx \log C-s\log r.
$$

Here $r=1,2,\ldots$ is a type's rank when types are sorted by decreasing count, $f(r)$ is the count of the type at rank $r$, $C\gt0$ is a scale constant and $s\gt0$ is the exponent. With $s\approx1$, the product $r\cdot f(r)$ stays roughly constant. On log–log axes the relation is a straight line with slope $-s$. Piantadosi's review shows that the law holds approximately across many languages and corpora, that it deviates systematically at the extremes, and that its explanation is still debated [29].

We can check it on real English text that is already on disk: the docstrings of the Python standard library installed in the project environment (Python 3.9).

```python
import ast, re, sysconfig
from collections import Counter
from pathlib import Path

lib = Path(sysconfig.get_paths()["stdlib"])        # text already on disk: stdlib docstrings
docs = []
for path in sorted(lib.glob("*.py")):
    try:
        tree = ast.parse(path.read_text(encoding="utf-8"))
    except (SyntaxError, UnicodeDecodeError):
        continue
    docs += [ast.get_docstring(n) or "" for n in ast.walk(tree)
             if isinstance(n, (ast.Module, ast.FunctionDef, ast.AsyncFunctionDef, ast.ClassDef))]
tokens = [w for d in docs for w in re.findall(r"[a-z]+", d.lower())]
counts = Counter(tokens)
print(len(tokens), "tokens,", len(counts), "types")
ranked = counts.most_common()
for r in (1, 2, 10, 100, 1000):
    word, f = ranked[r - 1]
    print(f"rank {r:4d}  {word:10s} f={f:5d}  r*f={r * f}")
hapax = sum(f == 1 for f in counts.values())
print(f"types seen once: {hapax} ({hapax / len(counts):.0%})")
```

With Python 3.9.7 it finds 134,620 tokens and 6,281 types. The product $r\cdot f(r)$ is 8,313 at rank 1 (“the”), 13,640 at rank 10 (“be”), 20,100 at rank 100 (“called”) and 18,000 at rank 1,000: not constant, but within a factor of about 2.5 while the rank changes by a factor of 1,000. A least-squares fit over ranks 10 to 1,000 in the figure script gives $s\approx1.00$. A third of all types, 2,003 of them (32%), occur exactly once. A different Python version ships different docstrings, so the exact counts can change.

<Figure src={zipfFig} alt="Log-log plot of word count against frequency rank for Python standard-library docstrings, close to a straight line with slope about minus one" caption="Figure 4 — Rank–frequency plot of the Python 3.9 standard-library docstrings. The dashed line is a power-law fit over ranks 10–1,000; the head and the tail of single-occurrence words bend away from it." />

Three consequences follow for NLP. First, any fixed word vocabulary leaves out part of the tail, so new text keeps producing unseen words; this is why subword vocabularies exist. Second, count-based probability estimates are reliable for frequent words and unreliable for the rare majority, which is why smoothing appears in n-gram models ([NLP 05](/en/learn/nlp/recurrent-neural-networks/)). Third, accuracy on all tokens is dominated by frequent words; an evaluation that matters for rare names or technical terms must measure them separately.

## 10. Linguistic diversity and model assumptions

**Plain version.** Languages package information differently, and communities speak and write in different varieties. A model built around one language's habits can miss what another language says.

Languages and communities distribute information differently across morphology, order, prosody and context. **Typology** compares such structural patterns. **Dialect** concerns a community's variety; **register** concerns usage associated with a setting or activity [3, 11]. These distinctions describe variation rather than rank ways of speaking.

### Translation does not preserve every structure

Compare “I calibrated this camera yesterday” with 這台相機，我昨天校正過. The Chinese example begins with the camera as the topic and then comments on it. Treating the first noun phrase as the actor would misidentify the calibrator. An English translation can preserve the event while changing how the message organizes old and new information.

The short Chinese 已經校正好了 can be informative when the camera and responsible person are already established. A word-for-word translation that demands overt counterparts for every English argument may make it look incomplete. In context it can answer a pending question directly. This does not mean that any omitted participant is always recoverable; the analysis must examine the actual discourse.

Similarly, 三台相機 and “three cameras” package number differently. A feature called “plural suffix” cannot be required universally merely because it suits one language. Structural categories may be useful for comparison while needing language-specific realizations and annotation decisions. A language using fewer obligatory endings can still express detailed distinctions through constructions, words and context.

### Variety, setting and mixed-language expression

A formal maintenance report and a chat between technicians can describe the same equipment with different vocabulary, abbreviation and explicitness. The report “The shutter failed to actuate” and the informal “The shutter didn't fire” may describe a related event in context, yet the wording provides different evidence about setting and intended readership. Unfamiliar wording is not automatically an error.

A speaker might write 把 exposure 調低一點 in a bilingual discussion. The English item occupies a position in an otherwise Chinese construction. **Code-switching** involves alternation between linguistic varieties within an interaction [14]; analyzing this example requires both the lexical choice and the surrounding grammar. One occurrence alone may not settle whether a community treats the item as switching or as an established borrowing.

An analysis should preserve the observed expression before standardizing it. Replacing a regional or technical expression with a presumed standard can help a particular search task, but may erase a distinction needed for another task. A corpus records language from particular speakers, settings and collection decisions; frequent corpus patterns are evidence of usage, not a universal inventory of everything speakers can say.

## 11. A complete worked reading

Put the levels together with an invented message:

> 小林說：「那台相機又過熱了，你明天能幫我看看嗎？」
>
> Lin said: “That camera overheated again. Can you take a look for me tomorrow?”

The translation helps an English reader but already chooses wording for 看看 and punctuation for the quoted interaction. Analyze the original before treating the translation as equivalent evidence.

| Level | What the message contributes | What remains to be established |
|---|---|---|
| Written form and segmentation | The quotation groups Lin's utterance; 相機 is a camera expression | Speech intonation and gesture are absent |
| Morphology and construction | 看看 is a reduplicated form in this request, not a counted pair of inspections | Exact activity and expected effort depend on the setting |
| Syntax | 小林 introduces the reported speaker; 你 is the addressee inside the quotation | The reporter need not be the addressee |
| Semantics | The camera is described as overheating; a future assistance question is expressed | Which camera, and whether the description is factually correct |
| Background and reference | 又 normally invokes a prior occurrence; 那台 selects a contextually identified camera | Prior overheating and the demonstrative referent require evidence |
| Pragmatics and discourse | The question naturally supports a request in a maintenance conversation | Whether that reading fits this interaction and whether it is accepted |

The quotation changes the reference frame. “我” refers to Lin as the quoted speaker. “你” refers to Lin's addressee, who may differ from the current reader. “明天” is relative to the quoted utterance's day. If the report arrives later, its arrival date does not redefine the requested day.

The ordinary reading of 看看 presents the activity as taking a look, often with a light or tentative framing in this construction [13]. It does not establish a precise inspection duration or a numerical repetition count. Translating it as “inspect twice” would confuse visible repetition with event counting.

Finally, separate linguistic evidence from factual evidence. The message provides evidence that Lin reported overheating and requested help. It does not by itself verify overheating, identify a serial number or show that anyone agreed to help. A structured output might record the speaker and reported event while retaining unresolved referents and dates. This is a more informative analysis than assigning a single topic label such as “camera repair.”

## Modern view

Recent surveys show why language analysis remains necessary for large language models. Ma and colleagues' 2025 survey organizes pragmatic evaluation by phenomena including reference and deixis, implicature and presupposition, speech acts, discourse and social pragmatics [10]. It compares task design, collection and evaluation, and identifies gaps between available tests and contextual language use. Its useful lesson here is to evaluate the particular inference required, rather than treating a broad language score as proof of all pragmatic abilities.

Mielke and colleagues' tokenization review organizes the open-vocabulary problem across words, characters and intermediate units [22]. Its useful question is how segmentation balances coverage, sequence length and reuse. These quantities remain distinct: a tokenizer can represent every input string while giving a model little training evidence for the relevant combinations. Zipf's law explains why the problem never goes away: however large the vocabulary, the tail keeps supplying rare forms [29].

Qin and colleagues' 2025 survey separates multilingual alignment through parameter tuning from alignment with frozen parameters, including prompting and translation-based approaches [12]. It reviews cross-language training, evaluation and challenges. This distinction identifies where a system's behavior is being adapted, but multilingual output alone does not establish that it preserves a construction's scope, register or conversational force.

Winata and colleagues' 2023 systematic survey groups code-switching research by languages, tasks and research trends [15]. Read it alongside Doğruöz and colleagues' account of structural and social patterns [14]: testing on text assembled from two monolingual sources is a different task from interpreting how bilingual speakers actually switch during an interaction. The exposure example in Section 10 makes that distinction concrete: the surrounding construction and conversational setting contribute information beyond identifying the English item.

Linguistic analysis also continues to refine apparently simple constructions. Lu and Müller's 2026 study analyzes Mandarin verb reduplication and its interaction with verb classes and aspect [13]. For this tutorial, the relevant observation is that limited-event meaning and context-dependent pragmatic effects deserve separate analysis. Visible repetition is not a general instruction to count events twice.

A concrete evaluation can vary one property while holding others fixed. Reverse Ada and Bo to test role direction; change camera attachment while preserving words; compare “not all” with “none”; move a quoted message into a later reporting context. Write the expected interpretations and acceptable alternatives first. Then a failure reveals which distinction was lost. These examples are small diagnostic tests, not a claim that passing them establishes general language competence.

Open problems include recovering relevant context, distinguishing genuine ambiguity from avoidable annotation disagreement, evaluating naturally mixed-language conversation and measuring interpretation across communities rather than only translated prompts. Some inputs legitimately admit several readings. A system that states the alternatives and the missing evidence may be more faithful to the language than one that confidently chooses a single answer.

## Key takeaways

- Spoken, signed and written language expose different information; a transcript is a partial record.
- Computational tokens need not coincide with words, morphemes, lexemes or sound units; subword pieces from BPE, WordPiece or a unigram model reflect frequency, not morphology.
- Stemming strips endings by rule; lemmatization maps to a dictionary form and needs the part of speech. Both discard information some tasks need.
- Morphological structure and Chinese segmentation encode analytical choices with possible alternatives, and corpora follow different standards.
- Word counts discard order; part-of-speech categories, grouping and grammatical relations carry additional meaning. Constituency shows groups, dependency shows heads.
- Scope determines truth conditions: “each has some” need not mean “some shared by all.”
- Assertion, background assumptions, cancellable inferences, reference, speech acts and discourse relations require the speaker's situation and conversational history.
- Corpora come with collection choices and annotation guidelines; check agreement, and expect a Zipfian long tail of rare words.

## Paper guides

### BPE — Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units

#### Challenge

A fixed word vocabulary turns a rare compound or unfamiliar name into an unknown token. Sennrich, Haddow and Birch ask whether a translation model can instead compose rare words from a manageable inventory of subwords [17]. Their thesis is that changing the representation can improve rare-word translation without a separate dictionary backoff. Read the chapter's tokenization and vocabulary discussion first; this paper changes the units supplied to a model, not its attention architecture.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperBpe} alt="Four original BPE character-pair merge operations" caption="Source: Sennrich et al., ACL 2016, Figure 1, PDF p. 4. Read downward: previously merged symbols become candidates in later merges. The dot is the end-of-word marker; these are learned operations, not a linguistic parse. [17]" />

Start with characters and an end-of-word marker in each word. Count adjacent symbol pairs across the training dictionary, weighting each word by its corpus frequency. Merge the most frequent pair, recount, and repeat for the chosen merge budget. In compact notation,

$$
(a^*,b^*)=\arg\max_{(a,b)}\sum_{w\in V} f(w)\,n_{a,b}(w).
$$

Here $V$ is the training word vocabulary, $f(w)$ is word $w$'s frequency, and $n_{a,b}(w)$ counts adjacent occurrences of symbols $a,b$ in its **current** segmentation. The starred pair is the next merge. At inference, apply the saved operations in their learned order; do not recount pairs on the test set.

Figure 1 uses the dictionary “low, lowest, newer, wider.” Its learned pieces allow an unseen “lower” to become “low er·.” The nearby Algorithm 1 uses a different toy dictionary, so its counts should not be read as the provenance of this figure. Merges stay within pre-tokenized words. Joint BPE learns a shared merge inventory from both languages to encourage consistent segmentation, useful for names that must be copied or transliterated.

#### Results

Table 2 reports English→German newstest2015 results. The eight-model joint-BPE-90k ensemble scores 24.7 BLEU versus 24.2 for the word-level dictionary-backoff system. Rare-word unigram F1 rises from 36.8 to 41.8. However, out-of-vocabulary unigram F1 falls from 36.8 to 33.6. “Better rare-word handling” therefore does not mean that every previously unseen word improves. The table's single-model and ensemble columns are different comparisons; keep them separate.

#### Ablations

Tables 1–2 expose the vocabulary-size/sequence-length tradeoff and compare separate with joint segmentation. Smaller units cover more forms but lengthen sequences. Separate BPE-60k and joint BPE-90k also use different vocabulary budgets, so their difference cannot isolate sharing alone. A useful follow-up experiment would fix the training corpus, merge budget and translation model while changing only whether the merge inventory is shared.

#### Conclusion

The reusable idea is to make open-vocabulary composition part of the input representation. Before reading the translation score, trace one word through the saved merges and check that detokenization reconstructs it. Continue to the word-vector objectives in [NLP 04](/en/learn/nlp/lexical-semantics-word-embeddings/) to distinguish choosing tokens from learning their vectors.

#### Problems

Frequent pairs need not correspond to morphemes or meaningful words. Unseen characters remain a coverage issue in this character-based formulation; it is not automatically the byte-level tokenizer used by later systems. A longer subword sequence also consumes more model context. Inspect errors by compounds, names and transliteration rather than treating one aggregate translation metric as proof of linguistic correctness.
