## What you'll learn

- Why segmentation can be posed as moving a curve until an energy stops decreasing, and what that buys over thresholding and edge linking.
- How a **snake** (an explicit, parametric active contour) is defined by internal and external energies, and how the Euler–Lagrange equation turns that energy into an iterative algorithm.
- How to pick the external force: image gradients, edge maps, **gradient vector flow (GVF)** and the **balloon** force, and why each one exists.
- How the **level-set method** represents a curve implicitly as the zero crossing of a function $\phi$, and how to evolve it with $\phi_t + F\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert = 0$ using upwind finite differences under a CFL limit.
- How the speed function encodes edge-based (**geodesic active contours**) and region-based (**Chan–Vese**, from Mumford–Shah) models, plus signed-distance reinitialization, narrow bands and morphological approximations.
- Where active contours live today: inside deep networks as contour-refinement heads and as loss functions.

## The big picture

IP 10 segmented images with local rules: threshold this pixel, link that edge, grow this region. Those rules know nothing about the *shape* of what they produce. The result can be a boundary with gaps, a region that leaks through a weak edge, or a speckle of isolated pixels.

Active contours take a different view. We start with a closed curve somewhere near the object and let it **move**. The curve is pushed by the image (toward edges, or toward a good split into "inside" and "outside") and held together by its own stiffness (it resists stretching and bending). When the pushes and the stiffness balance, the curve stops, and its final position is the segmentation. The output is always a closed, smooth boundary, never a heap of edge fragments.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Put a big rubber band around a toy on the table. Now let the band shrink. It slides inward until it hits the toy, and then it hugs the toy's outline. The rubber wants to be short and smooth; the toy stops it from shrinking any further. A snake is that rubber band, and the "toy" is wherever the picture has a strong edge. A level set is a cleverer band that can also snap into two bands if it finds two toys.
</Callout>

Following the textbook [1], this chapter covers the two families in that order. **Snakes** (Kass, Witkin and Terzopoulos [2]) store the curve as a list of points and move the points directly. **Level sets** (Osher and Sethian [5]) store the curve implicitly as the zero contour of a surface and move the surface instead, which makes splitting and merging automatic. Both are *variational*: we write down an energy, then do gradient descent on it. That idea, more than any particular formula, is what survived into deep learning.

## Background: why curves that move

In plain words: instead of deciding pixel by pixel, we describe the whole boundary at once and ask, "how good is this boundary?" Then we improve it a little at a time.

More precisely, an active contour method has three ingredients:

1. A **representation** of a closed curve $C$ in the image domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$: either explicit (a parametrized curve $v(s)$) or implicit (the zero level set of a function $\phi(x, y)$).
2. An **energy** $E(C)$ that is low when $C$ is a good boundary. It always mixes a *regularity* term (short, smooth curves are preferred) with a *data* term (edges are strong along the curve, or the regions inside and outside are each homogeneous).
3. An **evolution rule** that changes $C$ to decrease $E$, usually gradient descent written as a partial differential equation (PDE) in an artificial time $t$.

Compared with IP 10, the regularity term bridges gaps in edges, the output is always a closed curve ready for the shape descriptors of IP 12, and prior knowledge (a user's click, a smoothness preference, an intensity model) enters naturally.

The price is that the energy is non-convex: gradient descent finds a *local* minimum, so the answer depends on where you start.

## Image segmentation using snakes

A snake is a closed chain of points that slides over the image. Each point feels two kinds of pull: its neighbours keep the chain short and smooth, and the image drags it toward edges.

### The snake as a parametric curve

We write the contour as

$$
v(s) = \big(x(s),\, y(s)\big), \qquad s \in [0, 1], \quad v(0) = v(1),
$$

where $s$ is a parameter that runs once around the curve and $x(s)$, $y(s)$ are image coordinates. Derivatives with respect to $s$ are written $v_s = dv/ds$ and $v_{ss} = d^2 v / ds^2$. In a computer the curve is sampled at $n$ points $v_i = (x_i, y_i)$, $i = 0, \dots, n-1$, with indices taken modulo $n$ for a closed snake.

### The energy functional

In plain words: the energy gives every curve a score. It adds up how stretched and how bent the curve is, plus an image term that is low where we want the curve to settle; the snake looks for the curve with the lowest score.

The snake energy of [2] is

$$
E_{\text{snake}}(v) = \int_0^1 \Big[\, \underbrace{\tfrac12\big(\alpha\,\lvert v_s(s)\rvert^2 + \beta\,\lvert v_{ss}(s)\rvert^2\big)}_{E_{\text{int}}} \;+\; E_{\text{ext}}\big(v(s)\big) \Big]\, ds .
$$

- $\alpha \ge 0$ is the **elasticity** (tension) weight. The term $\lvert v_s\rvert^2$ is large when the curve is stretched, so $\alpha$ makes the snake behave like an elastic string that wants to shrink.
- $\beta \ge 0$ is the **rigidity** (bending) weight. The term $\lvert v_{ss}\rvert^2$ measures how sharply the curve turns, so $\beta$ makes the snake behave like a thin metal rod that resists kinks.
- $E_{\text{ext}}(x, y)$ is the **external (image) energy**, a scalar field over the image that is *low* where we want the snake to settle.

A common choice of external energy for a gray-level image $f$ is

$$
E_{\text{ext}}(x, y) = -\,w_{\text{edge}}\,\big\lvert \nabla\big(G_\sigma * f\big)(x, y)\big\rvert^2 \;-\; w_{\text{line}}\,(G_\sigma * f)(x, y),
$$

where $G_\sigma$ is a Gaussian of standard deviation $\sigma$, $*$ is convolution, $\nabla$ is the gradient, and $w_{\text{edge}}$, $w_{\text{line}}$ are weights. With $w_{\text{edge}} > 0$ the snake is attracted to strong edges. With $w_{\text{line}} > 0$ it is attracted to dark lines, and with $w_{\text{line}} \lt 0$ to bright lines. The Gaussian blur widens the valleys of $E_{\text{ext}}$ so that the snake can feel an edge from a few pixels away; [2] calls this scale-space continuation.

### From energy to motion: the Euler–Lagrange equation

To minimize this energy we use the calculus of variations: a minimizing curve must satisfy the **Euler–Lagrange equation** of the integrand. For constant $\alpha$ and $\beta$ it reads

$$
\alpha\, v_{ss} - \beta\, v_{ssss} - \nabla E_{\text{ext}}(v) = 0 .
$$

Read it as a force balance. $\alpha v_{ss}$ is the tension force (it points toward the local centre of curvature and shortens the curve), $-\beta v_{ssss}$ is the bending force (it straightens kinks), and $F_{\text{ext}} = -\nabla E_{\text{ext}}$ is the image force. At equilibrium they cancel.

We rarely can solve this equation directly, so we make $v$ depend on an artificial time $t$ and let it slide downhill:

$$
\gamma\, \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = \alpha\, v_{ss} - \beta\, v_{ssss} + F_{\text{ext}}(v),
$$

where $\gamma > 0$ is a viscosity (damping) constant. When $\partial v/\partial t = 0$ we are back at the Euler–Lagrange equation.

### Discretization and the iterative solution

In plain words: once the curve is sampled at points, the smoothness forces become one fixed matrix, so each step of the snake costs a single matrix–vector product per coordinate.

Sample the curve at $n$ points with unit spacing. Central differences give

$$
v_{ss} \approx v_{i+1} - 2v_i + v_{i-1}, \qquad v_{ssss} \approx v_{i+2} - 4v_{i+1} + 6v_i - 4v_{i-1} + v_{i-2}.
$$

Stack the $x$ coordinates into a vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (and likewise $\mathbf{y}$). Then $\alpha v_{ss} - \beta v_{ssss} = -A\mathbf{x}$, where $A$ is a symmetric, circulant, pentadiagonal $n\times n$ matrix whose rows contain

$$
\big[\;\beta,\;\; -\alpha - 4\beta,\;\; 2\alpha + 6\beta,\;\; -\alpha - 4\beta,\;\; \beta\;\big]
$$

centred on the diagonal (wrapping around at the ends). The internal forces are linear in the point positions; the external force is not. The classic trick of [2] is a **semi-implicit** step: treat the internal part implicitly (at the new time $t$) and the external part explicitly (at the old time $t-1$):

$$
\gamma\,(\mathbf{x}^{t} - \mathbf{x}^{t-1}) = -A\,\mathbf{x}^{t} + \mathbf{f}_x\big(\mathbf{x}^{t-1}, \mathbf{y}^{t-1}\big)
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
\mathbf{x}^{t} = (A + \gamma I)^{-1}\big(\gamma\,\mathbf{x}^{t-1} + \mathbf{f}_x\big),
$$

and the same for $\mathbf{y}$ with $\mathbf{f}_y$. Here $\mathbf{f}_x$, $\mathbf{f}_y$ are the components of $F_{\text{ext}}$ sampled (by interpolation) at the current points, and $I$ is the identity. The matrix $(A + \gamma I)$ is fixed, so it is inverted or factorized once; each iteration is then a cheap matrix–vector product. The implicit treatment of the stiff fourth-order term is what lets the snake take reasonable steps without blowing up. The step size is $1/\gamma$: a larger $\gamma$ means smaller, safer steps.

```python
import numpy as np

def snake_step_matrix(n, alpha, beta, gamma):
    """(A + gamma*I)^-1 for a closed snake with n points (h = 1)."""
    row = np.zeros(n)
    row[[0, 1, 2, -2, -1]] = [2*alpha + 6*beta, -alpha - 4*beta, beta,
                              beta, -alpha - 4*beta]
    A = np.stack([np.roll(row, i) for i in range(n)])
    return np.linalg.inv(A + gamma * np.eye(n))

M = snake_step_matrix(100, alpha=0.05, beta=0.05, gamma=1.0)
# one iteration, given external forces fx, fy sampled at the points:
# x = M @ (gamma * x + fx);  y = M @ (gamma * y + fy)
```

scikit-image's `active_contour` builds the same internal-energy matrix and offers the edge and line energies above, but it is not an exact copy of the update above: it computes the image gradient itself and caps how far a point may move per iteration [12]:

```python
import numpy as np
from skimage import data

img = rgb2gray(data.astronaut())

                       alpha=0.015, beta=10, gamma=0.001)
print(snake.shape)  # (400, 2): the final (row, col) points
```

### Choosing the external force

The external force decides *what* the snake looks for. Common choices:

- **Gradient of an edge map.** Compute an edge map $e(x, y) \ge 0$ that is large on edges, for example $e = \lvert\nabla(G_\sigma * f)\rvert$ (or its square), and use $F_{\text{ext}} = \kappa\, \nabla e$ with a weight $\kappa$. The force points uphill on $e$, toward the ridge of the edge.
- **Line attraction.** Use $F_{\text{ext}} = \pm\nabla (G_\sigma * f)$ to follow dark or bright curvilinear structures (roads, vessels, text strokes).

All of them share the same weakness. The gradient of an edge map is essentially zero away from edges (Figure 11.2a). A snake that starts far from the boundary feels nothing but its own tension. And inside a narrow concavity the forces from the two walls point sideways and cancel, so nothing pulls the snake *down* into it. These two problems, **limited capture range** and **poor convergence into concavities**, motivated GVF.

### Gradient vector flow (GVF)

GVF keeps the good part of $\nabla e$ (accurate, pointing at edges where edges exist) and fills in the rest of the image by smooth diffusion. Xu and Prince [3] define the GVF field $\mathbf{w} = (u, v)$ as the smoothest vector field that stays close to $\nabla e$ wherever $\lvert\nabla e\rvert$ is large; a weight $\mu > 0$ balances the two and should grow with the noise level. The field is computed by iterating a diffusion equation: far from edges it simply diffuses outward from the edges, carrying their direction with it. With unit time step and the 5-point Laplacian, the explicit scheme in the `gvf` function below is stable for $\mu \le 1/4$ when $e$ is scaled to $[0, 1]$.

*Optional: [The GVF energy and its diffusion equations](#the-gvf-energy-and-its-diffusion-equations) (in the Supplementary notes). The functional of [3], what each term asks for, and the update equations the code iterates.*

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi

def gvf(f, mu=0.2, iters=500):
    """Gradient vector flow of an edge map f scaled to [0, 1]."""
    fy, fx = np.gradient(f)
    mag2 = fx**2 + fy**2
    u, v = fx.copy(), fy.copy()
    for _ in range(iters):  # explicit scheme, stable for mu <= 0.25
        u += mu * ndi.laplace(u, mode="nearest") - (u - fx) * mag2
        v += mu * ndi.laplace(v, mode="nearest") - (v - fy) * mag2
    return u, v
```

The snake then uses $F_{\text{ext}} = \kappa\,\mathbf{w}$ in place of $\kappa\,\nabla e$, with no other change to the algorithm.

Figure 11.2 reproduces the classic test of [3] on a synthetic U shape. Two things changed: the snake now feels the object from anywhere in the image (large capture range), and inside the notch the diffused vectors point down rather than sideways, so the snake is pulled into the concavity. One implementation detail matters: as the snake enters the notch its length grows, so the points must be **resampled** to a roughly constant spacing every few dozen iterations, or the chain becomes too sparse to follow the walls.

### The balloon force

Cohen [4] attacked the capture-range problem from another angle. If you initialize a small snake *inside* the object, tension makes it shrink to a point before any edge force can reach it. So add a pressure:

$$
F_{\text{ext}}(v) = \kappa\,\nabla e(v) \;+\; k_1\, \mathbf{n}(s),
$$

where $\mathbf{n}(s)$ is the unit outward normal of the curve at $v(s)$ and $k_1$ is the pressure. With $k_1 > 0$ the snake inflates like a balloon; with $k_1 \lt 0$ it deflates. The edge force must be able to beat the pressure at a real boundary ($\kappa\,\lvert\nabla e\rvert > \lvert k_1\rvert$ there), but not at the weak, noisy gradients inside the object. Cohen also suggests normalizing the edge force, $\nabla e/\lvert\nabla e\rvert$, so that one constant controls its strength everywhere.

The balloon force has a cost: you must know in advance whether to inflate or deflate, and too much pressure carries the snake straight through weak edges. GVF and balloon forces are complementary and are often combined.

### Limitations of snakes

- **Initialization.** The energy has many local minima. Even with GVF, a snake started across two objects or on the wrong side of a strong clutter edge will converge to the wrong answer.
- **Parameter tuning.** $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\kappa$, the blur $\sigma$, the pressure $k_1$ and the GVF $\mu$ interact. Values that work on one image often fail on the next.
- **Point management.** The points drift along the curve, bunch up in some places and spread out in others. Practical snakes resample the curve and must detect when it crosses itself.
- **Topology is fixed.** A snake is one closed chain. It cannot split into two when it encloses two objects (Figure 11.6a), and it cannot merge with another snake, without extra, error-prone bookkeeping.

The last limitation is the one level sets remove.

## Segmentation using level sets

Instead of tracking the boundary itself, track a landscape whose shoreline is the boundary. Move the landscape up or down, and the shoreline moves with it. If the water rises enough to separate two hills, the one shoreline becomes two, with no special code.

### The implicit representation

In plain words: store a height at every pixel; the curve is the shoreline where the height is zero.

A **level-set function** is a scalar function $\phi: \Omega \times [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ such that the curve at time $t$ is its zero level set:

$$
C(t) = \{\, (x, y) \in \Omega \;:\; \phi(x, y, t) = 0 \,\}.
$$

We use the convention $\phi \lt 0$ inside the curve and $\phi > 0$ outside. (Some papers, including [8], use the opposite sign; the equations change sign accordingly.) Two geometric quantities come for free from $\phi$:

$$
\mathbf{N} = \frac{\nabla\phi}{\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert}, \qquad
\kappa = \nabla\cdot\frac{\nabla\phi}{\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert},
$$

where $\mathbf{N}$ is the unit **outward** normal and $\kappa$ is the **curvature** of the level curve through each point (positive on convex parts; for a circle of radius $r$, $\kappa = 1/r$).

The most convenient $\phi$ is the **signed distance function**: $\lvert\phi(x, y)\rvert$ is the Euclidean distance from $(x, y)$ to $C$, with the sign telling inside from outside. It satisfies $\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert = 1$ almost everywhere, which keeps the numerics well behaved.

### The level-set equation

Suppose each point of the curve moves along its outward normal with speed $F$ (positive means outward):

$$
\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = F\,\mathbf{N}.
$$

Points on the curve keep the value zero, so $\phi\big(C(t), t\big) = 0$ for all $t$. Differentiate with the chain rule:

$$
\phi_t + \nabla\phi\cdot\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = 0
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
\phi_t + F\,\nabla\phi\cdot\frac{\nabla\phi}{\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert} = 0
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
\boxed{\;\phi_t + F\,\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert = 0\;}
$$

This is the level-set equation of Osher and Sethian [5]. $\phi_t$ is the time derivative of $\phi$ and $F$ is the **speed function**, which may depend on position (image data), on the geometry of the curve (curvature $\kappa$), or on global statistics. The equation is solved on the fixed pixel grid for all $(x, y)$, not only on the curve; the curve is read off at any time by extracting the zero contour. Nothing in the equation cares how many pieces the zero set has, which is why splitting and merging happen naturally.

### Discrete computation: upwind differences and the CFL condition

In plain words: on a grid, the front must be updated with differences taken from the side it is coming from, the **upwind** side, as in the scheme of [5]. With $\phi^n_{ij}$ the value at pixel $(i, j)$ after $n$ steps of size $\Delta t$ (grid spacing $\Delta x = 1$), each step subtracts $\Delta t\,F_{ij}$ times an upwind estimate of $\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert$ built from one-sided differences; the `evolve` function below implements it. Using a plain central difference instead produces oscillations and corners that smear or explode; upwinding is what keeps the front sharp and stable.

*Optional: [The upwind scheme in formulas](#the-upwind-scheme-in-formulas) (in the Supplementary notes). The one-sided differences and the two upwind gradient estimates, written out.*

The explicit scheme is stable only if the front moves less than one cell per step. This is the **Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL)** condition:

$$
\Delta t \,\max_{ij}\lvert F_{ij}\rvert \le c\,\Delta x, \qquad 0 \lt c \le 1 .
$$

In practice you compute $\Delta t$ from the current maximum speed at each step, as the code below does.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi

def signed_distance(mask):
    """Negative inside, positive outside, ~0 on the boundary."""
    return ndi.distance_transform_edt(~mask) - ndi.distance_transform_edt(mask)

def evolve(phi, F, steps=100, cfl=0.5):
    """Upwind scheme for phi_t + F |grad phi| = 0 (F is an array or scalar)."""
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = np.pad(phi, 1, mode="edge")
        dxm = phi - p[1:-1, :-2]; dxp = p[1:-1, 2:] - phi   # backward / forward
        dym = phi - p[:-2, 1:-1]; dyp = p[2:, 1:-1] - phi
        grad_plus = np.sqrt(np.maximum(dxm, 0)**2 + np.minimum(dxp, 0)**2 +
                            np.maximum(dym, 0)**2 + np.minimum(dyp, 0)**2)
        grad_minus = np.sqrt(np.minimum(dxm, 0)**2 + np.maximum(dxp, 0)**2 +
                             np.minimum(dym, 0)**2 + np.maximum(dyp, 0)**2)
        dt = cfl / (np.abs(F).max() + 1e-12)             # CFL: |F| dt <= dx
        phi = phi - dt * (np.maximum(F, 0) * grad_plus + np.minimum(F, 0) * grad_minus)
    return phi

yy, xx = np.mgrid[:128, :128]
phi = signed_distance((xx - 64)**2 + (yy - 64)**2 < 20**2)
phi = evolve(phi, F=1.0, steps=20)                # grow outward by 20 * 0.5 = 10 px
phi = signed_distance(phi < 0)                     # re-initialize
print(np.sqrt((phi < 0).sum() / np.pi))            # radius, about 30
```

### Specifying the speed function

All the image analysis lives in $F$. Two families dominate.

#### Edge-based speed: geodesic active contours

In plain words: make the front fast in flat regions and slow on edges, so that it stops where the edges are.

Define an **edge-stopping function** that is close to $1$ in flat regions and close to $0$ on edges, for example

$$
g(x, y) = \frac{1}{1 + \lvert\nabla(G_\sigma * f)(x, y)\rvert^2}.
$$

Caselles, Kimmel and Sapiro [6] showed that a snake with $\beta = 0$ is closely related to finding a curve of minimal **weighted length**

$$
L_g(C) = \oint_C g\big(C(s)\big)\, ds,
$$

where $ds$ is arc length. In the metric defined by $g$, edges are "cheap" places to be, so a minimizer is a geodesic (shortest path) that hugs edges. This is the **geodesic active contour (GAC)**. Gradient descent on $L_g$, plus an optional constant pressure $c$ like Cohen's balloon, gives in our sign convention

$$
\phi_t = g\,\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert\,(\kappa - c) + \nabla g\cdot\nabla\phi .
$$

Each term has a job. $g\,\kappa\,\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert$ is curvature flow (shortening and smoothing) that slows to a stop near edges. $-c\,g\,\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert$ is the pressure: $c > 0$ inflates, $c \lt 0$ deflates, again switched off on edges. $\nabla g\cdot\nabla\phi$ is an advection term that pulls the curve into the bottom of the edge valley, where $g$ is minimal, and stops it from overshooting. Unlike a snake, the GAC is **intrinsic**: it does not depend on how the curve is parametrized, and in level-set form it splits and merges freely.

Because $g$ never reaches exactly zero, a strong pressure still leaks through weak or blurred edges. That is the motivation for region-based speeds.

#### Region-based speed: Mumford–Shah and Chan–Vese

In plain words: instead of looking for edges, choose the curve that splits the image into an inside and an outside that are each as uniform as possible.

Mumford and Shah [7] posed segmentation as finding a piecewise-smooth approximation of the image together with a set of boundaries. Their energy balances three wishes: stay close to the image, be smooth everywhere except across the boundaries, and keep the boundaries short. This energy is hard to minimize in general.

*Optional: [The Mumford–Shah energy](#the-mumfordshah-energy) (in the Supplementary notes). The full functional and what each of its three terms asks for.*

Chan and Vese [8] restricted $u$ to be **piecewise constant with two values**: $c_1$ inside $C$ and $c_2$ outside. The energy becomes

$$
E_{\text{CV}}(c_1, c_2, C) = \mu\,\lvert C\rvert + \lambda_1 \int_{\text{inside}(C)} (f - c_1)^2 \, dx\,dy + \lambda_2 \int_{\text{outside}(C)} (f - c_2)^2 \, dx\,dy,
$$

(the original also has an optional area term $\nu\,\text{Area}(\text{inside}(C))$, usually set to zero). $\mu \ge 0$ weights smoothness and $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 > 0$ weight how well each region is fitted by its constant. Note what is missing: there is no gradient. The model can segment objects whose boundaries are blurred or defined only by a change of average intensity, which is why [8] called it "active contours without edges".

To write this in level-set form, use the Heaviside function $H(z)$ ($1$ for $z \ge 0$, $0$ otherwise) and its derivative, the Dirac delta $\delta(z)$. With $\phi \lt 0$ inside, the inside indicator is $H(-\phi)$ and the length is $\lvert C\rvert = \int \delta(\phi)\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert$. For fixed $\phi$, the optimal constants are the region means

$$
c_1 = \frac{\int f\, H(-\phi)}{\int H(-\phi)}, \qquad c_2 = \frac{\int f\, H(\phi)}{\int H(\phi)} .
$$

For fixed $c_1, c_2$, gradient descent in $\phi$ gives

$$
\phi_t = \delta_\varepsilon(\phi)\,\Big[\, \mu\,\kappa + \lambda_1 (f - c_1)^2 - \lambda_2 (f - c_2)^2 \,\Big],
$$

where $\delta_\varepsilon$ is a smoothed delta of width $\varepsilon$. Read it pixel by pixel: if a pixel's intensity is far from $c_1$ but close to $c_2$, the bracket is positive, $\phi$ rises, and the pixel moves outside. The curvature term keeps the boundary short. The algorithm alternates between updating the means and updating $\phi$. Because $\delta_\varepsilon$ is non-zero away from the zero level set, the model can also **create new contours** far from the initial one, which helps detect interior holes and disconnected objects in the examples of [8]. This behavior depends on the chosen regularization; the nonconvex energy can still converge to different local minima from different initial curves.

*Optional: [Chan–Vese](#chanvese-locate-a-boundary-by-fitting-the-regions-on-both-sides) (in the Paper guides). Read the original region-fitting diagram, the initialization caveat and the difference between fitting energy and the complete objective.*

Chan–Vese is one member of a larger family of region-based models: any probability model of the regions can take the place of the two constants [14].

*Optional: [Other region models](#other-region-models) (in the Supplementary notes). How the same structure works with Gaussian, color, texture or motion models.*

```python
from skimage import data, img_as_float
from skimage.segmentation import chan_vese, morphological_chan_vese

cam = img_as_float(data.camera())

```

### Initializing and reinitializing as a signed distance function

The initial $\phi$ is usually the signed distance to a user-drawn curve, a box, a disk, or many small circles (a "checkerboard") so that the evolution starts near every possible object. As the PDE runs, $\phi$ drifts away from a distance function: it becomes very steep in some places and very flat in others. Steep regions force tiny time steps; flat regions make the zero crossing poorly located and sensitive to noise.

The classical remedy is **reinitialization**: every few iterations, replace $\phi$ by the signed distance to its current zero level set without moving that zero set, for example with a fast Euclidean distance transform as in the code above. Li, Xu, Gui and Fox [10] proposed **distance-regularized level-set evolution (DRLSE)**, which adds an energy penalty that keeps $\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert$ close to $1$ and so removes the need for reinitialization.

*Optional: [Reinitialization and DRLSE in detail](#reinitialization-and-drlse-in-detail) (in the Supplementary notes). The PDE alternative to the distance transform, its drawbacks, and the DRLSE penalty.*

### Improving efficiency

A full-grid level-set solver updates every pixel at every step, although only the zero crossing matters. Three standard speed-ups:

- **Narrow band.** Adalsteinsson and Sethian [9] update $\phi$ only within a band of half-width $w$ (a few pixels) around the zero level set. When the front approaches the edge of the band, the band is rebuilt around the new front (with a reinitialization). The work per step drops from the image area to roughly the curve length times $w$.
- **Larger stable steps.** Semi-implicit or operator-splitting schemes allow larger $\Delta t$ for the stiff curvature term, at the cost of solving a linear system per step.
- **Morphological approximations.** Márquez-Neila, Baumela and Álvarez [11] replace the PDE by a sequence of binary morphological operators (dilations, erosions and smoothing operators) acting on a *binary* level set. The result is fast, has no time step, needs no reinitialization and never becomes unstable.

scikit-image provides `morphological_geodesic_active_contour` and `morphological_chan_vese` based on [11], alongside the PDE-based `chan_vese` [12]. Keep in mind that the morphological variants **approximate** the PDEs. They move on the pixel grid, their "smoothing" parameter is a number of operator applications rather than the weight $\mu$, and their results are close to, not identical with, the continuous models.

*Optional: [How the morphological approximation works](#how-the-morphological-approximation-works) (in the Supplementary notes). Which operator replaces which PDE term, and what was proved about them.*

```python
from skimage import data, img_as_float
from skimage.segmentation import (inverse_gaussian_gradient, disk_level_set,
                                  morphological_geodesic_active_contour)

coins = img_as_float(data.coins())

                                             smoothing=1, balloon=-1, threshold=0.69)
```

Here `inverse_gaussian_gradient` builds the edge-stopping function $g$, `balloon=-1` shrinks the initial disk (the role of $c \lt 0$), and `threshold` decides which values of $g$ count as edges that stop the balloon.

## Snakes vs level sets in practice

Both families minimize similar energies. The difference is mostly in representation, and the representation decides what is easy.

In short: a snake has few unknowns (its $n$ points), is cheap per iteration, is easy to edit interactively and can carry point labels, but its topology is fixed and its points drift and can cross. A level set has one unknown per pixel (or per band pixel), but it splits and merges automatically, cannot self-intersect and extends directly to 3D. Their typical failures differ too: snakes stall on clutter or in concavities, edge-based level sets leak through weak edges, and region-based ones merge similar-looking regions.

*Optional: [Snakes vs level sets, point by point](#snakes-vs-level-sets-point-by-point) (in the Supplementary notes). A ten-row comparison table of the two representations.*

Xu, Yezzi and Prince studied the relationship between the two formulations [3]; for many energies the choice is about implementation, not about what can be modeled in principle. A rule of thumb:

- Use a **snake** when you need one boundary of known topology, interactively, fast, or with point correspondences (tracking a contour across video frames, fitting a lip outline, delineating a building footprint).
- Use a **level set** when the number of objects is unknown, objects may touch or split, the boundary is ill-defined by gradients (region-based models), or you work in 3D.

## Modern view

Active contours are no longer the default way to segment an image; deep networks are. But the ideas of this chapter, energies that combine boundary length with region fit, explicit contours deformed iteratively, and level sets as a shape representation, are still active ingredients in deep segmentation. Six papers give a good map.

**Deformable models in medical imaging.** McInerney and Terzopoulos [13] wrote the standard early survey. Its main takeaway still holds: deformable models are most useful when the image alone is ambiguous and the model must contribute smoothness and prior knowledge.

**Statistical region-based level sets.** Cremers, Rousson and Deriche [14] derive region-based level-set methods from one Bayesian framework in which each region gets a probability model (intensity, color, texture or motion). They argue that region-based energies are less sensitive to noise and have fewer local minima than edge-based snakes.

**A recent taxonomy.** Chen, Ge, Wang and Chen [15] sort active contour models into edge-based, region-based and hybrid families and compare them with deep segmentation. Classical models need no training data and give smooth closed boundaries but are sensitive to initialization and parameters; deep models are more accurate where labelled data exist; the two are complementary.

**Shape constraints in deep segmentation.** Bohlender, Oksuz and Mukhopadhyay [16] survey how shape knowledge, including active contours and signed distance maps, is injected into deep medical image segmentation to fix the fragmented regions and topological errors of pixel-wise classifiers.

*Optional: [The four surveys in more detail](#the-four-surveys-in-more-detail) (in the Paper guides). What each survey covers and how it organizes the field.*

**Explicit contours learned end to end.** Deep Snake by Peng et al. [17] keeps the snake's representation, a closed polygon deformed iteratively, but learns the update: a circular convolution over CNN features sampled at the vertices predicts per-vertex offsets toward the boundary, and the authors report real-time speed for instance segmentation. The circular convolution plays the role of the classical snake's pentadiagonal matrix $A$: it couples each vertex to its neighbours along the closed curve.

**Active-contour energies as loss functions.** Instead of running a contour at test time, several methods put the energy into the training loss: a supervised Chan–Vese-like loss with length and region terms [18], a label-free Mumford–Shah-style loss on a network's softmax outputs that also supports unsupervised and semi-supervised training [19], and the **boundary loss** [20], a regional integral weighted by the signed distance map of the ground truth, designed for highly unbalanced problems such as small lesions.

*Optional: [Three active-contour losses in detail](#three-active-contour-losses-in-detail) (in the Supplementary notes). How each loss is built and what its authors report.*

What deep learning changed, and what it did not:

- **Changed: the data term.** Hand-made forces ($\nabla e$, GVF, $g$, region means) are replaced by learned features. This is the main reason accuracy improved.
- **Not changed: the regularity prior.** Boundary length, curvature penalties, signed distance representations and region homogeneity reappear as loss terms and output representations, because pixel classifiers still produce ragged, fragmented boundaries without them.
- **Not changed: the explicit vs implicit trade-off.** Contour-based networks inherit the snake's fixed topology (one polygon per instance) and its speed; mask and distance-map networks inherit the level set's flexibility.
- **Still useful on its own.** When there are no labelled data, a Chan–Vese or GAC run is a few lines of scikit-image and needs no training, which makes it a good baseline, a pseudo-label generator, or a post-processing step that cleans up a network's mask.

## Key takeaways

- An active contour segments by **minimizing an energy over curves**: a regularity term (length, bending) plus a data term (edges or region fit). Gradient descent on that energy is a PDE in artificial time.
- A **snake** is an explicit chain of points. Its Euler–Lagrange equation $\alpha v_{ss} - \beta v_{ssss} + F_{\text{ext}} = 0$ is solved with a semi-implicit step $\mathbf{x}^t = (A + \gamma I)^{-1}(\gamma\mathbf{x}^{t-1} + \mathbf{f}_x)$.
- Plain gradient forces have a **small capture range** and fail in **concavities**. GVF diffuses edge vectors across the image; the balloon force inflates or deflates the curve.
- A **level set** stores the curve as $\{\phi = 0\}$ and evolves $\phi_t + F\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert = 0$ with **upwind differences** under a **CFL** step limit. Topology changes are automatic.
- The speed $F$ carries the model: **geodesic active contours** minimize edge-weighted length; **Chan–Vese** fits two region means and works without edges.
- Keep $\phi$ close to a **signed distance** (reinitialization or DRLSE), and save work with **narrow bands** or **morphological** approximations, which approximate rather than solve the PDEs.
- In deep learning the same ideas survive as **learned contour deformation** (Deep Snake) and as **loss functions** built from length, region and signed-distance terms.

## Supplementary notes

These notes are optional background. Nothing in the main text or the exercises depends on them.

### Deriving the snake equation

The snake's force balance in the main text follows from one general equation.

To minimize an integral of the form $\int L(v, v_s, v_{ss})\,ds$ we use the calculus of variations. A minimizer must satisfy the Euler–Lagrange equation

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial v} - \frac{d}{ds}\frac{\partial L}{\partial v_s} + \frac{d^2}{ds^2}\frac{\partial L}{\partial v_{ss}} = 0 .
$$

With $L = \tfrac12\alpha\lvert v_s\rvert^2 + \tfrac12\beta\lvert v_{ss}\rvert^2 + E_{\text{ext}}(v)$ we have $\partial L/\partial v = \nabla E_{\text{ext}}$, $\partial L/\partial v_s = \alpha v_s$ and $\partial L/\partial v_{ss} = \beta v_{ss}$. For constant $\alpha$ and $\beta$ this gives

$$
\alpha\, v_{ss} - \beta\, v_{ssss} - \nabla E_{\text{ext}}(v) = 0 .
$$

This is the force balance used in the main text.

### The GVF energy and its diffusion equations

GVF keeps the good part of $\nabla e$ (accurate, pointing at edges where edges exist) and fills in the rest of the image by smooth diffusion. Xu and Prince [3] define the GVF field $\mathbf{w}(x, y) = (u(x, y), v(x, y))$ as the minimizer of

$$
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{w}) = \iint_\Omega \mu\,\big(u_x^2 + u_y^2 + v_x^2 + v_y^2\big) \;+\; \lvert\nabla e\rvert^2\,\lvert \mathbf{w} - \nabla e\rvert^2 \; dx\,dy .
$$

- $u_x, u_y, v_x, v_y$ are the partial derivatives of the two field components; the first term asks the field to vary slowly.
- $\lvert\nabla e\rvert^2\,\lvert\mathbf{w} - \nabla e\rvert^2$ asks $\mathbf{w}$ to equal $\nabla e$, but only where $\lvert\nabla e\rvert$ is large, that is, near edges.
- $\mu > 0$ balances the two; it should grow with the noise level.

The Euler–Lagrange equations are a pair of decoupled diffusion equations. Solving them by gradient descent in time gives

$$
u_t = \mu\,\nabla^2 u - (u - e_x)\,\lvert\nabla e\rvert^2, \qquad
v_t = \mu\,\nabla^2 v - (v - e_y)\,\lvert\nabla e\rvert^2,
$$

where $\nabla^2$ is the Laplacian and $e_x$, $e_y$ are the components of $\nabla e$. Far from edges the second term vanishes and the field simply diffuses outward from the edges, carrying their direction with it. With unit time step and the 5-point Laplacian, the explicit scheme is stable for $\mu \le 1/4$ when $e$ is scaled to $[0, 1]$.

### The upwind scheme in formulas

The level-set equation $\phi_t + F\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert = 0$ is discretized on the pixel grid as follows.

Let $\phi^n_{ij}$ be the value at pixel $(i, j)$ after $n$ steps of size $\Delta t$, with grid spacing $\Delta x = 1$. The one-sided differences

$$
D^{-x}_{ij} = \phi_{ij} - \phi_{i-1,j}, \qquad D^{+x}_{ij} = \phi_{i+1,j} - \phi_{ij}
$$

(and $D^{\pm y}$ likewise) are combined so that information is always taken from the side the front is coming from. This is the **upwind** scheme of [5]:

$$
\phi^{n+1}_{ij} = \phi^n_{ij} - \Delta t\,\Big[\max(F_{ij}, 0)\,\nabla^{+}_{ij} + \min(F_{ij}, 0)\,\nabla^{-}_{ij}\Big],
$$

$$
\nabla^{+} = \sqrt{\max(D^{-x}, 0)^2 + \min(D^{+x}, 0)^2 + \max(D^{-y}, 0)^2 + \min(D^{+y}, 0)^2},
$$

$$
\nabla^{-} = \sqrt{\min(D^{-x}, 0)^2 + \max(D^{+x}, 0)^2 + \min(D^{-y}, 0)^2 + \max(D^{+y}, 0)^2}.
$$

If $F$ contains a curvature term $b\,\kappa$, that part behaves like diffusion and is discretized with central differences; it adds a stricter, second-order limit of roughly $\Delta t \le \Delta x^2 / (4b)$ in 2D. In practice you compute $\Delta t$ from the current maximum speed at each step.

### The Mumford–Shah energy

Mumford and Shah [7] posed segmentation as finding a piecewise-smooth approximation $u$ of the image $f$ together with a set of boundaries $C$:

$$
E_{\text{MS}}(u, C) = \int_\Omega (u - f)^2\, dx\,dy \;+\; \mu \int_{\Omega\setminus C} \lvert\nabla u\rvert^2\, dx\,dy \;+\; \nu\,\lvert C\rvert,
$$

where the first term asks $u$ to stay close to $f$, the second asks $u$ to be smooth *except across* $C$, and $\lvert C\rvert$ is the total boundary length. This energy is hard to minimize in general.

### Other region models

Cremers, Rousson and Deriche [14] show that the Chan–Vese model is one member of a large family: replace "constant intensity plus squared error" with any probability model of the region (Gaussian with unknown variance, color histograms, texture features, motion) and you get a region-based level-set method with the same structure, where the bracket of the Chan–Vese update becomes a log-likelihood ratio between inside and outside.

### Reinitialization and DRLSE in detail

As a level-set PDE runs, $\phi$ drifts away from a signed distance function, and two remedies exist.

The classical remedy is **reinitialization**: every few iterations, replace $\phi$ by the signed distance to its current zero level set without moving that zero set. You can recompute it with a fast Euclidean distance transform, as in the code above, or solve a PDE that drives $\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert$ toward $1$ while freezing the sign of $\phi$. Reinitialization is effective but it can shift the front slightly, and deciding *when* to do it is a heuristic.

Li, Xu, Gui and Fox [10] proposed **distance-regularized level-set evolution (DRLSE)**, which adds to the energy a penalty $\int p(\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert)$ whose minimum is at $\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert = 1$. The gradient flow of this penalty keeps $\phi$ close to a signed distance near the zero level set throughout the evolution, which removes the need for reinitialization.

### How the morphological approximation works

The morphological approximation of [11] replaces each term of the level-set PDE by a binary operator.

Márquez-Neila, Baumela and Álvarez [11] replace the PDE by a sequence of binary morphological operators acting on a *binary* level set $u \in \{0, 1\}$. Dilation and erosion play the role of the balloon term, a composition of line-based sup-inf and inf-sup operators approximates curvature flow, and the sign of $\nabla g\cdot\nabla u$ implements the advection term. They prove that these operators have the same *infinitesimal* behavior as the PDE terms. The result is fast, has no time step, needs no reinitialization and never becomes unstable.

### Snakes vs level sets, point by point

The table compares the explicit (snake) and implicit (level-set) representations of an active contour.

| | Snakes (explicit) | Level sets (implicit) |
|---|---|---|
| Unknowns | $n$ points on the curve | one value per pixel (or per band pixel) |
| Cost per iteration | $O(n)$ after one matrix factorization | $O(\text{pixels})$, or $O(\text{band})$ with a narrow band |
| Topology changes | not without extra logic | automatic splitting and merging |
| Self-intersection | possible; must be detected | impossible by construction |
| Point spacing | drifts; needs resampling | no points to manage |
| Shape priors, landmarks, open curves | easy: points can carry labels | harder: no correspondence between iterations |
| Interactive editing | natural (drag a point) | indirect |
| Extension to 3D | meshes; harder bookkeeping | same equations on a voxel grid |
| Sub-pixel accuracy | yes | yes, by interpolating the zero crossing |
| Typical failure | stalls on clutter or in concavities | leaks through weak edges (edge-based); merges similar-looking regions (region-based) |

### Three active-contour losses in detail

Several methods put an active-contour energy into the training loss of a network instead of running a contour at test time.

- Chen et al. [18] propose a loss for medical image segmentation that adds a boundary-length term and inside/outside region-fitting terms, a supervised analogue of the Chan–Vese energy, to a dense segmentation network, and report improvements over cross-entropy on cardiac MRI.
- Kim and Ye [19] treat the softmax outputs of a network as the characteristic functions of regions and use a Mumford–Shah-style piecewise-constant energy as the loss. Because the energy needs no labels, the same loss supports unsupervised and semi-supervised segmentation, and it can act as a regularizer in supervised training.
- Kervadec et al. [20] propose the **boundary loss**, inspired by graph-based optimization of active-contour flows. It writes a distance between the predicted and true boundaries as a regional integral weighted by the signed distance map of the ground truth, which is exactly the level-set function of the true contour. It is designed for highly unbalanced problems, such as small lesions, where overlap losses alone train poorly, and is used together with a regional loss.

### The four surveys in more detail

Choose a reading by topic: Boundary evidence, Region evidence, Representation, Shape prior.

Four surveys map active contours and their place next to deep learning.

<Figure src={paperReadingMap} alt="Contours: forces, regions and shape constraints" caption="Teaching reading map — the cards compare questions addressed by different papers. They are complementary categories, not a performance ranking or a diagram copied from one source." />

#### Deformable models in medical imaging

McInerney and Terzopoulos [13] wrote the standard early survey of deformable models in medical image analysis. They frame snakes and their 3D relatives as energy-minimizing physical models and review applications to segmentation, shape representation, matching and motion tracking across CT, MRI and other modalities. Their main takeaway still holds: deformable models are most useful when the image alone is ambiguous and the model must contribute smoothness and prior knowledge.

#### Statistical region-based level sets

Cremers, Rousson and Deriche [14] review region-based level-set methods and derive them from a common Bayesian framework: each region gets a probability model (for intensity, color, texture or motion), and the level set maximizes the posterior probability of the partition, optionally with a statistical shape prior. Their argument for region-based energies, which are less sensitive to noise and have fewer local minima than edge-based snakes, is the reason Chan–Vese-type terms dominate later work.

#### A recent taxonomy of active contour models

Chen, Ge, Wang and Chen [15] sort active contour models into edge-based (GAC, DRLSE), region-based (Chan–Vese and local-fitting variants designed for intensity inhomogeneity) and hybrid models, implement a set of representative models, and compare them with deep learning segmentation on synthetic, medical and natural images. Their conclusion is pragmatic: classical models need no training data and give smooth closed boundaries, but they are sensitive to initialization and parameters; deep models are more accurate where labelled data exist; the two are complementary.

#### Shape constraints in deep segmentation

Bohlender, Oksuz and Mukhopadhyay [16] survey how anatomical shape knowledge is injected into deep medical image segmentation: conditional random fields, statistical shape models, active contours, and implicit shape representations such as signed distance maps. Their motivation is exactly the weakness active contours were designed to fix: pixel-wise classifiers can produce fragmented regions and topological errors.

#### Reading these papers critically

Compare deformable models by the evidence they trust and the shapes they can represent. An edge-driven contour needs useful boundary evidence; a region model needs an appearance assumption; a level-set representation changes how topology can evolve; and a shape constraint restricts the set of acceptable solutions. These are partly independent choices. A method can use a region term and a shape prior without belonging to only one survey category.

For a segmentation result, inspect initialization, parameter selection and stopping as well as overlap scores. Sensitivity to a starting curve can matter more in use than one favorable average. Shape constraints can reject implausible predictions but also exclude valid pathology or unusual anatomy; that is a modeling tradeoff, not automatically an improvement. Read survey comparisons for the assumptions they expose, then use original studies to establish whether the same images, preprocessing and supervision were used. Numerical tables assembled from separate studies do not isolate the contour energy as the sole cause of a difference.

### Chan–Vese: locate a boundary by fitting the regions on both sides

#### Challenge

A contour driven only by image gradients can fail when the desired boundary is blurred or weak. Chan and Vese's TIP 2001 paper, “Active Contours Without Edges” [8], asks whether the regions themselves can determine the boundary. Its central assumption is that two regions can be usefully approximated by different constant intensities. “Without edges” means that the objective does not need an edge detector; it does not mean the output has no boundary. Read region segmentation and level-set notation before the numerical derivation.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperChanveseSource} alt="Four placements of a contour around a dark object illustrate how mixed inside or outside regions increase their intensity-fitting errors." caption="Original figure 1 — Compare what falls inside and outside each curve. A misplaced contour mixes the dark object with background in at least one region; the aligned contour minimizes the fitting term in this ideal two-region example." />

*Source: Chan and Vese [8], TIP 2001, PDF page 3 (printed page 268), figure 1. [Original PDF](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~lvese/PAPERS/IEEEIP2001.pdf).*

A region-based contour energy is

$$
E(C,c_1,c_2)=\mu\,\operatorname{Length}(C)+\nu\,\operatorname{Area}(\operatorname{inside}C)
+\lambda_1\int_{\operatorname{inside}C}(I(x)-c_1)^2\,dx
+\lambda_2\int_{\operatorname{outside}C}(I(x)-c_2)^2\,dx.
$$

$C$ is the closed contour, $x$ an image position, and $I(x)$ the observed intensity. $c_1,c_2$ are the mean intensities of the current inside and outside regions. Nonnegative $\mu$ penalizes boundary length, $\nu$ weights the area term, and positive $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ weight the two fitting errors. The integrals accumulate squared errors over the respective regions. The area weight may be set to zero; it should not be silently omitted when comparing configurations that use it.

**Follow the source diagram as an energy argument.** When the contour lies inside the dark object, the outside contains both bright and dark pixels, which one mean fits poorly. When it lies outside the object, the inside becomes mixed instead. For the ideal piecewise-constant example, matching the boundary removes both fitting errors. Real images also pay length/area costs, so that cartoon does not prove every true boundary minimizes the complete energy.

Represent the contour as the zero level set of a function $\phi(x)$, using the paper’s convention of positive values inside and negative values outside. The main chapter uses the opposite sign; switching conventions requires switching the region indicators and evolution signs consistently. Alternate between recomputing the region means and evolving $\phi$ to reduce the energy. Smooth approximations to the step and delta functions make the numerical evolution possible. Because the curve is implicit, components can split or merge without explicitly editing a polygon. The final segmentation still depends on numerical choices, regularization and initialization.

#### Results

Figures 4–6 demonstrate noisy, blurred and thin-structure examples. Figure 9 compares the proposed model with an edge-function model on a smooth contour, while later examples include natural images and contours not defined by a strong gradient. These are demonstrations of the region-fitting mechanism and topology handling, not a dataset-wide segmentation accuracy table. The reported hardware timings also belong to the original implementation and image sizes.

#### Ablations and parameter evidence

The paper studies regularizations of the step/delta functions and discusses local minima. Its length parameter controls scale: a larger penalty discourages small or complicated contours and can group structures that remain separate with a smaller penalty. This is parameter and numerical evidence, not a modern module-removal ablation. Compare initial contours and stopping rules as well as final masks; changing the initialization can change the optimization outcome even when the energy is unchanged.

#### Conclusion

The method substitutes regional statistical agreement for a strong local gradient cue. It is useful when an object and background have distinguishable average intensities despite weak boundaries. Read the energy before the solver: a faster evolution cannot repair a region model that does not describe the image.

#### Problems

Illumination variation, texture or multiple regions with similar means can violate the two-constant assumption. A length penalty may erase small objects, and a plausible-looking mask need not correspond to the semantic object a user wants. The energy is nonconvex; neither the illustration nor successful examples establish global convergence from every starting curve. Additional region models or constraints change the method and require separate evidence.
