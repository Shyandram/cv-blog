## The big picture

YOLOv4 is a paper about a recipe. Its authors combined established ideas with modifications of their own, collected published tricks for training and building detectors, tested which of them help a fast detector on one ordinary GPU, and assembled the winners into one model. That makes it a useful worked example: backbone, feature fusion, decoding, assignment, augmentation and post-processing are visible in one system. Later detectors change several of these decisions, giving us concrete alternatives to compare.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Imagine a cook who wants the best fast lunch that any home kitchen can make. Cookbooks list hundreds of tips. Some tips change only how you shop and prepare (they cost nothing at the table); others add a small step while cooking (they cost a few seconds per plate). The cook tries the tips one by one, keeps the ones that make the lunch tastier without making it slower, and writes down the final recipe. YOLOv4 is that recipe book for a detector: the "free" tips change only training, the "special" tips add a little work at test time.
</Callout>

Why it matters: one-stage detectors, including the YOLO family, are practical options for cameras, robots and industrial inspection. YOLOv4 sits in the middle of that line. Read closely, it explains the parts that its predecessors introduced (grids, anchors, multi-scale heads) and the parts that its successors replaced (anchors, NMS, the coupled head). This chapter therefore introduces YOLOv4 in detail in the main text and gives every predecessor and successor its own introduction in the Paper guides.

This chapter assumes [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/). Its [Core building blocks](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#core-building-blocks) define box regression targets, IoU and its variants, anchors and label assignment, non-maximum suppression (NMS), feature pyramids and the focal loss; its sections on [one-stage detectors](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#one-stage-detectors-yolo-ssd-and-retinanet), [anchor-free detectors](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#anchor-free-detectors-points-instead-of-boxes) and [DETR](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#set-prediction-with-transformers-detr-and-its-descendants) introduce the families we build on. ## Challenge

**Plain version.** YOLOv4 tests published techniques to combine accurate detection, real-time inference and training on a single conventional GPU.

### A fast detector that one ordinary GPU can train

The paper (Bochkovskiy, Wang and Liao, arXiv, April 2020) [27] starts from a practical complaint: the most accurate networks of the time did not run in real time and needed many GPUs and large mini-batches to train. Its stated goal is a detector that is fast "in production systems", meaning fast in measured frames per second on a GPU rather than small in a theoretical operation count, and that anyone can train and test on a single 1080 Ti or 2080 Ti card [27].

Two predecessors frame the problem. [YOLOv3](#yolov3-three-scales-and-darknet-53) [9] was fast but, by 2020, clearly less accurate than the best detectors on the strict COCO metric: at 608 × 608 it reached 33.0% AP in the YOLOv4 comparison table [27]. EfficientDet [28] was accurate and efficient in floating-point operations, but the paper's measurements show that low operation counts do not translate into high GPU frame rates (see [Backbone](#backbone-cspdarknet53)). YOLOv4 wants the accuracy of the second at the speed of the first.

### What "real-time" means and how YOLOv4 times it

A detector is real-time when the time it needs per frame is shorter than the time between frames. **Latency** is the time from receiving one frame to having its final boxes; **throughput** is how many frames are finished per second when many are processed together. For one frame,

$$
T_{\text{e2e}} = T_{\text{pre}} + T_{\text{net}} + T_{\text{post}},
$$

where $T_{\text{pre}}$ is pre-processing (decoding, resizing and normalizing the image, copying it to the accelerator), $T_{\text{net}}$ is the forward pass of the network, and $T_{\text{post}}$ is post-processing (decoding boxes, the confidence threshold, NMS). A camera at $f$ frames per second leaves $1/f$ seconds per frame: about 33.3 ms at 30 FPS. With batches of $B$ frames, throughput is $B / T_{\text{batch}}(B)$, where $T_{\text{batch}}(B)$ is the time for one batch; it grows with $B$, but every frame then waits for its whole batch, so a live camera cares about latency at **batch size 1**. Converting latency to FPS as $1000/T$ (with $T$ in milliseconds) is only meaningful at batch 1.

YOLOv4's speed tables follow this convention. Their captions state that the results are compared "with batch=1 without using tensorRT", that is, without NVIDIA's optimizing inference engine [29], and the tables are split by GPU architecture (Maxwell, Pascal and Volta) because different papers timed their models on different cards [27]. They mark "real-time" as 30 FPS or more. The paper does not state the numeric precision or whether NMS is inside the timed interval; the YOLOX report later timed its comparisons "with FP16-precision and batch=1 on a V100 to align the settings of YOLOv4" [15]. Keep these conditions in mind when you read the numbers in [Results](#results).

### Why the paper tests "freebies" and "specials"

Many published "features" claim to improve CNNs, but often only for one model, one task or small datasets. The paper therefore sorts candidate improvements into two bags and tests them [27]:

- **Bag of freebies (BoF):** methods that change only the training strategy or only increase the training cost, so inference stays exactly as fast. Data augmentation, regularization, label smoothing, box-regression losses and assignment rules belong here.
- **Bag of specials (BoS):** plug-in modules and post-processing steps that add a small inference cost but can improve accuracy noticeably. Receptive-field blocks, attention modules, feature-fusion paths, activation functions and NMS variants belong here.

The challenge, then, is not to invent one module but to find the combination that is best for a detector that must stay fast and must train on one GPU. The rest of this introduction follows the paper's own order: the architecture it selects, the freebies and specials it adds, the experiments that justify the choices, and what remains open.

## Method

**Plain version.** YOLOv4 retains the YOLOv3 head, adds CSPDarknet53 and an SPP/PANet neck, and combines training and inference refinements.

### The detector anatomy: input, backbone, neck and head

**Plain version.** Almost every detector has three parts. A **backbone** turns the image into feature maps at several resolutions. A **neck** mixes information between those resolutions. A **head** looks at every cell of every map and predicts boxes and classes.

The paper's Figure 2 and Section 2.1 give this vocabulary, which later papers adopted [27]:

- **Input:** an image, image patches or an image pyramid. YOLOv4 uses a single image resized to a square network resolution, typically 416, 512 or 608 pixels per side.
- **Backbone:** a network pre-trained on ImageNet [30] classification. GPU detectors use backbones such as VGG [31] or ResNet [32] variants; CPU detectors use mobile backbones.
- **Neck:** layers between backbone and head that collect feature maps from different stages, usually with several top-down and bottom-up paths, such as FPN [33] and PAN [34]; "additional blocks" such as SPP [35] enlarge the receptive field.
- **Head:** the predictor. A **dense prediction** head (one-stage detector) predicts at every location of the feature maps, with anchors (YOLO, SSD, RetinaNet) or without (CenterNet, FCOS). A **sparse prediction** head (two-stage detector, the R-CNN family) predicts only for a set of proposals.

YOLOv4's choice, in the paper's words reduced to a list, is: backbone CSPDarknet53 [36], neck SPP [35] and PAN [34], head YOLOv3 [9]. The head is **anchor-based** and **one-stage**: at three output strides it predicts, for each cell and each of three anchors, four box numbers, one objectness score and one score per class.

For a square input of side $L$ pixels and output strides 8, 16 and 32, the three output grids have $L/8$, $L/16$ and $L/32$ cells per side, and the number of predictions per image is

$$
N_{\text{pred}} = A \left[ \left(\tfrac{L}{8}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{L}{16}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{L}{32}\right)^2 \right],
$$

where $A = 3$ is the number of anchors per cell. At $L = 608$ the grids are $76^2 + 38^2 + 19^2 = 7{,}581$ cells, so $N_{\text{pred}} = 22{,}743$; at $L = 416$ they give 10,647 predictions. Each prediction is a vector of $4 + 1 + K$ numbers for $K$ classes; with COCO's 80 classes each output layer has $3 \times 85 = 255$ channels, the value in the released configuration [37]. All of these predictions must be filtered down to a few boxes by a confidence threshold and NMS, which is why post-processing cost matters for real-time use.

### Backbone: CSPDarknet53

**Plain version.** The backbone must see far enough to cover large objects and their context, have enough capacity for many objects at once, and run fast on a GPU. The paper compared three candidates and picked CSPDarknet53: YOLOv3's Darknet-53 with "cross-stage partial" connections that send only half of each stage's channels through the expensive blocks.

**Why this backbone.** Section 3.1 argues that a detector, unlike a classifier, needs a higher input resolution (for small objects), more layers (so the receptive field covers the larger input) and more parameters (to detect many objects of different sizes in one image). Table 1 of the paper compares three backbones at 512 × 512, with frame rates measured on an RTX 2070 [27]:

| Backbone | Receptive field | Parameters | BFLOPs | FPS (RTX 2070) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CSPResNeXt50 | 425 × 425 | 20.6 M | 31 | 62 |
| CSPDarknet53 | 725 × 725 | 27.6 M | 52 | 66 |
| EfficientNet-B3 [38] | 1311 × 1311 | 12.0 M | 11 | 26 |

Two lessons sit in this small table. First, operation counts do not predict GPU speed: EfficientNet-B3 needs about a fifth of CSPDarknet53's operations but runs at less than half its frame rate. Second, the paper reports that CSPResNeXt50 is the better ImageNet classifier while CSPDarknet53 is the better COCO detector backbone, so "best classifier" and "best detector backbone" differ; the ablations in [Backbones and pre-trained weights](#backbones-and-pre-trained-weights) test this directly. CSPDarknet53 has 29 convolutional layers of size $3 \times 3$ against 16 for CSPResNeXt50, hence its larger receptive field.

**What cross-stage partial connections do.** CSPNet [36] illustrates its design using DenseNet-style stages [39] and also applies it to other backbones, including ResNeXt [40]. In the DenseNet example, in which each layer receives the concatenation of all earlier outputs. The authors attribute part of the computational redundancy to duplicated gradient information. This is a design explanation, not a proof that all layers learn identical features. CSPNet splits the work. Write the base feature map of a stage as two channel groups,

$$
x_0 = [\,x_0',\; x_0''\,],
$$

where $[\cdot, \cdot]$ is concatenation along channels. Only $x_0''$ goes through the stage's $k$ blocks, producing $x_1, \dots, x_k$; their output passes through a transition layer (a $1 \times 1$ convolution) to give $x_T$; then $x_T$ is concatenated with the untouched part $x_0'$ and passed through a second transition layer:

$$
x_T = w_T * [\,x_0'', x_1, \dots, x_k\,], \qquad x_U = w_U * [\,x_0', x_T\,],
$$

where $*$ is convolution and $w_T, w_U$ are the transition weights. The CSPNet paper gives three purposes: splitting the paths can reduce duplicated gradient information, processing fewer channels can reduce computation, and the design can reduce memory traffic [36]. It also compares fusion orders and finds that the "fusion last" order, in which the block output is transformed before it is merged with $x_0'$, truncates the duplicated gradient flow and costs little accuracy [36]. Applied to Darknet-53, each residual stage becomes such a split–process–merge stage.

**Activation.** The backbone uses Mish (see [Activation: Mish](#activation-mish)); in the released configuration, Mish appears in the backbone and the leaky rectifier in the neck and head [37].

### Neck, part 1: the SPP block

**Plain version.** Objects can be much larger than what one cell "sees". The SPP block lets each cell of the coarsest feature map look at a large neighbourhood cheaply, by max-pooling over windows of several sizes and stacking the results as extra channels.

Spatial pyramid pooling was introduced to turn feature maps of any size into a fixed-length vector for classification [35]. That version cannot be used inside a fully convolutional detector. YOLOv3's SPP variant, which YOLOv4 adopts, keeps the spatial size: it applies max pooling with kernel sizes $k \in \{1, 5, 9, 13\}$ and stride 1, with padding so that the output has the same height and width, and concatenates the four results along channels [27]. For an input of shape $C \times H \times W$,

$$
\text{SPP}(F) = \big[\, F,\ \text{MaxPool}_{5}(F),\ \text{MaxPool}_{9}(F),\ \text{MaxPool}_{13}(F) \,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{4C \times H \times W},
$$

where $\text{MaxPool}_k$ is $k \times k$ max pooling with stride 1 and "same" padding, and $k = 1$ is the identity. The block is placed on the stride-32 map, where $H = W = 19$ for a 608 input, so a $13 \times 13$ window covers most of the map. The paper quotes YOLOv3-608 with SPP gaining 2.7% AP50 on COCO at 0.5% extra computation, and states that SPP "significantly increases the receptive field" while causing almost no reduction in speed [27]. The released configuration contains exactly the three max-pooling layers of sizes 5, 9 and 13 [37].

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import maximum_filter

def spp(x, kernels=(5, 9, 13)):
    """YOLO-style SPP. x: (C, H, W). Stride-1 max pooling with 'same' padding keeps H and W;
    the input and the pooled copies are concatenated along the channel axis."""
    pooled = [maximum_filter(x, size=(1, k, k), mode="constant", cval=-np.inf) for k in kernels]
    return np.concatenate([x] + pooled, axis=0)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(size=(512, 19, 19))          # e.g. the stride-32 map of a 608 x 608 input
print("SPP:", x.shape, "->", spp(x).shape)

one = np.zeros((1, 19, 19)); one[0, 9, 9] = 1.0   # a single strong activation
y = spp(one)
print("cells reached by one activation per group:", [int((g > 0).sum()) for g in y])
```

```text
SPP: (512, 19, 19) -> (2048, 19, 19)
cells reached by one activation per group: [1, 25, 81, 169]
```

The channel count quadruples while the grid stays $19 \times 19$, and one strong activation now influences up to $13 \times 13 = 169$ cells of the next layer. A $1 \times 1$ convolution after the block reduces the channels again.

### Neck, part 2: PANet path aggregation

**Plain version.** Deep feature maps know *what* is in the image; shallow ones know *where* edges are. A feature pyramid sends meaning downward to the fine maps; PANet adds a second path that sends precise location information back up to the coarse maps. YOLOv4 uses this two-way neck instead of YOLOv3's one-way one, and merges the paths by concatenation instead of addition.

FPN [33] (explained in [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#feature-pyramids-fpn)) builds levels $P_3, P_4, P_5$ by upsampling coarse features and adding them to finer ones (a top-down path). PANet [34] observed that the path from low-level features to the top of the pyramid is still very long, and added a **bottom-up path augmentation**. Starting from $N_2 = P_2$ in the original paper, each new level is

$$
N_{i+1} = \text{conv}_{3 \times 3}\Big( \text{conv}_{3 \times 3}^{\,\text{stride } 2}(N_i) + P_{i+1} \Big),
$$

where $\text{conv}^{\text{stride } 2}_{3 \times 3}$ halves the resolution of $N_i$, $+$ is element-wise addition with the FPN level $P_{i+1}$ of the same resolution, and the outer $3 \times 3$ convolution produces the new map [34]. In YOLOv4 the pyramid runs over strides 8, 16 and 32, and the three outputs $N_3, N_4, N_5$ feed the three heads. Section 3.1 says the authors chose PANet "instead of the FPN used in YOLOv3" [27].

**YOLOv4's modification.** Figure 6 of the paper replaces the shortcut (addition) in PAN with **concatenation**: $[\,\text{down}(N_i),\ P_{i+1}\,]$ along channels, followed by convolutions [27]. Concatenation keeps both inputs separate and lets the next convolution learn how to weight them, at the price of more channels. The paper lists this among the changes made "to make the designed detector more suitable for training on single GPU", but it does not report a separate ablation of addition versus concatenation.

### Head: the YOLOv3 head and box decoding

**Plain version.** At each cell, for each of three template boxes (anchors), the head outputs four numbers that move and stretch the template, one "is there an object?" score and one score per class. YOLOv4 keeps this head and fixes one numerical problem: in YOLOv3, a box centre close to a cell border was very hard to predict.

**Decoding.** The head follows [YOLOv2](#yolov2-and-yolo9000-anchors-priors-and-joint-training) [8] and [YOLOv3](#yolov3-three-scales-and-darknet-53) [9]. For a cell whose top-left corner is at $(c_x, c_y)$ in grid units and an anchor of width $p_w$ and height $p_h$, the network outputs $(t_x, t_y, t_w, t_h)$ and the box is

$$
b_x = \sigma(t_x) + c_x, \qquad b_y = \sigma(t_y) + c_y, \qquad b_w = p_w\, e^{t_w}, \qquad b_h = p_h\, e^{t_h},
$$

where $\sigma(t) = 1/(1 + e^{-t})$ is the logistic sigmoid, $(b_x, b_y)$ is the box centre in grid units (multiply by the stride for pixels) and $(b_w, b_h)$ its size. An objectness output $t_o$ gives $\sigma(t_o)$, and each class has its own sigmoid score, so a box can carry several labels; YOLOv3 trains the class scores with binary cross-entropy [9]. Each output stride has three anchors; the released YOLOv4 configuration uses the nine anchors (in pixels) $12 \times 16$, $19 \times 36$, $40 \times 28$ at stride 8, $36 \times 75$, $76 \times 55$, $72 \times 146$ at stride 16 and $142 \times 110$, $192 \times 243$, $459 \times 401$ at stride 32 [37].

**The grid-sensitivity problem.** Because $\sigma(t_x) \in (0, 1)$ never reaches 0 or 1, a centre that lies exactly on a cell border needs $t_x \to \pm\infty$, and centres near borders need very large $|t_x|$. Section 4.3 of the paper calls this "grid sensitivity": the network must output extreme values to place a centre near $c_x$ or $c_x + 1$ [27]. The fix is to multiply the sigmoid by a factor greater than 1. The paper states only that; the released code writes it as

$$
b_x = s\,\sigma(t_x) - \frac{s - 1}{2} + c_x, \qquad b_y = s\,\sigma(t_y) - \frac{s - 1}{2} + c_y,
$$

where $s \ge 1$ is the scale factor (`scale_x_y` in the configuration) and the shift $-(s - 1)/2$ keeps the range centred on the cell, so the centre can now cover $(c_x - (s-1)/2,\; c_x + 1 + (s-1)/2)$, slightly beyond the cell [37]. The released configuration uses $s = 1.2$, 1.1 and 1.05 at strides 8, 16 and 32 [37]. The snippet decodes one prediction and shows how much less extreme $t_x$ has to be.

```python
import numpy as np

sigmoid = lambda t: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-t))
logit = lambda p: np.log(p / (1 - p))

def decode(t, cell, anchor, stride, s=1.0):
    """One YOLOv3/YOLOv4 prediction t = (tx, ty, tw, th) -> box centre and size in pixels.
    s = 1 is the YOLOv3 formula; s > 1 is YOLOv4's grid-sensitivity fix."""
    tx, ty, tw, th = t
    bx = s * sigmoid(tx) - (s - 1) / 2 + cell[0]        # centre in grid units
    by = s * sigmoid(ty) - (s - 1) / 2 + cell[1]
    return bx * stride, by * stride, anchor[0] * np.exp(tw), anchor[1] * np.exp(th)

# cell (10, 7) of the stride-8 grid, anchor 36 x 75 pixels, raw outputs all zero
print("box (cx, cy, w, h):", np.round(decode((0, 0, 0, 0), (10, 7), (36, 75), 8), 1))

# which raw output tx puts the centre at a given position inside its cell?
for pos in (0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 1.0):
    row = []
    for s in (1.0, 1.2):
        p = (pos + (s - 1) / 2) / s                     # the sigmoid value that is needed
        row.append(f"s={s}: " + (f"{logit(p):5.2f}" if p < 1 else "  inf"))
    print(f"centre at {pos:4.2f} of the cell -> tx = " + " | ".join(row))
```

```text
box (cx, cy, w, h): [84. 60. 36. 75.]
centre at 0.50 of the cell -> tx = s=1.0:  0.00 | s=1.2:  0.00
centre at 0.90 of the cell -> tx = s=1.0:  2.20 | s=1.2:  1.61
centre at 0.99 of the cell -> tx = s=1.0:  4.60 | s=1.2:  2.29
centre at 1.00 of the cell -> tx = s=1.0:   inf | s=1.2:  2.40
```

With $s = 1.2$ a centre on the border needs $t_x = 2.40$ instead of infinity, so the targets the network must learn stay in a comfortable range. In the ablation table this is the column "S" (see [Detector bag of freebies](#detector-bag-of-freebies)).

**Training targets.** The head is trained with three terms: a box loss (CIoU in YOLOv4, see [Box loss](#box-loss-from-iou-to-ciou)), a binary cross-entropy objectness loss, and binary cross-entropy class losses on the positive predictions. In YOLOv3, a prediction whose box already overlaps a ground-truth box well but is not assigned to it is ignored in the objectness loss rather than pushed to "background"; YOLOv3 used an IoU of 0.5 for this rule [9], and the released YOLOv4 configuration sets it to 0.7 (`ignore_thresh`) [37]. Which predictions count as positives is the subject of the next section.

### Label assignment: several anchors per object

**Plain version.** During training, every object must be "given" to some predictions, which are then taught to fire on it. YOLOv3 gave each object to exactly one anchor, the best-shaped one. YOLOv4 keeps this best match and can add anchors whose shapes are similar enough, so an object can train several predictions.

In YOLOv3, an object is assigned to the single anchor whose shape has the highest IoU with the object's box, at the cell containing the object's centre [9]. YOLOv4's bag of freebies includes "using multiple anchors for a single ground truth". The released code assigns the best anchor first, then adds anchors with

$$
\text{IoU}(\text{truth}, \text{anchor}) \gt \tau
$$

as positives, where the IoU compares only widths and heights (both boxes placed on the same centre) and $\tau$ is the "IoU threshold" hyper-parameter [27]. The genetic search (below) selected $\tau = 0.213$, which is also the value in the released configuration (`iou_thresh`) [37]. Because the nine anchors are split across three strides, one object can now be learned on two output levels. The best-anchor fallback still provides a match when no anchor passes the threshold. The snippet demonstrates shape matching; a full implementation must also handle targets competing for the same prediction.

```python
import numpy as np

# the nine anchors (width, height in pixels) of yolov4.cfg; three per output stride
anchors = np.array([[12, 16], [19, 36], [40, 28], [36, 75], [76, 55], [72, 146],
                    [142, 110], [192, 243], [459, 401]], float)
stride = np.repeat([8, 16, 32], 3)

def shape_iou(wh, anchors):
    """IoU of boxes that share a centre: only width and height matter."""
    inter = np.minimum(wh[0], anchors[:, 0]) * np.minimum(wh[1], anchors[:, 1])
    return inter / (wh[0] * wh[1] + anchors.prod(1) - inter)

for wh in [(30, 60), (100, 90), (60, 120)]:
    iou = shape_iou(np.array(wh, float), anchors)
    pos = np.union1d([iou.argmax()], np.flatnonzero(iou > 0.213))
    print(f"object {wh[0]:3d}x{wh[1]:<3d} | best anchor {iou.argmax()} (IoU {iou.max():.2f}) | "
          f"IoU > 0.213: anchors {pos.tolist()} on strides {sorted(set(stride[pos].tolist()))}")
```

```text
object  30x60  | best anchor 3 (IoU 0.67) | IoU > 0.213: anchors [1, 2, 3, 4] on strides [8, 16]
object 100x90  | best anchor 6 (IoU 0.58) | IoU > 0.213: anchors [3, 4, 5, 6] on strides [16, 32]
object  60x120 | best anchor 5 (IoU 0.68) | IoU > 0.213: anchors [3, 4, 5, 6] on strides [16, 32]
```

Each object now trains four predictions instead of one. More positives give more training signal, especially for objects whose shape falls between two anchors, but they also teach several predictions to fire on the same object, which makes NMS indispensable. In the ablation table this is the column "IT". Later detectors replaced fixed shape rules with assignment based on the network's own predictions ([YOLOX](#yolox-anchor-free-yolo-with-simota), [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector)) and eventually with one-to-one assignment that removes NMS ([YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments)). [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#anchors-and-label-assignment) explains the IoU-based rules of two-stage detectors and of RetinaNet for comparison.

### Activation: Mish

**Plain version.** An activation function decides how a neuron responds to its input. YOLOv3 used the leaky rectifier, which has a sharp corner at zero. Mish is a smooth curve that behaves like the identity for large inputs and lets small negative values through, which the paper found to help both the classifier and the detector.

Mish is defined as [41]

$$
\text{Mish}(x) = x \tanh\!\big(\text{softplus}(x)\big) = x \tanh\!\big(\ln(1 + e^{x})\big),
$$

where $\text{softplus}(x) = \ln(1 + e^x)$. For large positive $x$, $\tanh(\text{softplus}(x)) \to 1$ and $\text{Mish}(x) \approx x$; for large negative $x$, it tends to 0; in between it dips slightly below zero, so it is not monotonic. The leaky rectifier of YOLO v1 to v3 is $\phi(x) = x$ for $x \gt 0$ and $0.1x$ otherwise [7]. The paper's activation candidates were ReLU, leaky ReLU, parametric ReLU, ReLU6, SELU, Swish [42] and Mish; it removed parametric ReLU and SELU as hard to train and ReLU6 as designed for quantized networks, and notes that Swish and Mish are both continuously differentiable [27].

```python
import numpy as np

mish = lambda x: x * np.tanh(np.log1p(np.exp(x)))   # x * tanh(softplus(x))
leaky = lambda x: np.where(x > 0, x, 0.1 * x)         # the leaky rectifier of YOLO v1-v3

for v in (-3.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 3.0):
    print(f"x = {v:4.1f} | Mish {mish(v):7.4f} | leaky {float(leaky(v)):5.2f}")
x = np.linspace(-6, 6, 120001)
i = mish(x).argmin()
print(f"Mish minimum {mish(x[i]):.4f} at x = {x[i]:.3f}")
h = 1e-6
print(f"slope at 0: Mish {(mish(h) - mish(-h)) / (2 * h):.4f}; leaky jumps from 0.1 to 1")
```

```text
x = -3.0 | Mish -0.1456 | leaky -0.30
x = -1.0 | Mish -0.3034 | leaky -0.10
x =  0.0 | Mish  0.0000 | leaky  0.00
x =  1.0 | Mish  0.8651 | leaky  1.00
x =  3.0 | Mish  2.9865 | leaky  3.00
Mish minimum -0.3088 at x = -1.192
slope at 0: Mish 0.6000; leaky jumps from 0.1 to 1

```

Mish is smooth everywhere (its slope at 0 is 0.6, while the leaky rectifier's slope jumps from 0.1 to 1), its smallest value is about $-0.31$, and for inputs above about 3 it is almost the identity. In the paper's classifier experiments Mish raised CSPResNeXt-50's top-1 accuracy from 77.9% to 78.9% on its own, and the detector built on the Mish-trained CSPDarknet53 was the best one (see [Ablations](#ablations)) [27]. The price is a slightly more expensive function than a rectifier.

### Box loss: from IoU to CIoU

**Plain version.** The box loss tells the network how wrong a predicted box is. Measuring errors in the four coordinates separately ignores that they describe one box. IoU-based losses measure the overlap instead, and the CIoU loss adds two more hints: how far apart the centres are and whether the shape is right.

The paper's Section 2.2 retraces the history [27]. Squared error on unnormalized box coordinates depends on their units and does not directly measure overlap. **UnitBox** uses $-\log(\text{IoU})$ for overlapping boxes [43]; the commonly used overlap loss $1-\text{IoU}$ remains finite at zero overlap. The latter considers the box as a whole and is invariant to a common rescaling, but is constant with zero gradient while boxes remain disjoint. **GIoU** [44] adds a penalty based on the smallest box enclosing both. **DIoU** and **CIoU** [45] add a centre-distance term and, for CIoU, an aspect-ratio term. The CIoU loss for a predicted box $B$ and ground truth $B^{gt}$ is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CIoU}} = 1 - \text{IoU} + \frac{\rho^2(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{b}^{gt})}{c^2} + \alpha v,
\qquad v = \frac{4}{\pi^2}\Big(\arctan\frac{w^{gt}}{h^{gt}} - \arctan\frac{w}{h}\Big)^2,
\qquad \alpha = \frac{v}{(1 - \text{IoU}) + v},
$$

where $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{b}^{gt}$ are the two box centres, $\rho$ is the Euclidean distance between them, $c$ is the diagonal length of the smallest box enclosing both, $(w, h)$ and $(w^{gt}, h^{gt})$ are the widths and heights, $v$ measures the aspect-ratio mismatch and $\alpha$ is a weight that gives the aspect term more influence once the boxes overlap well [45]. Dropping the last term gives the DIoU loss, and dropping the last two gives the IoU loss. [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#iou-and-its-variants) computes all four measures for disjoint and overlapping boxes; the snippet below instead splits the CIoU loss into its three parts for one tall ground-truth box.

```python
import numpy as np

def iou_terms(a, b):
    """a, b: boxes (x1, y1, x2, y2). Returns IoU, the DIoU penalty rho^2/c^2 and the CIoU term alpha*v."""
    iw = max(0.0, min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0])); ih = max(0.0, min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1]))
    inter = iw * ih
    wa, ha, wb, hb = a[2] - a[0], a[3] - a[1], b[2] - b[0], b[3] - b[1]
    iou = inter / (wa * ha + wb * hb - inter)
    cw = max(a[2], b[2]) - min(a[0], b[0]); ch = max(a[3], b[3]) - min(a[1], b[1])
    rho2 = ((a[0] + a[2] - b[0] - b[2]) ** 2 + (a[1] + a[3] - b[1] - b[3]) ** 2) / 4
    v = 4 / np.pi ** 2 * (np.arctan(wb / hb) - np.arctan(wa / ha)) ** 2
    alpha = v / ((1 - iou) + v) if v > 0 else 0.0
    return iou, rho2 / (cw ** 2 + ch ** 2), alpha * v

gt = (100, 100, 200, 300)                                  # 100 x 200 pixels, tall
preds = {"centred, lying flat":       (50, 150, 250, 250),
         "shifted, right shape":      (125, 125, 225, 325),
         "far away":                  (300, 100, 400, 300)}
for name, p in preds.items():
    iou, d, av = iou_terms(p, gt)
    print(f"{name:26s} IoU {iou:.3f} | L_IoU {1 - iou:.3f} | L_DIoU {1 - iou + d:.3f} | L_CIoU {1 - iou + d + av:.3f}")
```

```text
centred, lying flat        IoU 0.333 | L_IoU 0.667 | L_DIoU 0.667 | L_CIoU 0.700
shifted, right shape       IoU 0.488 | L_IoU 0.512 | L_DIoU 0.530 | L_CIoU 0.530
far away                   IoU 0.000 | L_IoU 1.000 | L_DIoU 1.308 | L_CIoU 1.308
```

The centred but wrongly shaped box gets no DIoU penalty (the centres coincide) but a CIoU penalty for its shape; the shifted box of the right shape gets a distance penalty only; the far-away box has IoU 0, so the IoU loss says nothing about how far it is, while the distance term does. YOLOv4 adopts CIoU, arguing that it gives better convergence speed and accuracy [27]; the released configuration sets `iou_loss=ciou` with a loss weight of 0.07 (`iou_normalizer`, the "loss normalizer" found by the genetic search) [37].

### Post-processing: NMS and DIoU-NMS

**Plain version.** With several positives per object, several predictions fire on each object, and non-maximum suppression keeps the best one and deletes the others that overlap it too much. Its cost depends on the scene. DIoU-NMS changes the deletion test so that two boxes whose centres are far apart are less likely to be treated as duplicates, which helps with neighbouring objects.

**Greedy NMS and its cost.** Greedy NMS (defined in [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#non-maximum-suppression-nms-and-soft-nms)) takes the highest-scoring box, computes its IoU with every remaining box, deletes those above a threshold, and repeats. If $n$ boxes enter and few are deleted, the number of IoU computations approaches

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} k = \frac{n(n-1)}{2},
$$

where $k$ counts the boxes still remaining after each kept box. If most boxes are duplicates of a few objects, they are deleted early and the work is much smaller. So NMS time depends on the number of candidates, on the confidence threshold that decides how many enter, and on the scene, unlike the forward pass, which takes about the same time for every image. The snippet times a NumPy greedy NMS on synthetic candidates; counts of IoU computations are deterministic, while the times depend on your machine.

```python
import numpy as np

def nms_count(boxes, scores, thr=0.5):
    """Greedy NMS that also counts how many box-pair IoUs it computes."""
    order = np.argsort(-scores)
    keep, n_iou = [], 0
    while order.size:
        i, rest = order[0], order[1:]
        keep.append(i)
        n_iou += rest.size
        x1 = np.maximum(boxes[i, 0], boxes[rest, 0]); y1 = np.maximum(boxes[i, 1], boxes[rest, 1])
        x2 = np.minimum(boxes[i, 2], boxes[rest, 2]); y2 = np.minimum(boxes[i, 3], boxes[rest, 3])
        inter = np.clip(x2 - x1, 0, None) * np.clip(y2 - y1, 0, None)
        area = lambda b: (b[..., 2] - b[..., 0]) * (b[..., 3] - b[..., 1])
        iou = inter / (area(boxes[i]) + area(boxes[rest]) - inter)
        order = rest[iou <= thr]
    return len(keep), n_iou

def random_boxes(n, n_objects, rng, size=640):
    """n candidate boxes jittered around n_objects true objects."""
    centres = rng.uniform(40, size - 40, (n_objects, 2))
    owner = rng.integers(0, n_objects, n)
    c = centres[owner] + rng.normal(0, 6, (n, 2))
    wh = rng.uniform(30, 60, (n, 2))
    return np.hstack([c - wh / 2, c + wh / 2]), rng.random(n)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for n_objects in (5, 500):                       # few objects vs a crowded scene
    for n in (100, 1000, 8400):
        b, s = random_boxes(n, n_objects, rng)
        kept, n_iou = nms_count(b, s)
        print(f"objects {n_objects:3d} | boxes {n:4d} | kept {kept:4d} | IoUs {n_iou:9,d}")
```

```text
objects   5 | boxes  100 | kept   21 | IoUs       770
objects   5 | boxes 1000 | kept   43 | IoUs     7,809
objects   5 | boxes 8400 | kept   86 | IoUs    90,699
objects 500 | boxes  100 | kept   94 | IoUs     4,621
objects 500 | boxes 1000 | kept  513 | IoUs   209,490
objects 500 | boxes 8400 | kept 1259 | IoUs 3,039,292
```

The printed counts are deterministic; Figure 2 separately measures wall-clock time, which varies by machine. With the same 8,400 candidates, the crowded scene needs about 34 times as many IoU computations as the sparse one. Optimized implementations can run on a GPU and cap the number of boxes, so these counts are a toy analysis rather than a runtime prediction: post-processing time grows with the number of candidates and with crowding. Figure 2 repeats the experiment over a range of candidate counts. This is the cost that the NMS-free successors remove ([YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments), [RT-DETR](#rt-detr-a-real-time-detr)); the RT-DETR paper measured it directly for real detectors.

**DIoU-NMS.** Greedy NMS deletes a box $B_i$ when its IoU with the kept box $M$ exceeds the threshold, so two real people standing close together can be merged into one. The DIoU paper proposed to subtract the centre-distance penalty before the test [45]:

$$
s_i \leftarrow \begin{cases} s_i, & \text{IoU}(M, B_i) - \dfrac{\rho^2(\mathbf{b}_M, \mathbf{b}_i)}{c^2} \lt \varepsilon, \\[2mm] 0, & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases}
$$

where $s_i$ is the score of $B_i$ (0 means deleted), $\rho$ is the distance between the centres $\mathbf{b}_M$ and $\mathbf{b}_i$, $c$ is the diagonal of the box enclosing both, and $\varepsilon$ is the NMS threshold. Two boxes with distant centres "probably locate different objects" and survive more easily [45]. YOLOv4 lists DIoU-NMS in its bag of specials for the detector [27]. The snippet repeats the box geometry so it can be run on its own.

```python
import numpy as np

def iou_terms(a, b):
    """a, b: boxes (x1, y1, x2, y2). Returns IoU, the DIoU penalty rho^2/c^2 and the CIoU term alpha*v."""
    iw = max(0.0, min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0])); ih = max(0.0, min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1]))
    inter = iw * ih
    wa, ha, wb, hb = a[2] - a[0], a[3] - a[1], b[2] - b[0], b[3] - b[1]
    iou = inter / (wa * ha + wb * hb - inter)
    cw = max(a[2], b[2]) - min(a[0], b[0]); ch = max(a[3], b[3]) - min(a[1], b[1])
    rho2 = ((a[0] + a[2] - b[0] - b[2]) ** 2 + (a[1] + a[3] - b[1] - b[3]) ** 2) / 4
    v = 4 / np.pi ** 2 * (np.arctan(wb / hb) - np.arctan(wa / ha)) ** 2
    alpha = v / ((1 - iou) + v) if v > 0 else 0.0
    return iou, rho2 / (cw ** 2 + ch ** 2), alpha * v


def nms(boxes, scores, thr=0.5, diou=False):
    order, keep = list(np.argsort(-scores)), []
    while order:
        m = order.pop(0); keep.append(int(m))
        crit = lambda j: iou_terms(boxes[m], boxes[j])[0] - (iou_terms(boxes[m], boxes[j])[1] if diou else 0)
        order = [j for j in order if crit(j) < thr]
    return keep

# two people side by side (centres 32 px apart) and one duplicate of the first person
boxes = np.array([[100, 100, 200, 200], [132, 100, 232, 200], [103, 100, 203, 200]], float)
scores = np.array([0.9, 0.8, 0.7])
print("greedy NMS keeps:", nms(boxes, scores), "| DIoU-NMS keeps:", nms(boxes, scores, diou=True))
```

```text
greedy NMS keeps: [0] | DIoU-NMS keeps: [0, 1]
```

The second person overlaps the first with IoU 0.515, just above the threshold, so greedy NMS deletes it; the distance penalty lowers the criterion to 0.478 and DIoU-NMS keeps it, while the near-duplicate (centres 3 pixels apart) is still removed. The effect is confined to a narrow band of overlaps, and cannot resolve all crowded-scene ambiguity. The YOLOv4 ablations do not isolate its contribution.

### Data augmentation: Mosaic and CutMix

**Plain version.** Mosaic glues four training images into one, so every training image shows objects in four different contexts and at smaller sizes. It is the augmentation most associated with YOLOv4 and the one most copied by later detectors.

Section 2.2 of the paper surveys pixel-wise augmentations (photometric changes, random scaling, cropping, flipping, rotation), occlusion-style augmentations that erase rectangles, and augmentations that combine images: MixUp [46] blends two images with weights $\lambda$ and $1 - \lambda$ and blends their labels the same way, and CutMix [47] pastes a rectangle from one image into another and mixes the labels in proportion to the pasted area [27]. YOLOv4 uses CutMix for the backbone's classifier training and introduces **Mosaic**, which "mixes 4 training images" [27]. The paper's acknowledgements credit Glenn Jocher for the idea of Mosaic, along with the genetic hyper-parameter search and the grid-sensitivity fix [27].

The paper gives two reasons for Mosaic: objects are seen "outside their normal context", and batch normalization computes its statistics from four different images per training image, which "significantly reduces the need for a large mini-batch size" [27]. That second reason links Mosaic to the single-GPU goal.

The paper does not specify the procedure in more detail than its Figure 3, and implementations differ in how they scale and crop. A simple version, written out below, chooses a random split point on the output canvas, places the four images so that each touches the split point with one corner, cuts away whatever falls outside each image's quadrant, and clips or drops the boxes accordingly.

```python
import numpy as np

def mosaic(images, boxes, size=416, rng=None, min_keep=0.25):
    """Place four images around a random split point (xc, yc) of a size x size canvas.
    images: four (H, W, 3) arrays; boxes: four (n, 4) arrays of (x1, y1, x2, y2) in pixels.
    Each image touches the split point with one corner; whatever falls outside its quadrant is cut.
    A box is kept if at least `min_keep` of its area survives the cut."""
    xc, yc = (int(v) for v in rng.uniform(0.3, 0.7, 2) * size)
    canvas = np.full((size, size, 3), 114, np.uint8)
    out, n_cut, n_drop = [], 0, 0
    for k, (img, bx) in enumerate(zip(images, boxes)):
        h, w = img.shape[:2]
        ox = xc - w if k in (0, 2) else xc          # where the image's top-left corner lands
        oy = yc - h if k in (0, 1) else yc
        rx0, rx1 = (0, xc) if k in (0, 2) else (xc, size)   # the quadrant it may fill
        ry0, ry1 = (0, yc) if k in (0, 1) else (yc, size)
        x0, x1, y0, y1 = max(rx0, ox), min(rx1, ox + w), max(ry0, oy), min(ry1, oy + h)
        canvas[y0:y1, x0:x1] = img[y0 - oy:y1 - oy, x0 - ox:x1 - ox]
        shifted = bx + [ox, oy, ox, oy]
        clipped = np.clip(shifted, [x0, y0, x0, y0], [x1, y1, x1, y1])
        area = lambda b: (b[:, 2] - b[:, 0]) * (b[:, 3] - b[:, 1])
        keep = area(clipped) >= min_keep * area(shifted)
        n_cut += int((keep & (area(clipped) < area(shifted))).sum()); n_drop += int((~keep).sum())
        out.append(clipped[keep])
    return canvas, np.concatenate(out), (xc, yc), n_cut, n_drop

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
imgs, bxs = [], []
for k in range(4):                                   # four synthetic 256 x 256 "photos"
    img = np.full((256, 256, 3), 60 * k + 40, np.uint8)
    b = []
    for _ in range(3):                               # three rectangles = three objects each
        x, y = rng.integers(0, 200, 2); w, h = rng.integers(30, 90, 2)
        x2, y2 = min(x + w, 256), min(y + h, 256)
        img[y:y2, x:x2] = rng.integers(0, 256, 3); b.append([x, y, x2, y2])
    imgs.append(img); bxs.append(np.array(b, float))

canvas, boxes, split, n_cut, n_drop = mosaic(imgs, bxs, rng=rng)
print("canvas", canvas.shape, "| split point", split)
print(f"objects in: {sum(len(b) for b in bxs)} | kept: {len(boxes)} (of which cut: {n_cut}) | dropped: {n_drop}")
```

```text
canvas (416, 416, 3) | split point (223, 181)
objects in: 12 | kept: 9 (of which cut: 3) | dropped: 3
```

Figure 3 draws this example. Three of the twelve objects survive only partly and three are cut away almost completely; the rule for dropping a box (here, less than a quarter of its area left) is an implementation choice that the paper does not discuss. Mosaic later became the standard YOLO augmentation, and [DEIM](#deim-dense-one-to-one-matching) uses it for a different purpose: to put more objects in each image so that one-to-one matching receives more positives.

### Self-adversarial training

**Plain version.** Self-adversarial training (SAT) first lets the network change the training image, not its weights, so that the image fools the network into seeing no object; then it trains the network on that altered image in the normal way. It is a data augmentation generated by the detector itself.

The paper introduces SAT as "a new data augmentation technique that operates in 2 forward backward stages" [27]. In the first stage the network "alters the original image instead of the network weights", executing "an adversarial attack on itself" to create "the deception that there is no desired object"; in the second stage it is "trained to detect an object on this modified image in the normal way" [27]. The paper describes the stages in words only. One way to write them, in our notation rather than the paper's, is

$$
x' = x + \eta\, \nabla_x \mathcal{L}\big(f_\theta(x), y\big), \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \lambda\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}\big(f_\theta(x'), y\big),
$$

where $x$ is the training image, $y$ its boxes and labels, $f_\theta$ the detector with weights $\theta$, $\mathcal{L}$ the detection loss, $\eta$ the size of the image change (the weights are frozen during this step) and $\lambda$ the learning rate. Stage 1 moves the image to increase the chosen objective; increasing a generic detection loss can also disturb localization rather than merely hide an object. This illustrative objective is not an exact SAT specification from the paper; stage 2 is an ordinary training step on the altered image. SAT requires additional training computation and adds no inference stage; the paper does not separately measure its training-time overhead. The paper lists SAT among the detector's bag of freebies, but its ablation tables contain no SAT column, so its contribution is not measured separately (see [Limits of the evidence](#limits-of-the-evidence)).

### Regularization: DropBlock and class label smoothing

**Plain version.** Regularizers make training harder on purpose so that the network does not memorize the training set. DropBlock switches off whole square patches of a feature map instead of scattered single units; label smoothing tells the network never to be completely sure about a class.

**DropBlock.** Dropout [48] zeroes random units independently. In a convolutional feature map, neighbouring units are strongly correlated, so a zeroed unit can be reconstructed from its neighbours and dropout regularizes weakly. DropBlock [49] zeroes contiguous $b \times b$ blocks. Following its Algorithm 1: sample seed positions $M_{ij} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\gamma)$ only where a whole block fits inside the map, zero a $b \times b$ square around each seed, multiply the activations by the mask, and rescale them by $\text{count}(M)/\text{count\_ones}(M)$ to compensate approximately for the fraction of surviving activations [49]. To drop roughly a fraction $1 - p_{\text{keep}}$ of the units on a $f \times f$ map, the seed probability is

$$
\gamma = \frac{1 - p_{\text{keep}}}{b^2} \cdot \frac{f^2}{(f - b + 1)^2},
$$

where $p_{\text{keep}}$ is the keep probability as in dropout, $b$ the block size, $f$ the feature-map size and $(f - b + 1)^2$ the number of valid seed positions; the formula is approximate because blocks can overlap [49]. The DropBlock paper also found that a fixed $p_{\text{keep}}$ hurts early training and gradually decreases it from 1 to the target value [49].

```python
import numpy as np

def dropblock_mask(feat, block, keep_prob, rng):
    """One DropBlock mask for a feat x feat map (Ghiasi et al., Algorithm 1)."""
    gamma = (1 - keep_prob) / block ** 2 * feat ** 2 / (feat - block + 1) ** 2
    half = block // 2
    seeds = np.zeros((feat, feat), bool)                       # only where a whole block fits
    seeds[half:feat - half, half:feat - half] = rng.random((feat - block + 1,) * 2) < gamma
    mask = np.ones((feat, feat))
    for i, j in zip(*np.nonzero(seeds)):
        mask[i - half:i + half + 1, j - half:j + half + 1] = 0.0
    return mask, gamma

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
feat, block, keep = 38, 7, 0.9
masks = [dropblock_mask(feat, block, keep, rng)[0] for _ in range(2000)]
gamma = dropblock_mask(feat, block, keep, rng)[1]
dropped = 1 - np.mean([m.mean() for m in masks])
print(f"gamma = {gamma:.5f} | target drop rate {1 - keep:.2f} | measured {dropped:.3f}")
m = masks[0]
print("first mask: dropped units", int((m == 0).sum()), "| rescale factor", round(m.size / m.sum(), 3))
```

```text
gamma = 0.00288 | target drop rate 0.10 | measured 0.094
first mask: dropped units 171 | rescale factor 1.134
```

On a $38 \times 38$ map with $7 \times 7$ blocks, a seed probability below 0.3% already removes about 9.4% of the units, a little less than the 10% target because overlapping blocks are counted once. YOLOv4 chose DropBlock "without hesitation" because its authors' comparison with other methods was favourable [27], and lists it in both the backbone's and the detector's bag of freebies; the detector ablation table has no DropBlock column.

**Class label smoothing.** With hard one-hot targets, cross-entropy keeps pushing the correct logit up, which encourages over-confidence. Label smoothing [50] replaces the target distribution by

$$
q'(k) = (1 - \epsilon)\,\delta_{k,y} + \frac{\epsilon}{K},
$$

where $k$ indexes the $K$ classes, $y$ is the true class, $\delta_{k,y}$ is 1 if $k = y$ and 0 otherwise, and $\epsilon$ is the smoothing amount [50]. YOLOv4's detector uses independent sigmoid scores, and its ablation table describes LS as "class label smoothing for sigmoid activation" [27]; for one two-way sigmoid output the same formula with $K = 2$ moves the targets from 1 and 0 to $1 - \epsilon/2$ and $\epsilon/2$. The paper does not state the value of $\epsilon$. Label smoothing helped the classifier (CSPResNeXt-50: 77.9% → 78.1% top-1) but lowered the detector's AP when used alone (38.0% → 33.0%; see [Detector bag of freebies](#detector-bag-of-freebies)) [27].

### Normalization: cross mini-batch normalization

**Plain version.** Batch normalization needs statistics computed over many images. On one GPU, a detector only fits a few images at a time. Cross mini-batch normalization (CmBN) collects the statistics over the several small mini-batches that together make up one training batch.

**Batch normalization** [51] normalizes each channel with the mean $\mu_{\mathcal{B}}$ and variance $\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}^2$ of the current mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$: $\hat{z} = \gamma\,(z - \mu_{\mathcal{B}}) / \sqrt{\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}^2 + \epsilon} + \beta$, where $z$ is an activation, $\gamma, \beta$ are learned scale and shift and $\epsilon$ is a small constant. With only 4 or 8 images per mini-batch these statistics are noisy. **Cross-iteration batch normalization (CBN)** [52] averages statistics over several recent training iterations; because the weights change between iterations, it compensates the old statistics with a Taylor-polynomial approximation of how they would look under the current weights [52]. Synchronizing statistics across GPUs does not solve the single-GPU constraint.

YOLOv4 trains with a batch of 64 images split into mini-batches of 8 or 4 (the configuration's `subdivisions`), and the weights are updated once per batch [27], [37]. **CmBN** "collects statistics only between mini-batches within a single batch" [27]. In our reading of the paper's Figure 4, the statistics used for the $t$-th mini-batch of a batch accumulate those of mini-batches $1, \dots, t$ of the same batch, for example

$$
\mu^{(t)} = \frac{1}{t} \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} \mu_{\mathcal{B}_\tau}, \qquad \sigma^{2\,(t)} = \frac{1}{t} \sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left(\sigma^2_{\mathcal{B}_\tau}+\mu_{\mathcal{B}_\tau}^2\right)-\left(\mu^{(t)}\right)^2,
$$

where $\mathcal{B}_\tau$ is the $\tau$-th equally sized mini-batch and the variances are population moments. Averaging variances alone would omit differences between mini-batch means. Since weights do not change inside this batch, no cross-update compensation is needed. This illustrates correct moment aggregation; it is not an equation supplied by YOLOv4 or a complete specification of its released implementation. In the ablation (column CBN) CmBN alone gave 38.4% AP against the baseline's 38.0% [27].

### Attention and fusion: the modified SAM and weighted residuals

**Plain version.** An attention module lets the network re-weight its features, emphasizing informative positions. YOLOv4 takes the spatial attention module of CBAM and simplifies it to an element-wise gate. It also lists a learned-weight way of adding feature maps, taken from EfficientDet.

**SAM.** The spatial attention module of CBAM [53] pools the feature map $F \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ along the channel axis by average and by max, concatenates the two $1 \times H \times W$ maps, and convolves them:

$$
M_s(F) = \sigma\big(f^{7 \times 7}([\,\text{AvgPool}_c(F);\ \text{MaxPool}_c(F)\,])\big), \qquad F' = M_s(F) \otimes F,
$$

where $f^{7 \times 7}$ is a $7 \times 7$ convolution, $\sigma$ the sigmoid and $\otimes$ multiplication broadcast over channels, so one weight per position scales all channels [53]. The paper notes that SAM costs about 0.1% extra computation on ImageNet classifiers and "does not affect the speed of inference on the GPU at all", whereas it estimates that the representative channel-attention module adds about 10% to GPU inference time [27]. YOLOv4 modifies SAM "from spatial-wise attention to point-wise attention" (its Figure 5): in that figure the two pooling steps are replaced by a convolution applied to $F$ itself, so the gate $\sigma(\text{conv}(F))$ has the same shape $C \times H \times W$ as $F$ and scales every element separately [27]. In the specials ablation, adding SAM to CSPResNeXt50-PANet-SPP raised AP from 42.4% to 42.7% [27].

**Multi-input weighted residual connections (MiWRC).** EfficientDet's feature network adds several inputs with learned non-negative weights, using "fast normalized fusion" [28]:

$$
O = \sum_i \frac{w_i}{\varepsilon + \sum_j w_j}\, I_i,
$$

where $I_i$ are the input feature maps, $w_i \ge 0$ (enforced by a ReLU) are learned weights and $\varepsilon = 0.0001$ avoids division by zero [28]. YOLOv4 lists MiWRC in the bag of specials for the backbone but reports no experiment that isolates it.

### Training schedule: cosine annealing, genetic search and random shapes

**Plain version.** Several freebies concern how training is run rather than what the network computes: how the learning rate changes, how the many hyper-parameters are chosen, and which input sizes the network sees.

**Cosine annealing.** Instead of dropping the learning rate in steps, SGDR [54] lowers it along half a cosine:

$$
\eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})\Big(1 + \cos\big(\pi\, T_{\text{cur}} / T_i\big)\Big),
$$

where $\eta_{\max}$ and $\eta_{\min}$ bound the learning rate, $T_{\text{cur}}$ counts the epochs since the last restart and $T_i$ is the length of the current cycle, so $\eta_t$ falls smoothly from $\eta_{\max}$ to $\eta_{\min}$ [54]. YOLOv4's column CA ("altering the learning rate during sinusoid training") gave 38.7% AP alone against 38.0% for the step schedule [27].

**Genetic hyper-parameter search (GA).** A genetic algorithm keeps a population of hyper-parameter settings, trains briefly with each, keeps the best (the "fittest"), and creates new settings by mutating and combining them. The paper uses it "for selecting the optimal hyperparameters during network training on the first 10% of time periods" [27]. The search ran YOLOv3-SPP with the GIoU loss for 300 epochs on a 5,000-image "min-val" set and selected a learning rate of 0.00261, momentum 0.949, the assignment IoU threshold 0.213 and the box-loss normalizer 0.07 [27]. Alone, GA gave 38.9% AP against 38.0% [27].

**Random training shapes and dynamic mini-batch size.** Like [YOLOv2](#yolov2-and-yolo9000-anchors-priors-and-joint-training)'s multi-scale training [8], YOLOv4 changes the input resolution during training ("random training shapes") so that one model works at several resolutions [27]. "Dynamic mini-batch size" (DM) automatically increases the mini-batch size when the random shape is small, so that the GPU is used fully; alone it lowered AP to 35.3% [27].

**Optimized anchors (OA).** The anchors are re-estimated for the 512 × 512 training resolution, in the spirit of YOLOv2's k-means "dimension clusters" (explained in the [DLCV 03 Supplementary notes](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/notes/#anchor-shapes-by-k-means-clustering-with-an-iou-distance)) [27].

### The complete recipe

**Plain version.** Putting the pieces together, YOLOv4 is CSPDarknet53 + SPP + PAN + YOLOv3 head, trained with a long list of freebies and fitted with a few cheap specials.

Section 3.4 of the paper lists the final model as follows [27]:

| Part | What YOLOv4 uses | Kind |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backbone | CSPDarknet53 | architecture |
| Neck | SPP, PAN (modified: concatenation) | architecture |
| Head | YOLOv3 (anchor-based, 3 scales × 3 anchors) | architecture |
| Backbone, freebies | CutMix and Mosaic, DropBlock, class label smoothing | training only |
| Backbone, specials | Mish, cross-stage partial connections, MiWRC | small inference cost |
| Detector, freebies | CIoU loss, CmBN, DropBlock, Mosaic, self-adversarial training, grid-sensitivity elimination, multiple anchors per ground truth, cosine annealing, genetically searched hyper-parameters, random training shapes | training only |
| Detector, specials | Mish, SPP block, modified SAM, modified PAN, DIoU-NMS | small inference cost |

Later detectors kept the overall shape of this recipe (a CSP-style backbone, an FPN–PAN neck, Mosaic, IoU-based losses, many positives per object) and replaced specific items; [Problems](#problems) maps those changes to the components above.

## Results

**Plain version.** On COCO, YOLOv4 reached 43.5% AP at 608 × 608. Its 416-pixel model exceeded 30 FPS on all three tested GPU generations; the 608-pixel model reached only 23 FPS on Maxwell. The useful result is a measured speed–accuracy trade-off, not real-time operation for every resolution and GPU.

### Training and evaluation setup

All detector experiments train on COCO [55] and report accuracy on test-dev 2017 with the COCO AP metrics [56] (AP averaged over IoU thresholds 0.50 to 0.95, plus AP50, AP75 and AP for small, medium and large objects) [27]. The classifier experiments train on ImageNet [30] and report ILSVRC 2012 validation accuracy [27].

The detector's default schedule is 500,500 training steps with a step-decay learning rate starting at 0.01 and multiplied by 0.1 at steps 400,000 and 450,000; momentum 0.9 and weight decay 0.0005; one GPU, multi-scale training, a batch size of 64 and a mini-batch size of 8 or 4 depending on memory [27]. The classifier's default is 8,000,000 steps, batch 128 with mini-batch 32, a polynomial-decay learning rate starting at 0.1 with 1,000 warm-up steps, momentum 0.9 and weight decay 0.005; the BoF classifier experiments train 50% longer [27]. Everything runs on a single 1080 Ti or 2080 Ti [27].

### The main comparison

Tables 8, 9 and 10 of the paper compare YOLOv4 with other detectors on COCO test-dev, one table per GPU architecture: Maxwell (GTX Titan X or Tesla M40), Pascal (Titan X Pascal, Titan Xp, GTX 1080 Ti or Tesla P100) and Volta (Titan Volta or Tesla V100), all "with batch=1 without using tensorRT" [27]. YOLOv4's own rows are identical in accuracy across the three tables, because only the speed is re-measured:

| Input | AP | AP50 | AP75 | APS | APM | APL | FPS Maxwell | FPS Pascal | FPS Volta |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 416 | 41.2 | 62.8 | 44.3 | 20.4 | 44.4 | 56.0 | 38 | 54 | 96 |
| 512 | 43.0 | 64.9 | 46.5 | 24.3 | 46.1 | 55.2 | 31 | 43 | 83 |
| 608 | 43.5 | 65.7 | 47.3 | 26.7 | 46.7 | 53.3 | 23 | 33 | 62 |

How to read it against the other rows:

- **Against YOLOv3** (Table 8, Maxwell): YOLOv3-608 reaches 33.0% AP and YOLOv3-SPP-608 36.2% AP, both at 20 FPS, so YOLOv4-608 is about 10 AP points more accurate at a slightly higher frame rate on the same GPU class [27].
- **Against EfficientDet** (Table 10, Volta): EfficientDet-D0 to D3 reach 33.8%, 39.6%, 43.0% and 45.8% AP at 62.5, 50.0, 41.7 and 23.8 FPS. YOLOv4-512 matches D2's 43.0% AP at 83 FPS instead of 41.7; D3 is more accurate (45.8%) but runs at 23.8 FPS, below the 30 FPS line [27]. This is the basis of the paper's claim that YOLOv4 runs about twice as fast as EfficientDet at comparable accuracy.
- **Against the slower, more accurate detectors**: several two-stage and keypoint-based detectors in Table 9 reach higher AP (the highest, 46.8%, runs at 1.3 FPS), but none of them is real-time [27]. YOLOv4 does not claim the highest AP overall, only the best accuracy among detectors at 30 FPS or more.

Two details deserve attention. First, the large-object AP *falls* as the input grows (56.0 → 55.2 → 53.3), while small-object AP rises from 20.4 to 26.7; a larger input helps small objects but makes large objects relatively larger than the receptive field and the anchors were tuned for. Second, the abstract quotes "∼65 FPS on Tesla V100" for the 43.5% AP model, while Table 10 lists 62 FPS for it [27]; the table is the more specific source.

### Speed: a measurement checklist

When you measure, or read, a latency number for any detector, check the following; each item is a condition YOLOv4's tables fix, leave open, or vary.

1. **Hardware, engine and precision** are stated and the same for every model being compared. YOLOv4 states the GPU family and "without TensorRT", but not the precision.
2. **Batch size 1** for latency; throughput numbers with large batches do not transfer to a live camera.
3. **End to end**: pre-processing, the network and post-processing including NMS, or a clear statement of what is excluded. YOLOv4 does not state whether NMS is timed; its successors' end-to-end benchmarks add NMS explicitly (see [RT-DETR](#rt-detr-a-real-time-detr) and [YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments)), averaged over the COCO validation images because NMS time depends on the image.
4. **Same device, not only the same architecture family**: YOLOv4's tables group cards of one GPU generation (for example a Titan X Maxwell and a Tesla M40), and the speeds of other methods come from their own papers, so small FPS differences between rows are not measurements on identical hardware.
5. **Warm-up and averaging**: the first iterations of any engine are slow (memory allocation, kernel selection), so discard them and average over many images.
6. **Input size and confidence threshold** used during timing; for NMS-based detectors, the threshold used for computing AP (often very low) also changes the post-processing time.

## Ablations

**Plain version.** The paper's experiments are a long series of "try one change, keep it if it helps" tables: first for the backbone as a classifier, then for the detector's freebies and specials, then for the backbone choice and the mini-batch size. Reading them carefully shows which components really matter and which were kept on weaker evidence.

- **Mosaic is the strongest single freebie** (+1.1 AP alone), followed by GA, cosine annealing and CmBN, which each add a few tenths.

- **The best rows do not contain LS, CBN, CA or DM**, although Section 3.4 lists label smoothing, CmBN and cosine annealing in YOLOv4's detector freebies. The table never shows a row with all of the final freebies together, so their joint effect on the detector is not measured here.

### Detector bag of specials

Table 5 adds modules to CSPResNeXt50-PANet-SPP at 512 × 512 [27]: the base with SPP and PAN reaches 42.4% AP; adding SAM gives 42.7% (AP50 64.6, AP75 46.3); the other combinations it tests (a receptive-field block, a Gaussian box-uncertainty head on top of SAM, and an adaptive feature-fusion block with the receptive-field block) all lower AP, to between 41.1% and 41.8% [27]. What it proves: SPP, PAN and SAM are the useful specials for this detector; adding more modules is not free. DIoU-NMS, Mish in the detector and MiWRC have no row.

### Backbones and pre-trained weights

Table 6 trains the detector with different classifier weights for initialization, all at 512 × 512 with otherwise similar settings [27]:

| Detector backbone (initialized from) | AP | AP50 | AP75 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CSPResNeXt50 (default classifier) | 42.4 | 64.4 | 45.9 |
| CSPResNeXt50 (classifier with BoF) | 42.3 | 64.3 | 45.7 |
| CSPResNeXt50 (classifier with BoF + Mish) | 42.3 | 64.2 | 45.8 |
| CSPDarknet53 (classifier with BoF) | 42.4 | 64.5 | 46.0 |
| CSPDarknet53 (classifier with BoF + Mish) | 43.0 | 64.9 | 46.5 |

What it proves: for CSPResNeXt50, a better classifier (79.8% instead of 77.9% top-1) does *not* give a better detector; for CSPDarknet53, the better classifier does. This is the experimental basis for the backbone choice, and the general lesson that ImageNet accuracy is a poor proxy for detection quality.

### Mini-batch size

Table 7 trains with mini-batches of 4 and 8 images [27]. Without the freebies and specials, CSPResNeXt50-PANet-SPP at 608 reaches 37.1% AP with mini-batch 4 and 38.4% with mini-batch 8; with them, CSPDarknet53-PANet-SPP at 512 reaches 41.6% and 41.7%. What it proves, within this setting: after the full recipe the mini-batch size "has almost no effect", which supports the claim that the detector can be trained well on one conventional GPU. Mosaic's four-images-per-sample statistics and CmBN are the components designed for this; the table does not separate their contributions.

## Conclusion

YOLOv4 shows that a classic one-stage, anchor-based detector, given a stronger backbone and neck and a carefully tested set of training tricks, can match much slower detectors in accuracy while running in real time, and can be trained on a single consumer GPU. Its experiments establish that Mosaic, IoU-type box losses, genetic hyper-parameter search, more positives per object, SPP, PAN and point-wise SAM help such a detector, that some popular tricks do not, and that the best classifier backbone is not automatically the best detector backbone. The paper's lasting contribution is less any single module than the vocabulary (backbone, neck, head; freebies and specials) and the practice of testing training tricks on the detector itself.

## Problems

**Plain version.** YOLOv4 kept the parts that later detectors found most limiting: anchors, a shared head for classes and boxes, many positives per object and therefore NMS. Its evidence also has gaps: several listed components are never tested on the detector, the ablations are single runs on the test set, and the paper was never peer reviewed.

### Limits of the method

- **Anchors are dataset-specific.** The nine anchors are clustered for COCO (and re-optimized for one input size); a new dataset or resolution needs new anchors, and an object whose shape matches no anchor is learned poorly. [YOLOX](#yolox-anchor-free-yolo-with-simota) argues exactly this when it drops anchors.
- **A coupled head.** One set of convolutions predicts classes and boxes together. [YOLOX](#yolox-anchor-free-yolo-with-simota) shows that separating them speeds up convergence.
- **Fixed-rule assignment.** Positives are chosen by anchor shape and object centre, not by how well the network currently predicts; later work assigns positives from the network's own predictions (SimOTA in YOLOX, task-aligned assignment in [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector)).
- **NMS dependency.** Several positives per object make NMS necessary, with its threshold to tune, its cost that grows with the scene (Figure 2) and its failure on neighbouring objects; DIoU-NMS only narrows that failure. The NMS-free line starts with [YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments).
- **Large objects at high resolution.** APL drops from 56.0 at 416 to 53.3 at 608 in the paper's own results [27].

### Limits of the evidence

- **Not peer reviewed.** YOLOv4 exists as an arXiv preprint [27]; it was never published at a reviewed venue (unlike [YOLOv7](#yolov7-a-trainable-bag-of-freebies), by two of the same authors).
- **Ablations on test-dev, single runs.** The detector ablations report test-dev results, a split intended for final evaluation, and give single numbers without variance; differences of a few tenths of AP may be within run-to-run variation.
- **Untested components.** Self-adversarial training, DropBlock in the detector, DIoU-NMS, MiWRC and the PAN modification have no ablation row, and no row combines all the final freebies. The final recipe therefore includes components whose effect on this detector is asserted rather than measured.
- **Speed conditions.** Numeric precision and the treatment of NMS are not stated, and competitor speeds come from other papers on the same GPU generation, not the same card. The abstract's "∼65 FPS" and Table 10's 62 FPS disagree slightly.

### The paper and the released configuration

The configuration file `cfg/yolov4.cfg` in the authors' repository (as accessed for this chapter) differs from the paper's list in places [37]: it uses a step learning-rate schedule (`policy=steps`, steps 400,000 and 450,000) rather than cosine annealing, greedy NMS (`nms_kind=greedynms`) rather than DIoU-NMS, and contains no SAM or DropBlock layer, while it does use Mosaic, the CIoU loss, the grid-sensitivity factors and the 0.213 assignment threshold. Its learning rate is 0.0013 rather than the 0.00261 found by the genetic search. A repository file can change after publication, so this does not show what was used for the paper's tables; it shows that "YOLOv4" in practice may not include every item of Section 3.4, which matters when you compare reproductions.

### Export, reduced precision and re-parameterization

Training frameworks are not deployment engines. A trained detector is usually **exported** to an exchange format such as ONNX [57] and compiled by an inference engine for the target device, for example TensorRT for NVIDIA GPUs [29]. Three things change on the way:

- **Precision.** FP16 uses half the storage per represented value compared with FP32; the speed and accuracy changes depend on the graph and runtime. INT8 quantization represents selected weights and activations with 8-bit integers and scale parameters [58]. Calibration or quantization-aware training is needed. Validate all output stages and check AP at strict IoU thresholds after quantizing, not only AP50. [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector) reports that re-parameterized models are harder to quantize and changes its training to fix this [16].
- **Graph simplification.** Batch normalization is folded into the preceding convolution, and re-parameterized multi-branch blocks (used from YOLOv6 and YOLOv7 on) are folded into single convolutions; the folding is derived in the Supplementary notes.
- **Unsupported operators and post-processing.** Every operator must exist in the target engine. Mish, for example, is a less common operator than a rectifier. [RT-DETRv2](#rt-detrv2-a-bag-of-freebies-for-rt-detr) adds a discrete sampling operator for this reason [22], and [YOLO26](#yolo26-a-dfl-free-end-to-end-yolo) cites simpler export as one reason for removing its distribution-based box head [20]. Removing NMS can simplify one part of export, but decoding, score selection and coordinate restoration still have to be implemented and timed.

*Optional: [Folding a re-parameterized block into one convolution](#folding-a-re-parameterized-block-into-one-convolution) (in the Supplementary notes). The batch-norm folding formula and a NumPy check that three branches become one convolution with the same output.*

### What later one-stage detectors changed

Each change below replaces or extends one YOLOv4 component; the linked paper guides explain the method in full.

| YOLOv4 component | What came later | Paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anchor-based coupled head | Anchor-free decoupled head, one prediction per location | [YOLOX](#yolox-anchor-free-yolo-with-simota) |
| Fixed-rule positives (IoU of shapes > 0.213) | Prediction-aware assignment: SimOTA, then task-aligned assignment | [YOLOX](#yolox-anchor-free-yolo-with-simota), [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector) |
| CSPDarknet53 blocks | Re-parameterizable blocks folded into plain convolutions for inference | [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector), [YOLOv7](#yolov7-a-trainable-bag-of-freebies) |
| Training only the final head | Auxiliary heads and branches that supervise the network during training only | [YOLOv7](#yolov7-a-trainable-bag-of-freebies), [YOLOv9](#yolov9-programmable-gradient-information) |
| Several positives + NMS (DIoU-NMS) | A one-to-one head trained alongside a one-to-many head; no NMS | [YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments), [YOLO26](#yolo26-a-dfl-free-end-to-end-yolo) |
| SAM, a cheap spatial gate | Real self-attention made affordable (area attention); hypergraph correlation | [YOLOv12](#yolov12-an-attention-centric-yolo), [YOLOv13](#yolov13-hypergraph-correlation) |
| CIoU on four box numbers | Distribution-based box sides (DFL), later removed again | [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector), [YOLO26](#yolo26-a-dfl-free-end-to-end-yolo) |
| SGD with step or cosine schedule | MuSGD, a hybrid of SGD and the Muon optimizer | [YOLO26](#yolo26-a-dfl-free-end-to-end-yolo) |

What stayed: the CSP idea in backbones, an FPN-plus-PAN neck, Mosaic, IoU-type box losses and the habit of measuring speed on real hardware.

### Beyond one-stage: real-time DETRs

**Plain version.** DETR showed that a transformer can output the final set of boxes directly, with one prediction per object and no NMS, but it was slow to train and slow to run. Real-time DETRs keep the direct-set idea and redesign the expensive parts; they are not one-stage dense detectors, but since 2023 they compete with YOLOs at the same speed.

DETR [59] and its descendants are introduced in [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#set-prediction-with-transformers-detr-and-its-descendants). The real-time line that started with RT-DETR differs from YOLOv4 in exactly the component that YOLOv4 left untouched: it trains with one-to-one (Hungarian [60]) matching, so each object has a single prediction and no NMS is needed. Each paper has its own introduction in the Paper guides:

| Paper | What it changes |
| --- | --- |
| [RT-DETR](#rt-detr-a-real-time-detr) | A hybrid encoder (attention on the coarsest level only, convolutional fusion across levels), uncertainty-minimal query selection, adjustable decoder depth, and an end-to-end speed benchmark that times NMS for YOLOs |
| [RT-DETRv2](#rt-detrv2-a-bag-of-freebies-for-rt-detr) | Per-scale sampling points, an optional discrete sampling operator for deployment, dynamic augmentation and scale-specific learning rates |
| [D-FINE](#d-fine-boxes-as-refined-distributions) | Box edges as probability distributions refined decoder layer by layer, with self-distillation from the last layer |
| [DEIM](#deim-dense-one-to-one-matching) | More positives under one-to-one matching through Mosaic and MixUp, and a matchability-aware classification loss |
| [RF-DETR](#rf-detr-architecture-search-on-a-dinov2-backbone) | A DINOv2 backbone and weight-sharing architecture search that yields many speed–accuracy trade-offs without retraining |
| [DEIMv2](#deimv2-dinov3-features-for-real-time-detrs) | DINOv3 features turned into multi-scale features by a light adapter, plus pruned convolutional backbones for the smallest models |

## Modern view

### What the surveys say

**A Comprehensive Review of YOLO Architectures in Computer Vision: From YOLOv1 to YOLOv8 and YOLO-NAS** (Terven, Córdova-Esparza and Romero-González, *Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction*, 2023) [61] walks through 16 versions in order, starting from the metrics and post-processing, then the architecture and training tricks of each. Its discussion organizes the history by four axes: anchors (absent in YOLO v1, added in YOLOv2, removed again from YOLOX on), framework (Darknet, then PyTorch), backbone (Darknet, then cross-stage partial connections, re-parameterization and architecture search), and the constant speed–accuracy trade-off. It expects COCO to be replaced by harder benchmarks and YOLO models to expand into tracking and multimodal tasks.

**YOLO Evolution: A Comprehensive Benchmark and Architectural Review of YOLOv12, YOLO11, and Their Previous Versions** (Jegham, Koh, Abdelatti and Hendawi, arXiv, 2024, revised 2025) [62] is an experimental comparison rather than a history: it evaluates versions from YOLOv3 to YOLOv12 on datasets chosen for varying object sizes, aspect ratios and small single-class objects, and reports precision, recall, mAP, processing time, GFLOPs and model size. Its takeaway is that no version wins everywhere: in its experiments YOLO11 gave the best overall balance, YOLOv10 was fast but weaker on overlapping objects, and YOLOv12's added complexity did not pay off. Treat these as one group's results on its datasets, not general rankings.

**Ultralytics YOLO Evolution: An Overview of YOLO27, YOLO26, YOLO11, YOLOv8, and YOLOv5** (Sapkota and Karkee, arXiv, 2025, revised 2026) [63] reviews only the Ultralytics releases, covering architecture, benchmarking, deployment (export and quantization) and applications, and lists open challenges: dense scenes, combining CNNs with transformers, open-vocabulary perception, domain generalization and hardware-aware optimization. It is a third-party review, not an Ultralytics publication.

**Object Detection with Transformers: A Review** (Shehzadi et al., *Sensors*, 2025) [64] covers the DETR side: improvements to DETR grouped by backbone, query design and attention mechanism, which is the context for the real-time DETRs.

### The state of the art, 2023–2026

- **NMS-free is becoming the default.** [YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments) [17] introduced it to YOLOs in 2024; [YOLO26](#yolo26-a-dfl-free-end-to-end-yolo) [20] ships it as the default deployment path; the real-time DETRs discussed here omit NMS at inference. The announced YOLO27 describes query-based decoding without NMS for its larger models [65].
- **Attention is affordable.** [YOLOv12](#yolov12-an-attention-centric-yolo) [18] and [YOLOv13](#yolov13-hypergraph-correlation) [19] put attention-like modules inside YOLOs, where YOLOv4 could only afford a cheap spatial gate; [RT-DETR](#rt-detr-a-real-time-detr) [21] restricts attention to the coarsest level.
- **Pretrained vision transformers as backbones.** [RF-DETR](#rf-detr-architecture-search-on-a-dinov2-backbone) [25] and [DEIMv2](#deimv2-dinov3-features-for-real-time-detrs) [26] start from self-supervised backbones (DINOv2 [66], DINOv3 [67]), whose benefits must be checked separately for accuracy, transfer and latency.
- **Training efficiency.** [DEIM](#deim-dense-one-to-one-matching) [24] and YOLO26 (with a new optimizer and a progressive loss) target shorter training rather than only higher AP, a direct descendant of YOLOv4's single-GPU goal.

### Open problems

1. **Fair speed comparison.** There is no shared, maintained end-to-end benchmark across devices; most tables still mix conditions, and [RF-DETR](#rf-detr-architecture-search-on-a-dinov2-backbone) shows that even GPU power throttling changes reported latencies.
2. **Small and crowded objects** remain the main accuracy gap at real-time input sizes; YOLO26's small-object assignment and DEIMv2's unchanged small-object AP both illustrate it.
3. **Quantization-friendly heads.** Score and box outputs lose accuracy at INT8, and attention layers complicate low-precision deployment.
4. **Peer review and reproducibility.** YOLOv4 itself and several widely deployed successors have no peer-reviewed paper, released code can differ from the paper, and version numbers are claimed freely; claims need independent checks.
5. **Beyond closed sets.** Bringing open-vocabulary detection to the same latency as closed-set YOLOs without losing accuracy.

## Key takeaways

- Real-time performance is an end-to-end latency budget on a stated device, not an isolated FPS value.
- YOLOv4 combines CSPDarknet53, SPP and a modified PANet with the YOLOv3 head.
- Distinguish training-only techniques from inference components when interpreting cost.
- Ablations support some components more directly than others; isolated gains and combined gains can differ.
- Classifier accuracy and operation counts do not determine detector accuracy or measured speed.
- Later detectors revisit heads, assignment, matching and feature fusion; compare complete training and inference settings.

### Folding a re-parameterized block into one convolution

**Plain version.** A block with three parallel branches (a $3 \times 3$ convolution, a $1 \times 1$ convolution and a shortcut) trains well but runs slowly. Because every branch is linear until the activation, the three can be added into a single $3 \times 3$ convolution after training, and the network computes exactly the same function, faster.

At inference, batch normalization [51] is an affine map per channel. For a bias-free convolution with kernel $W$ followed by batch normalization with learned scale $\gamma$, shift $\beta$, and running mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$,

$$
\text{BN}(W * x) = \gamma\,\frac{W * x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} + \beta = W' * x + b', \qquad W' = \frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}}\,W, \quad b' = \beta - \frac{\gamma\,\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}},
$$

where $*$ is convolution, $\epsilon$ a small constant, and all operations are per output channel. A $1 \times 1$ kernel is a $3 \times 3$ kernel that is zero except at its centre, and the identity is a $1 \times 1$ kernel equal to the identity matrix across channels. Once each branch is written as a $3 \times 3$ kernel with a bias, the sum of branches is one convolution with the summed kernel and summed bias. This is the structural re-parameterization of RepVGG [68].

```python
import numpy as np

def conv(x, w, b):
    """'Same' convolution (cross-correlation). x: (C, H, W), w: (O, C, k, k), b: (O,)."""
    k = w.shape[-1]; p = k // 2
    xp = np.pad(x, ((0, 0), (p, p), (p, p)))
    H, W = x.shape[1:]
    out = sum(np.einsum("oc,chw->ohw", w[:, :, i, j], xp[:, i:i + H, j:j + W])
              for i in range(k) for j in range(k))
    return out + b[:, None, None]

def fold_bn(w, gamma, beta, mu, var, eps=1e-5):
    """A bias-free conv followed by inference-mode batch norm -> one conv with a bias."""
    scale = gamma / np.sqrt(var + eps)
    return w * scale[:, None, None, None], beta - mu * scale

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
C = 4                                                   # channels in = channels out
x = rng.normal(size=(C, 10, 10))
bn = lambda: (rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, C), rng.normal(size=C), rng.normal(size=C), rng.uniform(0.5, 2, C))
w3, w1 = rng.normal(size=(C, C, 3, 3)), rng.normal(size=(C, C, 1, 1))
bn3, bn1, bn_id = bn(), bn(), bn()

def bn_apply(z, g, b, mu, var, eps=1e-5):
    return g[:, None, None] * (z - mu[:, None, None]) / np.sqrt(var[:, None, None] + eps) + b[:, None, None]

# training-time block: 3x3 branch + 1x1 branch + identity branch, each with its own batch norm
y_train = (bn_apply(conv(x, w3, np.zeros(C)), *bn3) + bn_apply(conv(x, w1, np.zeros(C)), *bn1)
           + bn_apply(x, *bn_id))

# deployment: express every branch as a 3x3 kernel, fold its batch norm, then add the kernels
w1_as3 = np.pad(w1, ((0, 0), (0, 0), (1, 1), (1, 1)))  # 1x1 kernel in the centre of a 3x3 one
w_id = np.zeros((C, C, 3, 3)); w_id[np.arange(C), np.arange(C), 1, 1] = 1.0
folded = [fold_bn(w, *p) for w, p in [(w3, bn3), (w1_as3, bn1), (w_id, bn_id)]]
w_dep, b_dep = sum(f[0] for f in folded), sum(f[1] for f in folded)
y_dep = conv(x, w_dep, b_dep)
print("same output:", np.allclose(y_train, y_dep))
print("branches at training: 3 | convolutions at inference: 1")
```

```text
same output: True
branches at training: 3 | convolutions at inference: 1
```

The folding is exact only for inference-mode batch normalization (fixed running statistics) and only before the nonlinearity. The YOLOv7 paper's "planned" re-parameterization exists because some combinations, such as an identity branch inside a block that already has a residual connection, hurt accuracy [1], and YOLOv6 reports that folded models can be harder to quantize [16].

### YOLO v1: detection as a single regression

*Paper:* J. Redmon, S. Divvala, R. Girshick and A. Farhadi, "You only look once: Unified, real-time object detection," CVPR 2016 [7]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the detector anatomy](#the-detector-anatomy-input-backbone-neck-and-head).

#### Challenge

In 2015 the accurate detectors were pipelines: a proposal method suggested about 2,000 candidate regions, a convolutional network classified each, and further stages refined boxes and removed duplicates. Each stage had to be tuned separately, and the systems were slow; the paper notes that R-CNN took more than 40 seconds per image at test time, and that among the GPU detectors of the time only a fast deformable-parts implementation ran at 30 FPS or more [7]. Fast R-CNN [69] and Faster R-CNN [70] made the pipeline faster but not real-time. The challenge was a detector that is real-time by design, trained end to end on the detection objective.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolov1Source} alt="Follow one image through convolutional features and the final grid prediction; this original architecture is not interchangeable with later multi-scale YOLO heads." caption="Original paper figure 3 — Follow one image through convolutional features and the final grid prediction; this original architecture is not interchangeable with later multi-scale YOLO heads." />

*Figure source: [7], arXiv:1506.02640v5, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.02640).*

**One grid, one tensor.** The image is resized to $448 \times 448$ and divided into an $S \times S$ grid. The cell that contains an object's centre is responsible for that object. Each cell predicts $B$ boxes, each with five numbers $(x, y, w, h, \text{confidence})$, and one set of $C$ conditional class probabilities $\Pr(\text{Class}_i \mid \text{Object})$, so the whole output is a tensor of shape

$$
S \times S \times (5B + C),
$$

which for PASCAL VOC [71] ($S = 7$, $B = 2$, $C = 20$) is $7 \times 7 \times 30$ [7]. The box centre $(x, y)$ is an offset within the cell, between 0 and 1; $w$ and $h$ are relative to the whole image. The confidence is trained toward $\Pr(\text{Object}) \cdot \text{IoU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}}$, zero when no object is present. At test time the class-specific score of each box is

$$
\Pr(\text{Class}_i \mid \text{Object}) \cdot \Pr(\text{Object}) \cdot \text{IoU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}} = \Pr(\text{Class}_i) \cdot \text{IoU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}},
$$

which encodes both the class probability and how well the box fits [7]. The network therefore makes only $7 \times 7 \times 2 = 98$ box predictions per image, against about 2,000 proposals for a selective-search pipeline.

**Network.** 24 convolutional layers (inspired by GoogLeNet, with $1 \times 1$ reduction layers followed by $3 \times 3$ convolutions) and 2 fully connected layers; "Fast YOLO" uses 9 convolutional layers with fewer filters [7]. The first 20 convolutional layers are pre-trained on ImageNet [30] at $224 \times 224$ (about a week of training, 88% single-crop top-5 accuracy); four convolutional and two fully connected layers are added for detection and the input resolution is doubled to $448 \times 448$. All layers except the last use the leaky rectifier $\phi(x) = x$ for $x \gt 0$ and $0.1x$ otherwise; the last layer is linear [7].

**Loss.** The paper optimizes a sum of squared errors with two corrections. Most cells contain no object, so their confidence errors would overwhelm the others: the coordinate terms are weighted by $\lambda_{\text{coord}} = 5$ and the no-object confidence terms by $\lambda_{\text{noobj}} = 0.5$. Errors in large boxes should matter less than in small ones, so the network predicts $\sqrt{w}$ and $\sqrt{h}$. The loss is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L} ={}& \lambda_{\text{coord}} \sum_{i=0}^{S^2} \sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij} \Big[ (x_i - \hat{x}_i)^2 + (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \big(\sqrt{w_i} - \sqrt{\hat{w}_i}\big)^2 + \big(\sqrt{h_i} - \sqrt{\hat{h}_i}\big)^2 \Big] \\
&+ \sum_{i=0}^{S^2} \sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij} (C_i - \hat{C}_i)^2 + \lambda_{\text{noobj}} \sum_{i=0}^{S^2} \sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{noobj}}_{ij} (C_i - \hat{C}_i)^2 + \sum_{i=0}^{S^2} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{i} \sum_{c} \big(p_i(c) - \hat{p}_i(c)\big)^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $i$ indexes cells and $j$ the boxes of a cell, $\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{i}$ is 1 if an object appears in cell $i$, $\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}$ is 1 if box predictor $j$ of cell $i$ is "responsible" for that object (the predictor with the highest current IoU with it), $\mathbb{1}^{\text{noobj}}_{ij}$ is its complement, hatted symbols are predictions and unhatted ones targets, $C$ is the confidence and $p_i(c)$ the class probability [7]. Because only the responsible predictor is trained on each object, the two predictors of a cell specialize in different sizes and shapes; this is the first YOLO label-assignment rule.

**Training.** About 135 epochs on VOC 2007 and 2012 train and validation data, batch 64, momentum 0.9, weight decay 0.0005; the learning rate is raised from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ over the first epochs, kept for 75 epochs, then $10^{-3}$ for 30 and $10^{-4}$ for 30. Dropout [48] with rate 0.5 after the first fully connected layer, random scaling and translations of up to 20% of the image size, and random exposure and saturation changes of up to a factor 1.5 in HSV space [7]. Greedy NMS is still applied at test time; the paper reports that it adds 2–3% mAP.

#### Results

On PASCAL VOC 2007 test (trained on 2007+2012), YOLO reaches 63.4% mAP at 45 FPS and Fast YOLO 52.7% mAP at 155 FPS, on a Titan X GPU without batching; the real-time deformable-parts baselines reach 26.1% at 30 FPS and 16.0% at 100 FPS [7]. Slower detectors are more accurate: Fast R-CNN 70.0% at 0.5 FPS, Faster R-CNN with VGG-16 73.2% at 7 FPS, and a YOLO trained with a VGG-16 backbone 66.4% at 21 FPS [7]. On VOC 2012 test YOLO scores 57.9% mAP [7]. The headline is speed: more than twice the mAP of earlier real-time systems.

#### Ablations

The paper has no component-by-component ablation table; its analyses play that role. An error breakdown on VOC 2007 (following a standard diagnosis tool) shows that 19.0% of YOLO's top detections are localization errors and 4.75% background errors, against 8.6% and 13.6% for Fast R-CNN: YOLO localizes worse but mistakes background for objects about three times less often [7]. Using YOLO to rescore Fast R-CNN's detections raises Fast R-CNN from 71.8% to 75.0% mAP, which the paper attributes to these different error types rather than to simple ensembling [7]. A person-detection test on artwork shows that YOLO's accuracy drops less than that of R-CNN-style detectors when moving from photographs to paintings [7].

#### Conclusion

YOLO v1 established detection as a single regression from image to boxes and classes, trained end to end, and showed that such a detector can run at video rate with useful accuracy. Every later YOLO keeps its "look once, predict densely on a grid" structure.

#### Problems

The paper lists its own limits [7]: each cell predicts only two boxes and one class, so nearby small objects (a flock of birds) compete for one cell; boxes are learned from data with no shape prior, so unusual aspect ratios generalize poorly; coarse features after many downsampling steps limit localization; and the squared-error loss treats small and large boxes alike, despite the square-root correction. Localization was its main source of error. [YOLOv2](#yolov2-and-yolo9000-anchors-priors-and-joint-training) answered with anchors, batch normalization and a fully convolutional design; [SSD](#ssd-multi-scale-default-boxes) answered with predictions on several feature maps.

### YOLOv2 and YOLO9000: anchors, priors and joint training

*Paper:* J. Redmon and A. Farhadi, "YOLO9000: Better, faster, stronger," CVPR 2017 [8]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the YOLOv3 head and box decoding](#head-the-yolov3-head-and-box-decoding).

#### Challenge

YOLO v1 was fast but made many localization errors and had low recall compared with proposal-based methods [8]. The paper sets three goals in its title: *better* (more accurate, especially in recall and localization, without losing speed), *faster* (a cheaper backbone than VGG-16 [31]) and *stronger* (detect many more categories than detection datasets label, by also learning from classification data).

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolov2Source} alt="The cell-relative center and prior-relative width/height parameterizations constrain different parts of a predicted box." caption="Original paper figure 3 — The cell-relative center and prior-relative width/height parameterizations constrain different parts of a predicted box." />

*Figure source: [8], arXiv:1612.08242v1, PDF page 4. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1612.08242).*

The "better" part is a sequence of changes, each measured (see Ablations):

- **Batch normalization** [51] on all convolutional layers, which also allows dropout to be removed.
- **High-resolution classifier:** the backbone is fine-tuned on ImageNet at $448 \times 448$ for 10 epochs before detection training, so it adapts to the higher detection resolution.
- **Convolutional with anchor boxes:** the fully connected layers are removed, the input is $416 \times 416$ so the $32\times$-downsampled output is a $13 \times 13$ grid with a single centre cell, and boxes are predicted relative to anchors, with class and objectness predicted per anchor.
- **Dimension clusters:** instead of hand-picked anchors, k-means clustering on the training boxes finds the anchor shapes, with the distance $d(\text{box}, \text{centroid}) = 1 - \text{IoU}(\text{box}, \text{centroid})$ so that large boxes do not dominate as they would with Euclidean distance; $k = 5$ is chosen as a trade-off between recall and model complexity [8]. The k-means loop is worked through in the [DLCV 03 Supplementary notes](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/notes/#anchor-shapes-by-k-means-clustering-with-an-iou-distance).
- **Direct location prediction:** region-proposal-style offsets $x = t_x w_a - x_a$ are unconstrained, so an anchor can drift anywhere and early training is unstable. YOLOv2 instead predicts the centre relative to the cell with a sigmoid, $b_x = \sigma(t_x) + c_x$, $b_y = \sigma(t_y) + c_y$, $b_w = p_w e^{t_w}$, $b_h = p_h e^{t_h}$, and the objectness $\sigma(t_o) = \Pr(\text{object}) \cdot \text{IoU}(b, \text{object})$, where $(c_x, c_y)$ is the cell offset and $(p_w, p_h)$ the prior's size [8]. This is the formula YOLOv4 inherits and then corrects for grid sensitivity.
- **Fine-grained features:** a passthrough layer brings $26 \times 26$ features to the $13 \times 13$ output by stacking adjacent features into channels, turning a $26 \times 26 \times 512$ map into $13 \times 13 \times 2048$ and concatenating it with the output features [8].
- **Multi-scale training:** every 10 batches the network picks a new input size from $\{320, 352, \dots, 608\}$ (multiples of 32), so one set of weights works at several resolutions and offers a speed–accuracy trade-off at test time [8].

The "faster" part is **Darknet-19**, a 19-layer convolutional backbone with 5 max-pooling layers, mostly $3 \times 3$ filters with $1 \times 1$ compressions, batch normalization and global average pooling; it needs 5.58 billion operations per image and reaches 72.9% top-1 and 91.2% top-5 accuracy on ImageNet, while VGG-16's convolutional layers need 30.69 billion and YOLO v1's custom network 8.52 billion [8].

The "stronger" part is **YOLO9000**. Classification labels (ImageNet) and detection labels (COCO [55]) are not mutually exclusive ("Norfolk terrier" is also a "dog"), so a single softmax over all labels is wrong. The paper builds **WordTree**, a hierarchy derived from WordNet, and predicts a conditional probability at every node, $\Pr(\text{child} \mid \text{parent})$, with one softmax per group of siblings; the probability of a label is the product of the conditionals along its path from the root [8]. Joint training mixes detection and classification images: for a detection image the full loss is back-propagated; for a classification image only the classification loss, at and above the label's level in the tree, using the box that predicts that class with the highest probability [8]. The combined tree has 9,418 classes [8].

#### Results

On VOC 2007 test, the same trained YOLOv2 runs at different input sizes on a GTX Titan X (the original, not the Pascal model): 69.0% mAP at 91 FPS ($288 \times 288$), 76.8% at 67 FPS ($416$) and 78.6% at 40 FPS ($544$), against 73.2% at 7 FPS for Faster R-CNN with VGG-16, 76.4% at 5 FPS for Faster R-CNN with ResNet and 76.8% at 19 FPS for SSD500 [8]. On VOC 2012 test YOLOv2-544 scores 73.4% mAP, and on COCO test-dev 21.6% AP and 44.0% AP50 [8]. YOLO9000 reaches 19.7% mAP on the ImageNet detection validation set and 16.0% mAP on the 156 of its classes that have no labelled detection data at all [8].

#### Ablations

Table 2 of the paper, "the path from YOLO to YOLOv2", adds the changes one by one and reports VOC 2007 mAP: YOLO v1 63.4; + batch normalization 65.8; + high-resolution classifier 69.5; + convolutional with anchor boxes 69.2; + the new network 69.6; + dimension priors and direct location prediction 74.4; + passthrough 75.4; + multi-scale training 76.8; + high-resolution detector 78.6 [8]. Two steps barely change mAP: switching to anchor boxes keeps mAP (69.5 → 69.2) but raises recall from 81% to 88%, giving "more room to improve", and the new network cuts computation by 33% at equal mAP [8]. Table 1 shows why clustering matters: 5 clustered priors reach an average IoU of 61.0 with the closest box, as good as 9 hand-picked anchors (60.9), and 9 clustered priors reach 67.2 [8].

#### Conclusion

YOLOv2 turned YOLO into a fully convolutional, anchor-based detector with learned anchor shapes, bounded centre offsets and multi-scale training, and showed with YOLO9000 that a detector can be trained on detection and classification data together. Its decoding formula and its dimension clusters survive, almost unchanged, in YOLOv3 and YOLOv4.

#### Problems

Its single $13 \times 13$ output, even with the passthrough layer, limits small-object detection (21.6% COCO AP is low next to later detectors), the bounded sigmoid creates the grid-sensitivity problem that YOLOv4 later fixes, and YOLO9000's accuracy on classes without detection data (16.0% mAP) is far from that on detected classes. [YOLOv3](#yolov3-three-scales-and-darknet-53) moved to three output scales and a deeper backbone.

### YOLOv3: three scales and Darknet-53

*Paper:* J. Redmon and A. Farhadi, "YOLOv3: An incremental improvement," tech. report, arXiv, 2018 [9]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the YOLOv3 head and box decoding](#head-the-yolov3-head-and-box-decoding), which YOLOv4 keeps.

#### Challenge

By 2018 YOLOv2 was fast but weaker than newer detectors such as RetinaNet [11] on COCO, particularly for small objects. YOLOv3 collects "a bunch of little design changes" from other work to improve accuracy while staying fast; the report is short and informal and was written, in its own words, so that other papers could cite the changes [9].

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolov3Source} alt="Read the center offsets separately from exponential changes to prior-box width and height; this figure explains box decoding, not the entire three-scale detector." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Read the center offsets separately from exponential changes to prior-box width and height; this figure explains box decoding, not the entire three-scale detector." />

*Figure source: [9], arXiv:1804.02767v1, PDF page 2. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.02767).*

- **Box prediction.** The YOLOv2 decoding is kept ($b_x = \sigma(t_x) + c_x$, $b_w = p_w e^{t_w}$, and so on), with a sum-of-squared-error loss on the raw outputs $t$ [9]. Objectness is predicted by logistic regression: its target is 1 for the anchor that overlaps a ground-truth object more than any other anchor; anchors that are not the best but overlap an object by more than 0.5 IoU are ignored; each object is assigned to exactly one anchor, and unassigned anchors receive only an objectness loss [9].
- **Class prediction.** Independent logistic classifiers with binary cross-entropy replace the softmax, so a box can carry overlapping labels (for example "woman" and "person") [9].
- **Predictions across scales.** Boxes are predicted at three scales with an FPN-like design [33]: after the last backbone features, a few convolutions predict a tensor; then the features from two layers earlier are upsampled by 2 and concatenated with an earlier, finer feature map, and the process is repeated twice. With 3 boxes per scale and COCO's 80 classes, each scale outputs an $N \times N \times [3 \cdot (4 + 1 + 80)]$ tensor [9]. The nine anchors, from k-means on COCO, are divided evenly across the scales: $10 \times 13$, $16 \times 30$, $33 \times 23$, $30 \times 61$, $62 \times 45$, $59 \times 119$, $116 \times 90$, $156 \times 198$, $373 \times 326$ [9].
- **Darknet-53.** A new backbone of 53 convolutional layers, successive $3 \times 3$ and $1 \times 1$ convolutions with residual shortcuts [32], and stride-2 convolutions instead of pooling [9].

**A useful shape check.** With a $416\times416$ input, the three output grids have $13^2$, $26^2$ and $52^2$ cells. Three anchors at each cell give $3(13^2+26^2+52^2)=10{,}647$ candidate predictions. With $K$ classes, each prediction contains four box values, one objectness value and $K$ class values. These dense candidates are not the final detection set: objectness and class scores must be decoded and filtered, then duplicates suppressed.

Independent logistic class outputs allow more than one class to have a high score for the same box. This differs from a softmax that forces class probabilities to sum to one. It does not mean every benchmark or deployment actually permits several labels per object. The decoder and evaluation protocol still determine how class-specific detections are produced. YOLOv4 retains this head structure while changing much of the surrounding recipe.

#### Results

Darknet-53, tested at $256 \times 256$ on a Titan X, reaches 77.2% top-1 and 93.8% top-5 accuracy on ImageNet with 18.7 billion operations at 78 FPS, against 77.6% / 93.8% at 37 FPS for ResNet-152 and 77.1% / 93.7% at 53 FPS for ResNet-101 [9]. On COCO, YOLOv3 at $320 \times 320$ reaches 28.2% AP in 22 ms; at $608 \times 608$ it reaches 33.0% AP, 57.9% AP50, 34.4% AP75 and 18.3% / 35.4% / 41.9% AP for small, medium and large objects in 51 ms [9]. Its AP50 (57.9%) is close to RetinaNet's 57.5% at 198 ms, which the report summarizes as "similar performance but 3.8× faster"; times come from an M40 or Titan X [9].

#### Ablations

There is no ablation table, but the report lists "things we tried that didn't work" [9]: predicting $x, y$ offsets as multiples of the anchor size with a linear activation decreased stability; linear instead of logistic $x, y$ predictions dropped mAP by a couple of points; the focal loss dropped mAP by about 2 points, which the authors suspect is because separate objectness and conditional class predictions already handle the imbalance; and Faster R-CNN-style dual IoU thresholds for positives and negatives did not give good results.

#### Conclusion

YOLOv3 added multi-scale prediction, multi-label classification and a strong, fast residual backbone to YOLOv2's design. Its head, decoding and assignment rules are what YOLOv4 keeps as "the YOLOv3 head".

#### Problems

The report itself points out the main weakness: performance drops sharply as the IoU threshold rises from 0.5 to 0.95, so YOLOv3 "struggles to get the boxes perfectly aligned with the object"; it also notes comparatively worse medium- and large-object AP [9]. One anchor per object gives few positives, the sigmoid creates grid sensitivity, and the box loss is squared error on raw outputs. YOLOv4 addresses each: CIoU loss, several anchors per object, the grid-sensitivity factor and a stronger backbone and neck. This was the last YOLO by its original authors; later numbers came from other teams.

### SSD: multi-scale default boxes

*Paper:* W. Liu, D. Anguelov, D. Erhan, C. Szegedy, S. Reed, C.-Y. Fu and A. C. Berg, "SSD: Single shot multibox detector," ECCV 2016 [10]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the detector anatomy](#the-detector-anatomy-input-backbone-neck-and-head) (multi-scale dense prediction). [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#one-stage-detectors-yolo-ssd-and-retinanet) gives a short overview.

#### Challenge

YOLO v1 was fast but predicted from one coarse grid with no shape priors, so it was much less accurate than Faster R-CNN. SSD asks whether a single-shot detector can match proposal-based accuracy at real-time speed, by predicting from several feature maps of different resolutions with a set of default boxes at each location [10].

#### Method

<Figure src={paperSsdSource} alt="Compare SSD's predictions from several feature resolutions with the illustrated YOLO head; default-box scale and feature-map scale work together." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Compare SSD's predictions from several feature resolutions with the illustrated YOLO head; default-box scale and feature-map scale work together." />

*Figure source: [10], arXiv:1512.02325v5, PDF page 4. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.02325).*

**Multi-scale feature maps.** A truncated VGG-16 [31] base network (with its fully connected layers converted to convolutions) is followed by extra convolutional layers that shrink the feature map step by step. Small convolutional predictors are attached to six maps of decreasing resolution, from conv4_3 to the last extra layer, so early maps handle small objects and later maps large ones [10].

**Default boxes.** At each location of a map with $m$ prediction maps in total, default boxes (anchors) of scale

$$
s_k = s_{\min} + \frac{s_{\max} - s_{\min}}{m - 1}(k - 1), \qquad k \in [1, m],
$$

are placed, where $s_{\min} = 0.2$ and $s_{\max} = 0.9$ (as fractions of the image size), with aspect ratios $a_r \in \{1, 2, 3, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3}\}$, width $s_k\sqrt{a_r}$ and height $s_k/\sqrt{a_r}$, plus one extra box of scale $\sqrt{s_k s_{k+1}}$ for ratio 1: six default boxes per location [10]. For each default box the predictor outputs four offsets and $C + 1$ class scores (including background). The $300 \times 300$ model makes 8,732 predictions per class [10].

**Matching.** Each ground-truth box is matched to the default box with the highest Jaccard overlap (IoU), and then to every default box with IoU above 0.5, so several overlapping default boxes become positives [10].

**Loss.** With $x^p_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}$ indicating that default box $i$ is matched to ground truth $j$ of class $p$,

$$
\mathcal{L}(x, c, l, g) = \frac{1}{N}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\text{conf}}(x, c) + \alpha\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}(x, l, g)\big),
$$

where $N$ is the number of matched default boxes, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{conf}}$ is the softmax cross-entropy over class scores $c$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}$ is a smooth L1 loss between predicted offsets $l$ and ground-truth offsets $g$ (centre offsets normalized by the default box's size, log-ratios of width and height), and $\alpha = 1$ [10].

**Hard negative mining and augmentation.** Most default boxes are negatives; SSD keeps only the negatives with the highest confidence loss, at most three for every positive [10]. Each training image is either used whole, or a patch is sampled so that its minimum IoU with the objects is 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 or 0.9, or a random patch is sampled; patches are resized, flipped and photometrically distorted [10].

#### Results

On VOC 2007 test (trained on 2007+2012), SSD300 reaches 74.3% mAP and SSD512 76.8%, against 73.2% for Faster R-CNN with VGG-16 and 63.4% for YOLO v1 [10]. Speeds on a Titan X: SSD300 at 46 FPS with batch 1 and 59 FPS with batch 8; SSD512 at 19 and 22 FPS [10]. On COCO test-dev, SSD300 reaches 23.2% AP and SSD512 26.8% AP [10]. Note the two batch sizes: the frequently quoted 59 FPS is a batch-8 throughput.

#### Ablations

Table 2 of the paper, on SSD300 and VOC 2007: without the extensive data augmentation mAP is 65.5%; with it, but without the extra aspect ratios, 71.6%; adding the $\{\tfrac{1}{2}, 2\}$ boxes gives 73.7%; with all boxes but without the atrous (dilated) version of VGG-16, 74.2%; with everything, 74.3% [10]. Data augmentation alone is worth 8.8 points, and removing the $\{\tfrac{1}{3}, 3\}$ boxes costs 0.6 points and the $\{\tfrac{1}{2}, 2\}$ boxes another 2.1 [10]. The atrous network gives about the same accuracy and is about 20% faster [10]. Table 3 removes prediction layers while keeping a similar total number of boxes: accuracy drops monotonically from 74.3% to 62.4% as fewer layers are used, so predicting from several resolutions is a main source of SSD's accuracy [10].

#### Conclusion

SSD showed that dense, single-shot prediction from several feature maps with default boxes of several shapes can match two-stage accuracy at real-time speed, and that heavy augmentation is part of the detector. Its matching rule (best box plus all boxes above an IoU) is the template for YOLOv4's "several anchors per object".

#### Problems

The paper notes that SSD is much weaker on small objects than on large ones, and that higher input resolution helps but does not close the gap [10]. The shallow conv4_3 features used for small objects are semantically weak, the default-box design adds hyper-parameters (scales, ratios, which layers), and duplicates are removed by NMS. FPN-style necks [33] and the focal loss of [RetinaNet](#retinanet-and-the-focal-loss) addressed the weak small-object features and the foreground–background imbalance.

### RetinaNet and the focal loss

*Paper:* T.-Y. Lin, P. Goyal, R. Girshick, K. He and P. Dollár, "Focal loss for dense object detection," ICCV 2017 [11]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [box loss](#box-loss-from-iou-to-ciou) and the classification terms of the head; [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#focal-loss-and-class-imbalance) defines the focal loss.

#### Challenge

In 2017, one-stage detectors such as SSD and YOLO were faster but less accurate than two-stage detectors. The paper argues that the main reason is class imbalance during training: a dense detector evaluates about 100,000 candidate locations per image, almost all easy background, and their many small losses dominate training [11]. Two-stage detectors avoid this with proposals and sampled mini-batches (for example 1:3 positives to negatives); one-stage detectors used hard example mining [72], which discards most of the data.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperRetinanetSource} alt="The feature pyramid supplies multiple scales to shared classification and regression subnetworks; focal loss changes classification training rather than this pyramid's geometry." caption="Original paper figure 3 — The feature pyramid supplies multiple scales to shared classification and regression subnetworks; focal loss changes classification training rather than this pyramid's geometry." />

*Figure source: [11], Published paper PDF, PDF page 5. [Original PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_ICCV_2017/papers/Lin_Focal_Loss_for_ICCV_2017_paper.pdf).*

**Focal loss.** For a binary classification with predicted probability $p$ for the true class and $p_t = p$ if the label is positive and $1 - p$ otherwise,

$$
\text{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t\,(1 - p_t)^{\gamma} \log(p_t),
$$

where $\gamma \ge 0$ is the focusing parameter and $\alpha_t$ a class-balancing weight ($\alpha$ for positives, $1 - \alpha$ for negatives) [11]. With $\gamma = 0$ this is the balanced cross-entropy. The factor $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$ shrinks the loss of well-classified examples: with $\gamma = 2$, an example with $p_t = 0.9$ gets 100 times less loss than with plain cross-entropy, so training focuses on hard examples. The paper uses $\gamma = 2$ and $\alpha = 0.25$ [11].

**RetinaNet.** A ResNet [32] backbone with an FPN [33] on levels $P_3$ to $P_7$; at each level, anchors of areas $32^2$ to $512^2$ with three aspect ratios $\{1{:}2, 1{:}1, 2{:}1\}$ and three scales $\{2^0, 2^{1/3}, 2^{2/3}\}$, so $A = 9$ anchors per location [11]. Anchors with IoU at least 0.5 with a ground-truth box are foreground, those below 0.4 are background, and the rest are ignored. Two small fully convolutional subnets run on every level with shared weights across levels: a classification subnet (four $3 \times 3$ convolutions with 256 channels and ReLU, then a $3 \times 3$ convolution with $KA$ sigmoid outputs) and a class-agnostic box subnet with the same structure and $4A$ outputs [11]. The focal loss is summed over all anchors and normalized by the number of positive anchors.

**Initialization.** If all anchors start with a probability around 0.5, the many background anchors produce a huge loss at the first iterations. The bias of the final classification layer is therefore initialized to $b = -\log((1 - \pi)/\pi)$ with prior $\pi = 0.01$, so every anchor starts with a foreground probability of about 0.01 [11].

**Inference.** Per level, at most 1,000 top-scoring predictions above a confidence of 0.05 are decoded; the predictions of all levels are merged and NMS with threshold 0.5 is applied [11].

**Separate an easy negative from a hard negative.** For a background location that the model confidently classifies as background, the modulating factor makes its classification contribution small. A misclassified background location still receives a substantial gradient. The mechanism changes the relative weighting of examples; it neither removes the box-regression objective nor guarantees that the largest remaining losses correspond to useful annotations.

This distinction matters with noisy labels. A mislabeled object can remain hard indefinitely, so focusing training on hard examples may also emphasize annotation errors. The focal-loss parameters must therefore be interpreted together with the foreground/background balance and the annotation quality. At inference the loss is absent: the network still produces dense boxes and class scores, and NMS remains part of the detector. A training-only loss change can improve the deployed prediction without becoming an extra inference module.

#### Results

The best model, ResNet-101-FPN trained with scale jitter and 1.5 times longer, reaches 39.1% AP on COCO test-dev, ahead of all one-stage and two-stage detectors the paper compares with [11]. Table 1(e) gives the speed–accuracy trade-off on test-dev, timed on an Nvidia M40: ResNet-50 at 500 pixels reaches 32.5% AP in 72 ms, ResNet-101 at 800 pixels 37.8% AP in 198 ms [11].

#### Ablations

Table 1 of the paper (COCO minival, ResNet-50 at 600 pixels unless noted) [11]:

- **(a) Balanced cross-entropy:** the best $\alpha$ gives 31.1% AP ($\alpha = 0.75$).
- **(b) Focal loss:** $\gamma = 2$, $\alpha = 0.25$ gives 34.0% AP, a gain of 2.9 points with the same network; results are fairly robust to the exact values ($\gamma = 1$: 33.7%, $\gamma = 5$: 32.2%).
- **(c) Anchors:** one scale and one ratio gives 30.3% AP; three scales and three ratios 34.0%; more saturates.
- **(d) Focal loss against hard example mining** (ResNet-101): the best online hard example mining variant reaches 32.8% AP, the focal loss 36.0%.

What it proves: the loss, not the architecture, closes most of the gap to two-stage detectors.

#### Conclusion

RetinaNet showed that a dense one-stage detector can be the most accurate detector once the training loss handles the foreground–background imbalance, and it fixed the modern one-stage layout: an FPN neck and separate, shared classification and box subnets.

#### Problems

It is accurate but not real-time (198 ms for the most accurate model), its anchors and IoU thresholds are hand-designed, and NMS is still required. YOLOv3 reported that the focal loss did not help its design (see [YOLOv3](#yolov3-three-scales-and-darknet-53)), and YOLOv4 mentions the focal loss only as a way to address imbalance in its survey; quality-aware variants of the focal loss, such as the varifocal loss [73], returned in [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector) and [DEIM](#deim-dense-one-to-one-matching). [FCOS](#fcos-per-pixel-anchor-free-detection) removed the anchors.

### FCOS: per-pixel anchor-free detection

*Paper:* Z. Tian, C. Shen, H. Chen and T. He, "FCOS: Fully convolutional one-stage object detection," ICCV 2019 [12]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [label assignment](#label-assignment-several-anchors-per-object). [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#anchor-free-detectors-points-instead-of-boxes) introduces the regression targets and centre-ness.

#### Challenge

Anchor-based detectors such as RetinaNet depend on anchor hyper-parameters (sizes, aspect ratios, IoU thresholds) that must be tuned per dataset, produce a large number of anchors, most of them negative, and compute many IoUs during training [12]. FCOS asks whether a detector can work per pixel, like semantic segmentation, with no anchors at all, and still match anchor-based accuracy.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperFcosSource} alt="Each pyramid location predicts classes and box distances; centerness accompanies those predictions instead of choosing predefined anchor boxes." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Each pyramid location predicts classes and box distances; centerness accompanies those predictions instead of choosing predefined anchor boxes." />

*Figure source: [12], arXiv:1904.01355v5, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.01355).*

**Per-location regression.** Every location $(x, y)$ on a feature map (mapped back to image coordinates) that falls inside a ground-truth box is a positive and regresses the four distances to the box sides,

$$
l^* = x - x_1, \qquad t^* = y - y_1, \qquad r^* = x_2 - x, \qquad b^* = y_2 - y,
$$

where $(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2)$ is the box [12]. The network predicts these distances through $\exp(s_i x)$ with a trainable scalar $s_i$ per level, so that one shared head can serve levels of different scales [12].

**Multi-level assignment.** Five levels $P_3$ to $P_7$ (strides 8 to 128) come from an FPN [33]. Instead of assigning anchors of different sizes to levels, FCOS limits the regression range per level: a location on level $i$ is a negative if $\max(l^*, t^*, r^*, b^*) \gt m_i$ or $\lt m_{i-1}$, with $m_2, \dots, m_7 = 0, 64, 128, 256, 512, \infty$ [12]. Most overlaps are between objects of very different sizes, which then land on different levels; if a location still belongs to several boxes, the box with the smallest area is chosen [12].

**Centre-ness.** Locations far from an object's centre produce poor boxes. A one-layer branch predicts

$$
\text{centerness}^* = \sqrt{\frac{\min(l^*, r^*)}{\max(l^*, r^*)} \times \frac{\min(t^*, b^*)}{\max(t^*, b^*)}},
$$

which is 1 at the centre and decays to 0 at the box edges; it is trained with binary cross-entropy and multiplied into the class score at test time, so off-centre boxes are ranked lower before NMS [12].

**Loss.** $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{pos}}} \sum \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} + \frac{\lambda}{N_{\text{pos}}} \sum \mathbb{1}_{c^* \gt 0}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$ is the focal loss [11], $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$ the IoU loss of UnitBox [43], $N_{\text{pos}}$ the number of positives, $\mathbb{1}_{c^* \gt 0}$ selects positive locations and $\lambda = 1$ [12].

**Decode one location.** Suppose a feature location maps to image point $(40,32)$ and predicts distances $(l,t,r,b)=(12,8,20,16)$. The decoded box is $(40-12,32-8,40+20,32+16)=(28,24,60,48)$. Distances are positive, so the location lies inside its box. If targets were normalized by feature stride during training, the corresponding stride must be restored before this calculation; otherwise boxes would be systematically too small.

The word *anchor-free* removes predefined box shapes, not spatial reference locations. A feature grid still has a stride and a coordinate convention. The original FCOS design also keeps per-level regression ranges, while later centre sampling restricts positives further. These are separate decisions from predicting edge distances. Keeping assignment, parameterization and score ranking separate prevents an easy mistake: assuming every later anchor-free YOLO uses FCOS's decoder.

#### Results

On COCO test-dev with single-model, single-scale testing, FCOS with ResNet-101-FPN reaches 41.5% AP against 39.1% for RetinaNet with the same backbone, and the version with ResNeXt-64x4d-101-FPN and the post-submission improvements reaches 44.7% AP [12].

#### Ablations

On COCO minival with ResNet-50-FPN [12]: with RetinaNet's training and testing settings, FCOS already reaches 36.3% AP against RetinaNet's 35.9%; with group normalization in the heads and an NMS threshold of 0.6 it reaches 37.1%. The **centre-ness** ablation (Table 4) is decisive: without it 33.5% AP, with centre-ness computed from the predicted regression vector also 33.5%, with the separate centre-ness branch 37.1% [12]. Further "improvements" added after submission (centre-ness on the regression branch, sampling only the central part of boxes as positives, GIoU loss [44], normalizing targets by the stride) bring the minival result to 38.6% [12]. On recall, FCOS without FPN already reaches a best possible recall of 95.55%, and with FPN it is close to the best anchor-based setting; with FPN, ambiguous locations (inside several boxes) fall to a small fraction [12].

#### Conclusion

FCOS showed that anchors are not necessary: per-location distance regression, multi-level range assignment and a centre-ness score match or beat anchor-based RetinaNet with fewer hyper-parameters. The comparison helps separate anchor-free prediction from the parameterization used by a particular head. YOLOX uses centre offsets and log sizes, not FCOS's four edge distances [15], [74].

#### Problems

Its assignment is still a fixed geometric rule (inside the box, inside the level's range, optionally near the centre), it still needs NMS, and the paper's improvements list shows that much of the gain depends on training details. Prediction-aware assignment ([YOLOX](#yolox-anchor-free-yolo-with-simota), [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector)) and one-to-one heads ([YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments)) went further.

### CenterNet: objects as points

*Paper:* X. Zhou, D. Wang and P. Krähenbühl, "Objects as points," arXiv, 2019 [13]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [post-processing](#post-processing-nms-and-diou-nms). [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#anchor-free-detectors-points-instead-of-boxes) introduces the heatmap formulation. Note the name clash: the "CenterNet" in YOLOv4's comparison tables is a different detector, based on keypoint triplets, by Duan et al. [75].

#### Challenge

Most detectors enumerate many potential boxes and then remove duplicates with NMS, a post-processing step that is not differentiable and is hard to train end to end [13]. The paper asks whether an object can be represented by a single point, its centre, so that detection becomes keypoint estimation and duplicates disappear without IoU-based NMS.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperCenternetSource} alt="A center keypoint anchors an object, while predicted size and local offset recover its box; point localization is distinct from grouping arbitrary corners." caption="Original paper figure 2 — A center keypoint anchors an object, while predicted size and local offset recover its box; point localization is distinct from grouping arbitrary corners." />

*Figure source: [13], arXiv:1904.07850v2, PDF page 2. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.07850).*

**Heatmap of centres.** For an input of size $W \times H$ and output stride $R = 4$, the network predicts a keypoint heatmap $\hat{Y} \in [0, 1]^{\frac{W}{R} \times \frac{H}{R} \times C}$, one channel per class [13]. Each ground-truth centre is splatted onto the heatmap with a Gaussian whose standard deviation adapts to the object size; overlapping Gaussians of the same class take the element-wise maximum.

**Focal loss on the heatmap.** With $Y_{xyc}$ the target heatmap,

$$
\mathcal{L}_k = \frac{-1}{N} \sum_{xyc} \begin{cases} (1 - \hat{Y}_{xyc})^{\alpha} \log(\hat{Y}_{xyc}), & Y_{xyc} = 1, \\ (1 - Y_{xyc})^{\beta}\, (\hat{Y}_{xyc})^{\alpha} \log(1 - \hat{Y}_{xyc}), & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases}
$$

where $N$ is the number of objects, $\alpha = 2$ and $\beta = 4$, and the factor $(1 - Y_{xyc})^\beta$ reduces the penalty for locations near a true centre [13].

**Size and offset.** At each location the network also predicts the box size $(w, h)$ in raw pixels, trained with an L1 loss at the centre points only, and a sub-pixel offset that recovers the discretization error of the output stride, also with an L1 loss. The total loss is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}} = \mathcal{L}_k + \lambda_{\text{size}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{size}} + \lambda_{\text{off}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{off}}$ with $\lambda_{\text{size}} = 0.1$ and $\lambda_{\text{off}} = 1$ [13]. The network outputs $C + 4$ channels per location; each output has its own $3 \times 3$ convolution, ReLU and $1 \times 1$ convolution on a shared backbone.

**Decoding without NMS.** Peaks are locations whose value is greater than or equal to their 8 neighbours, which a $3 \times 3$ max pooling finds; the top 100 peaks are kept and turned into boxes from the predicted size and offset [13].

**Keep coordinate systems explicit.** If the network selects grid point $(u,v)$, predicts sub-cell offset $(o_x,o_y)$ and uses output stride $R$, its image-space centre is $(R(u+o_x),R(v+o_y))$. A predicted pixel-space size $(w,h)$ gives corners at the centre plus or minus $(w/2,h/2)$. Width and height should not be multiplied by the stride again when they are already expressed in input pixels.

Local-maximum selection is still a kind of heatmap suppression. The narrower claim is that the default decoder does not need **IoU-based box NMS**. Two nearby centres may remain separate peaks, while two same-class objects mapped to the same output location cannot be independently represented by one size/offset vector. Thus removing box NMS changes the representation and its failure modes; it does not make duplicate handling or output resolution irrelevant.

#### Results

On COCO validation, timed on a Titan Xp, CenterNet with a ResNet-18 backbone reaches 28.1% AP at 142 FPS, a mid-sized backbone 37.4% AP at 52 FPS, and the largest (a 104-layer hourglass network) 40.3% AP at 14 FPS, 42.2% with flip testing at 7.8 FPS and 45.1% with multi-scale testing at 1.4 FPS [13]. The same design extends to 3D boxes and human pose by regressing more quantities at the centre.

#### Ablations

The paper checks that IoU-based NMS is unnecessary by applying it anyway: AP rises from 39.2% to 39.7% for the mid-sized backbone with flip testing and stays at 42.2% for the largest one, so NMS is not used [13]. It also counts **centre collisions**: in COCO training data, 614 pairs of objects fall on the same centre point at stride 4, out of 860,001 objects, so fewer than 0.1% of objects cannot be represented [13]. Further ablations cover the size-loss weight ($\lambda_{\text{size}} \le 0.1$ works well) and longer training schedules.

#### Conclusion

CenterNet showed that a detector can be a keypoint estimator: one positive per object (its centre), no anchors and no IoU-based NMS, with a simple, fast and general design. It is an instructive example of geometric centre supervision before the later NMS-free YOLOs. Its fixed centre target is different from learned bipartite matching.

#### Problems

It remains an arXiv paper; objects whose centres coincide on the stride-4 grid cannot both be represented (fewer than 0.1% of COCO objects, as counted above); its size regression in raw pixels needs a hand-set loss weight; and its best accuracy uses multi-scale testing, which merges the results with NMS again [13]. Training one prediction per object directly, with a learned matching rather than a geometric one, became the route of [YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments) and the real-time DETRs.

### YOLOR: explicit features and learned implicit representations

Wang, Yeh and Liao's 2021 technical report asks whether a detector can benefit from learned representations that do not depend on the current image [14]. Its practical meaning is much narrower than the analogy to human subconscious learning in the introduction: these are ordinary trainable tensors, optimized through the task loss.

#### Challenge

A shared backbone produces image-dependent features, but different tasks and prediction channels may prefer different feature spaces. One response is to build larger task-specific branches. YOLOR instead asks whether a small, persistent learned representation can align features, refine predictions and help joint learning. This follows naturally after YOLOv4: keep a strong detector recipe and investigate how to change its representations without rebuilding the whole inference network.

The central distinction is between **explicit features**, computed from the current input, and **implicit representations**, model parameters shared across inputs. An implicit tensor is not an additional annotation, an image-specific latent inferred by another network, or evidence that a model understands a task like a person. Keeping these meanings separate makes the paper much easier to read.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolorSource} alt="Compare vector, network and factorized representations of implicit knowledge; these are learned input-independent parameters, not extra observed image features." caption="Original paper figure 7 — Compare vector, network and factorized representations of implicit knowledge; these are learned input-independent parameters, not extra observed image features." />

*Figure source: [14], arXiv:2105.04206v1, PDF page 5. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.04206).*

**Represent and combine.** Write an image-dependent feature as $F(x;\theta)$ and a learned, image-independent representation as $g(\phi)$. Here $x$ is the input image, $\theta$ are the feature extractor's parameters and $\phi$ are the implicit representation's parameters. YOLOR studies addition, multiplication and concatenation:

$$
H = F(x;\theta)+g(\phi),\qquad
H = F(x;\theta)\odot g(\phi),\qquad
H = \operatorname{concat}(F(x;\theta),g(\phi)).
$$

$H$ is the combined representation and $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication. A channel vector can be broadcast over spatial positions; concatenation requires matching the remaining dimensions. These are alternatives, not three mandatory successive layers.

**Feature alignment.** The first application adds implicit representations to feature-pyramid levels. Each level contains different spatial and semantic information. A learned offset can adjust the feature space seen by a downstream shared operation. The tensor has no spatial knowledge about a particular object; its effect must emerge through gradients accumulated across training images.

**Prediction refinement.** The second application modifies output channels. The choice of operator should respect the decoder. Centre offsets are added to grid coordinates, whereas width and height scale anchor dimensions multiplicatively. The paper therefore tests both generic operators and versions tailored to these meanings. This is more informative than treating all output channels as interchangeable numbers.

**Joint learning.** The third application combines detection with classification or embedding tasks. Implicit representations allow the common features to be adjusted for the objectives. The report demonstrates particular joint-training experiments; it does not establish that an arbitrary set of tasks can share one representation without interference.

**Parameterizations.** A directly learned vector is the simplest option. A small neural network can map an implicit code into the required representation, or matrix factorization can generate it from lower-dimensional factors. These approaches change the capacity and parameterization of the adjustment. They are not a second image encoder: the representation remains independent of the current observation.

**Possible deployment simplification.** When an additive implicit vector lies immediately before an affine $1\times1$ prediction convolution, and a multiplicative vector immediately after it, they can be absorbed into weights and bias. At a spatial position, write

$$
y = m\odot\bigl(W(x+a)+b\bigr)
  = W'x+b',\qquad
W'=\operatorname{diag}(m)W,\quad
b'=m\odot(Wa+b).
$$

$x\in\mathbb R^C$ is the local input feature, $a\in\mathbb R^C$ the additive vector, $W\in\mathbb R^{D\times C}$ the prediction matrix, $b\in\mathbb R^D$ its bias, and $m\in\mathbb R^D$ the multiplicative vector. $y$ is the output and $W',b'$ are the equivalent fused parameters. This identity requires the stated affine arrangement: intervening nonlinearities, incompatible shapes or spatial boundary effects invalidate this simple derivation. It explains why a richer training parameterization need not imply a proportionally larger deployment graph.

#### Results

Table 8 is a useful controlled starting point. On the YOLOv4-P6-light baseline at $1280\times1280$, validation AP changes from **51.4 to 51.9** after adding the tested implicit representations and to **52.5** after fine-tuning [14]. The latter step changes training as well, so the full 1.1-point difference cannot be attributed to the implicit tensors alone.

Table 9 reports the larger YOLOR model at **55.4 test AP and 30 FPS on a V100**, compared with **55.5 AP and 16 FPS** for the listed Scaled-YOLOv4 model [14]. This is a comparison of complete systems in that table, not proof that adding one tensor doubles any detector's speed. The paper also distinguishes extra pretraining, segmentation labels and additional images in competing rows; keep those columns when interpreting the result.

#### Ablations

The small experiments are more revealing than the headline result:

| Experiment | Baseline AP | Modified AP | What it tests |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Table 1: additive feature alignment | 47.8 | 47.9 | A small feature-space adjustment |
| Table 2: additive prediction refinement | 47.8 | 47.8 | Not every modification improves aggregate AP |
| Table 4: multiplicative feature alignment | 47.8 | 47.4 | Operator choice can hurt |
| Table 4: multiplicative prediction refinement | 47.8 | 48.0 | Multiplication has a different effect at the output |
| Table 5: width/height-focused refinement | 47.8 | 48.1 | Matching the operator to channel meaning |

These rows use the report's YOLOv4-CSP-fast setting at $640\times640$ [14]. Do not combine them with the different baseline in Table 3 or the larger model in Table 8. Table 6 additionally compares direct vectors, neural-network parameterization and matrix factorization. Table 7 shows that the tested implicit variants add very few parameters and operations; this supports the narrow efficiency claim for those models, rather than a universal zero-cost claim.

#### Conclusion

YOLOR's contribution is a way to adjust task representations using small learned constants and carefully chosen operators. It connects YOLOv4's feature-fusion recipe to YOLOv7's broader concern with training parameterization. Read it as an investigation of where and how to insert an adjustment, rather than a numbered replacement for every component of YOLOv4.

#### Problems

The strongest conceptual language is broader than the evidence. Detection and selected joint tasks do not demonstrate general-purpose understanding. Several ablations differ by only tenths of an AP point, without uncertainty estimates that would establish their stability across seeds. Addition, multiplication and factorization are useful engineering choices, but their benefit depends on placement, training and the baseline.

Finally, always distinguish the technical report, particular released model configurations and later implementations. A family name alone does not specify which implicit modules remain in the exported graph, which input resolution was used or how speed was measured. Those details are necessary to reproduce the claimed advantage.

### YOLOX: anchor-free YOLO with SimOTA

*Paper:* Z. Ge, S. Liu, F. Wang, Z. Li and J. Sun, "YOLOX: Exceeding YOLO series in 2021," arXiv, 2021 [15]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the YOLOv3 head and box decoding](#head-the-yolov3-head-and-box-decoding) and [label assignment](#label-assignment-several-anchors-per-object).

#### Challenge

By 2021, detection research had moved to anchor-free detectors, prediction-aware label assignment and end-to-end (NMS-free) training, but YOLOv4 and YOLOv5 [3] still used anchors and hand-crafted assignment rules [15]. The YOLOX report brings those advances into a YOLO. It deliberately starts from YOLOv3 with SPP rather than YOLOv4, because it considers YOLOv4 and YOLOv5 "a little over-optimized for the anchor-based pipeline" [15].

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYoloxOriginal} alt="Compare the coupled head with YOLOX’s separate classification and regression paths. The IoU branch belongs to the regression path; this head diagram does not specify the whole detector." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Compare the coupled head with YOLOX’s separate classification and regression paths. The IoU branch belongs to the regression path; this head diagram does not specify the whole detector." />

*Figure source: [15], arXiv:2107.08430v2, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08430v2).*

**A strong baseline.** YOLOv3 with a Darknet-53 backbone and an SPP layer, trained with EMA weight averaging, a cosine learning-rate schedule, an IoU loss with an IoU-aware branch, and BCE for the class and objectness branches; 300 epochs with 5 warm-up epochs, SGD with momentum 0.9, learning rate $0.01 \times \text{BatchSize}/64$, batch 128, inputs drawn from 448 to 832 pixels; only flips, colour jitter and multi-scale inputs for augmentation at this stage [15].

**Decoupled head.** YOLO heads were coupled: one convolution produced class scores, objectness and box numbers together. YOLOX uses a light decoupled head per FPN level: a $1 \times 1$ convolution reduces the channels to 256, followed by two parallel branches with two $3 \times 3$ convolutions each, one for classification and one for regression (with an IoU branch on the regression side) [15]. The motivation is the known conflict between classification and regression features. Figure 4 shows the resulting anatomy, which most later YOLOs share.

<Figure src={figAnatomy} alt="Block diagram. A 640 by 640 input feeds a backbone with stages C3, C4 and C5 at strides 8, 16 and 32. A neck turns them into P3, P4 and P5 with orange top-down arrows (FPN) and pink bottom-up arrows (PAN). Each level feeds a decoupled head with a class branch and a box branch, producing 80 by 80, 40 by 40 and 20 by 20 grids, which together give 8,400 candidates. A note says a one-to-many head needs threshold plus NMS, while a one-to-one head uses top-k without NMS." caption="Figure 4 — The backbone–neck–head design of anchor-free YOLOs after YOLOX, for a 640 × 640 input: an FPN–PAN neck and a decoupled head on strides 8, 16 and 32. Each cell of each grid makes one prediction, giving 8,400 candidates per image (YOLOv4 makes three per cell)." />

**Strong augmentation.** Mosaic and MixUp [46], switched off for the last 15 epochs so that training ends on images that look like test images; with this augmentation, ImageNet pre-training was "no more beneficial" and all later models are trained from scratch [15].

**Anchor-free.** Predictions per location drop from 3 to 1, and the head directly predicts two offsets from the cell's top-left corner plus the box's height and width; the centre location of each object is its positive and a scale range assigns objects to FPN levels, as in FCOS [15]. The report gives two reasons against anchors: clustered anchors are domain-specific, and they multiply the number of predictions, which can become a latency bottleneck when moving outputs between devices [15]. Unlike FCOS's four edge distances, YOLOX predicts a center offset and logarithmic width/height. For grid coordinates $(g_x,g_y)$ and stride $s$, its decoding is $c_x=(t_x+g_x)s$, $c_y=(t_y+g_y)s$, $w=e^{t_w}s$, $h=e^{t_h}s$, where $t_x,t_y,t_w,t_h$ are raw box outputs. The released head applies no sigmoid to the center offsets; objectness and class probabilities are separate [74]. The snippet uses that parameterization and multiplies objectness by class probability for filtering.

```python
import numpy as np

def make_grid(img_size=640, strides=(8, 16, 32)):
    grids, all_strides = [], []
    for stride in strides:
        size = img_size // stride
        gy, gx = np.mgrid[:size, :size]
        grids.append(np.stack([gx.ravel(), gy.ravel()], axis=1))
        all_strides.append(np.full(size*size, stride))
    return np.concatenate(grids), np.concatenate(all_strides)

def decode(raw, grid, stride, threshold=0.25):
    # raw: center offsets, log(width/stride), log(height/stride), objectness and class logits.
    center = (raw[:, :2] + grid) * stride[:, None]
    size = np.exp(raw[:, 2:4]) * stride[:, None]
    boxes = np.concatenate([center-size/2, center+size/2], axis=1)
    probability = 1/(1+np.exp(-raw[:, 4:]))
    scores = probability[:, :1] * probability[:, 1:]
    classes, best = scores.argmax(1), scores.max(1)
    keep = best > threshold
    return boxes[keep], classes[keep], best[keep]

grid, stride = make_grid()
raw = np.zeros((len(grid), 5+3))
raw[:, 4:] = -6  # background objectness and class logits
index = np.flatnonzero((stride==16) & (grid[:, 0]==18) & (grid[:, 1]==12))[0]
raw[index, :4] = [0., 0.25, np.log(9.), np.log(5.5)]
raw[index, 4] = 4.  # objectness
raw[index, 5+1] = 4.  # class 1
boxes, classes, scores = decode(raw, grid, stride)
print("predictions per 640x640 image:", len(grid))
print("kept:", len(boxes), "| box:", boxes[0], "| class:", classes[0], "| score:", round(float(scores[0]), 3))
```

predictions per 640x640 image: 8400
kept: 1 | box: [216. 152. 360. 240.] | class: 1 | score: 0.964

The grid location $(18,12)$ at stride 16 gives center $(288,196)$ after the offset, width 144 and height 88. This is a decoding demonstration, not a trained detector or a reproduction of a benchmark score. YOLOv4's three anchors per cell would produce three times as many raw box candidates at this resolution.

**Multi positives.** One positive per object (the centre cell) wastes good predictions, so YOLOX makes the centre $3 \times 3$ area positive ("centre sampling" in FCOS) [15].

**SimOTA.** OTA [76] treats label assignment as optimal transport between ground truths and predictions, solved with the Sinkhorn–Knopp algorithm, which adds about 25% training time. SimOTA keeps OTA's four insights (loss-aware, centre prior, a dynamic number of positives per object, a global view) and approximates the solution: for each pair of ground truth $g_i$ and prediction $p_j$ it computes the cost

$$
c_{ij} = L^{\text{cls}}_{ij} + \lambda L^{\text{reg}}_{ij},
$$

where $L^{\text{cls}}_{ij}$ and $L^{\text{reg}}_{ij}$ are the classification and regression losses between them and $\lambda$ a balancing coefficient, and selects for each ground truth the $k$ lowest-cost predictions within a fixed centre region as positives, with $k$ chosen per object by OTA's dynamic-$k$ estimation [15].

**Optional end-to-end variant.** Two extra convolutional layers, one-to-one label assignment and a stop-gradient make the detector NMS-free, but slightly less accurate and slower, so it is left out of the final models [15].

#### Results

All latencies are measured with FP16 precision and batch 1 on a single Tesla V100, and the roadmap table excludes post-processing [15]. On COCO test-dev, YOLOX-L reaches 50.0% AP at 69.0 FPS and YOLOX-DarkNet53 47.4% AP at 90.1 FPS; in the same table, YOLOv4-CSP is listed at 47.5% AP and 73.0 FPS and YOLOv5-L at 48.2% AP and 73.0 FPS [15]. At the small end, YOLOX-Nano with 0.91 M parameters and 1.08 GFLOPs reaches 25.3% AP on COCO val [15]. YOLOX won the Streaming Perception Challenge at the CVPR 2021 autonomous-driving workshop with a single YOLOX-L model [15].

#### Ablations

The decoupled head also converges much faster (the training curves of Figure 3). #### Conclusion

YOLOX shows that the anchor-based head YOLOv4 kept is not necessary for a fast YOLO: an anchor-free decoupled head with centre sampling and SimOTA, trained with strong augmentation from scratch, adds about 9 AP to a YOLOv3 baseline at nearly the same latency.

#### Problems

It is an arXiv report; its NMS-free variant loses accuracy and speed, so YOLOX still depends on NMS; SimOTA's dynamic $k$ adds training cost and, according to the YOLOv6 report, can slow training and make it unstable [16]. Its latencies exclude post-processing. [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector) replaced SimOTA with task-aligned assignment; [YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments) made the NMS-free head competitive.

### YOLOv6: a re-parameterized industrial detector

The original report and the v3.0 update describe different stages of this evolving recipe; implementation choices must be matched to the cited version [16], [77].

*Paper:* C. Li, L. Li, H. Jiang, K. Weng, Y. Geng et al. (Meituan), "YOLOv6: A single-stage object detection framework for industrial applications," tech. report, arXiv, 2022 [16]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the backbone](#backbone-cspdarknet53) and [export and re-parameterization](#export-reduced-precision-and-re-parameterization).

#### Challenge

Industrial deployment cares about measured speed on real hardware, about throughput at larger batches as well as latency, and about quantized (INT8) models. Multi-branch networks such as CSPDarknet53 are accurate but less parallel on GPUs, re-parameterized models are hard to quantize, and the many recent assignment and loss choices had not been compared within one YOLO [16]. YOLOv6 is an engineering study that makes those choices systematically.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolov6Source} alt="The backbone and Rep-PAN neck feed decoupled detection heads; training-time reparameterizable blocks and deployment-time operations must be distinguished." caption="Original paper figure 2 — The backbone and Rep-PAN neck feed decoupled detection heads; training-time reparameterizable blocks and deployment-time operations must be distinguished." />

*Figure source: [16], arXiv:2209.02976v1, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.02976).*

**EfficientRep backbone and Rep-PAN neck.** RepVGG [68] showed that a multi-branch block used during training ($3 \times 3$ convolution, $1 \times 1$ convolution and identity, each with batch normalization) can be folded into a single $3 \times 3$ convolution for inference (the folding is derived in the [Supplementary notes](#folding-a-re-parameterized-block-into-one-convolution)). Small YOLOv6 models build their backbone from such RepBlocks, which become stacks of $3 \times 3$ convolutions with ReLU at inference; for medium and large models, whose parameters would grow too quickly with plain stacks, YOLOv6 uses a CSPStackRep block: three $1 \times 1$ convolutions, a stack of sub-blocks of two RepVGG blocks with a residual connection, and a cross-stage partial connection [16]. The neck keeps the PAN topology of YOLOv4 and YOLOv5 with the same blocks (Rep-PAN) [16].

**Efficient decoupled head.** Following YOLOX's decoupled head but with only one $3 \times 3$ convolution per branch ("hybrid channels"), and anchor-free, anchor-point-based box prediction (distances from the anchor point to the box sides) [16].

**Label assignment.** SimOTA was used in an early version but slowed training and was "not rare to fall into unstable training"; YOLOv6 adopts task-alignment learning (TAL) from TOOD [78], which assigns positives by a metric that combines the classification score and the IoU of the predicted box [16]. [YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments) writes this metric out.

**Losses.** Varifocal loss (VFL) [73] for classification, which weights positives by their IoU and down-weights easy negatives; SIoU [79] box loss for the smallest models and GIoU [44] for the others; the distribution focal loss (DFL) [80] for the medium and large models only, because it outputs many more regression values; and no objectness loss, which did not help [16].

**Industry-handy improvements.** Training for 400 instead of 300 epochs for small models; **self-distillation**, in which a pre-trained copy of the same model is the teacher, with

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = \text{KL}(p^{\text{cls}}_t \,\|\, p^{\text{cls}}_s) + \text{KL}(p^{\text{reg}}_t \,\|\, p^{\text{reg}}_s), \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{det}} + \alpha\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}},
$$

where $p_t$ and $p_s$ are the teacher's and student's class predictions and DFL box distributions, KL is the Kullback–Leibler divergence and $\alpha$ is decayed with a cosine schedule so that the student relies on the teacher's soft labels early and on the hard labels later [16]; and a fix for the accuracy loss when evaluating without the extra grey borders that Mosaic training leaves around images ("Mosaic fading" and 3-pixel grey borders around a $634 \times 634$ image) [16].

**Quantization.** Re-parameterized models quantize poorly after training. YOLOv6 trains with RepOptimizer [81], which re-parameterizes the gradients instead of the architecture and yields weights that are friendlier to post-training quantization, and applies quantization-aware training with channel-wise distillation and graph optimization [16].

#### Results

Speeds are measured on a Tesla T4 with TensorRT 7.2 in FP16, at batch 1 (FPS and latency) and batch 32 (throughput); accuracy is COCO val2017 at 640 × 640, all models trained 300 epochs without pre-training or extra data [16]. The report does not say whether NMS is inside the timing. YOLOv6-N reaches 35.9% AP at 1.2 ms (802 FPS at batch 1, 1,234 at batch 32), YOLOv6-S 43.5% AP at 2.8 ms, and YOLOv6-L 52.5% AP at 10.2 ms [16]. In the same table YOLOv5-S is listed at 37.4% AP and 2.7 ms and YOLOX-S at 40.5% AP and 3.0 ms [16]. The quantized YOLOv6-S reaches 42.3% AP at 869 FPS (batch 32) [16].

#### Ablations

Table 5 builds YOLOv6-N step by step (FPS at batch 32 on T4) [16]: baseline 28.0% AP; + decoupled head 29.4%; + anchor-free 30.7% (and much faster, 637 → 962 FPS); + EfficientRep backbone and Rep-PAN neck 34.3%; + hybrid channels in the head 34.5% at 1,242 FPS. For assignment, on YOLOv6-N, ATSS [82] gives 32.5% AP, SimOTA 34.5% and TAL 35.0% [16]. Self-distillation on YOLOv6-L adds 0.4 AP on the classification branch alone, 0.3 more with box distillation and 0.6 more with the decayed weighting [16]. For quantization, plain post-training INT8 quantization drops YOLOv6-S (v1.0) from 42.4% to 35.0% AP; with RepOptimizer training it drops only to 40.9% [16]. Table 2 shows that single-path RepBlocks are best for the smallest model but multi-branch CSPStackRep blocks are better from the medium size up [16].

#### Conclusion

YOLOv6 shows that, for deployment on GPUs, re-parameterized single-path blocks, a lighter decoupled anchor-free head and task-aligned assignment give a better speed–accuracy trade-off than YOLOv4's design, and that quantization must be considered during training when re-parameterization is used.

#### Problems

It is a technical report, never peer reviewed; its tables mix released models re-evaluated by the authors (marked in the table) with reported numbers; the gains rely on many engineering details (longer training, self-distillation, grey borders) that are not part of the detector itself; and it still uses NMS. Later versions of YOLOv6 appeared as further releases. [YOLOv7](#yolov7-a-trainable-bag-of-freebies), released weeks earlier, took a different route to re-parameterization.

### YOLOv7: a trainable bag of freebies

*Paper:* C.-Y. Wang, A. Bochkovskiy and H.-Y. M. Liao, "YOLOv7: Trainable bag-of-freebies sets new state-of-the-art for real-time object detectors," CVPR 2023 [1]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the complete recipe](#the-complete-recipe); two of its authors wrote YOLOv4.

#### Challenge

YOLOv4's bag of freebies improved training without changing inference. Two newer training tools raised new questions, according to the paper: re-parameterized modules (which fold several branches into one at inference) did not work everywhere they were inserted, and dynamic label assignment became unclear once a network has several output heads (a lead head and auxiliary heads) [1]. YOLOv7 also wants a scaling method for concatenation-based architectures, for which the usual width/depth scaling rules do not hold.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolov7Source} alt="Track how ELAN and E-ELAN arrange concatenation paths and channel groups; the comparison concerns feature aggregation and gradient paths." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Track how ELAN and E-ELAN arrange concatenation paths and channel groups; the comparison concerns feature aggregation and gradient paths." />

*Figure source: [1], arXiv:2207.02696v1, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.02696).*

**E-ELAN.** ELAN [83] designs a network by controlling the shortest and longest gradient paths. Stacking more of its blocks eventually destroys this balance. Extended ELAN (E-ELAN) changes only the computational blocks: it uses group convolution to expand their channels and cardinality, shuffles the resulting feature maps into $g$ groups, concatenates them, and adds the $g$ groups back together ("merge cardinality"), so that different groups learn more diverse features while the transition layers and the gradient paths stay unchanged [1].

**Compound scaling for concatenation-based models.** In a concatenation-based block, scaling the depth (more layers in the block) also increases the block's output width, which changes the input width of the next transition layer. YOLOv7 therefore scales the depth of the computational blocks and, by the same factor of output-width change, the width of the transition layers [1].

**Planned re-parameterized convolution.** RepConv combines a $3 \times 3$ convolution, a $1 \times 1$ convolution and an identity branch. The paper finds that the identity branch can disrupt the intended gradient-path diversity of ResNet-style [32] and the concatenation of DenseNet-style [39] layers, which provide diverse gradients. It therefore uses **RepConvN**, RepConv without the identity branch, wherever a layer already has a residual or concatenation connection [1].

**Coarse for auxiliary, fine for lead.** With deep supervision, auxiliary heads in middle layers are trained alongside the final "lead" head. Instead of assigning labels to each head independently, YOLOv7's assigner uses the lead head's predictions together with the ground truth to generate soft labels for both heads: fine labels for the lead head, and coarser labels with more positive grid cells for the auxiliary head, which should favour recall; a constraint in the decoder keeps the extra coarse positives from producing perfect soft labels, so the fine labels always have the higher upper bound [1].

**Other freebies.** Batch normalization folded into the convolution at inference, YOLOR-style implicit knowledge folded into the convolution weights, and an EMA model used as the final inference model [1]. All models are trained from scratch on COCO.

#### Results

Table 2 of the CVPR paper compares on COCO with a V100, without TensorRT and without extra training data [1]. YOLOv7 reaches 51.4% AP on test-dev (51.2% on val) at 118 FPS with 36.9 M parameters; YOLOv7-X 53.1% at 98 FPS; YOLOv7-E6E, at 1280 pixels, 56.8% at 35 FPS [1]. In the same table YOLOX-L is listed at 50.1% AP and 69 FPS [1]. In Table 1's shared training setting, the YOLOv4 row gives 49.7% validation AP with 64.4 M parameters and 142.8 GFLOPs, while YOLOv7 gives 51.2% with 36.9 M parameters and 104.7 GFLOPs [1]. These are the table's absolute values; its retrained YOLOv4 result should not be substituted for the original 2020 paper's result.

#### Ablations

All on COCO val [1]:

- **Compound scaling** (Table 3): scaling only the width of a base model to 1.25 gives 52.4% AP, only the depth to 2.0 gives 52.7%, the compound method 52.9%.
- **Planned re-parameterization** (Tables 4 and 5): replacing convolutions in different positions of a 3-stacked ELAN with RepConv changes AP only slightly (52.17% to 52.33% against a 52.26% base); in residual models, a reversed CSP block with RepConvN improves AP slightly, while the plain variant without re-parameterization does not.
- **Auxiliary head** (Table 6, YOLOv7-E6 at 1280): base 55.6% AP; independent assignment 55.8%; lead-guided 55.9%; coarse-to-fine lead-guided 55.9% (AP75 61.1 against 61.0).
- **Partial auxiliary head** (Table 8, YOLOv7-E6E): base 56.3%; auxiliary head 56.5%; partial auxiliary head 56.8%.

The component gains are often a few tenths of AP. They are measured in different settings and cannot simply be added; the full models must be evaluated as complete recipes.

#### Conclusion

YOLOv7 extends the YOLOv4 philosophy to "trainable" freebies: re-parameterization planned around the network's residual and concatenation paths, and label assignment shared between lead and auxiliary heads, together with E-ELAN and a compound scaling rule. It reached the best speed–accuracy trade-off among the V100 detectors it compared.

#### Problems

The improvements per component are small, single-run numbers; the timings are without TensorRT, unlike the T4/TensorRT tables of most later papers, so they are hard to compare directly; the head is still anchor-based in the main models and NMS is still required. Its successor by the same group, [YOLOv9](#yolov9-programmable-gradient-information), revisited the auxiliary supervision from an information-flow perspective.

### YOLOv9: programmable gradient information

*Paper:* C.-Y. Wang, I-H. Yeh and H.-Y. M. Liao, "YOLOv9: Learning what you want to learn using programmable gradient information," ECCV 2024 [2]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the backbone](#backbone-cspdarknet53), whose CSP design YOLOv9 generalizes. Numbers below are from the arXiv version of the paper.

#### Challenge

As data passes through many layers, information about the input is lost: by the data-processing inequality the paper writes $I(X, X) \ge I(X, f_\theta(X)) \ge I(X, g_\phi(f_\theta(X)))$, where $I$ is mutual information, $X$ the input and $f_\theta$, $g_\phi$ successive transformations [2]. When the information needed to compute the loss is gone, the gradients become unreliable and the network may learn false correlations. Reversible architectures keep all information but cost inference time, and deep supervision with auxiliary heads can make shallow features specialize and lose information needed elsewhere [2]. Lightweight models suffer most.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolov9Source} alt="Separate the inference main branch from the auxiliary reversible and multi-level training paths; the diagram contrasts ways to preserve useful gradient information." caption="Original paper figure 3 — Separate the inference main branch from the auxiliary reversible and multi-level training paths; the diagram contrasts ways to preserve useful gradient information." />

*Figure source: [2], arXiv:2402.13616v2, PDF page 5. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.13616).*

**Programmable gradient information (PGI).** An auxiliary supervision framework with three parts [2]:

- the **main branch**, the network used at inference;
- an **auxiliary reversible branch**, used only during training, designed to retain useful input information and improve supervision. The motivating ideal is a reversible function $r_\psi$ with inverse $v_\zeta$ satisfies $X = v_\zeta(r_\psi(X))$, so this ideal transform loses no input information [2]. This identity motivates the architecture; it does not prove that every implemented feature tensor or gradient is lossless or correct. Because this branch is removed at inference, its cost is paid only in training;
- **multi-level auxiliary information**, an integration network between the auxiliary feature-pyramid heads and the main branch, which combines the gradients from the different prediction heads so that each pyramid level of the main branch receives information about all target objects rather than only those of its own size [2].

**GELAN.** The generalized efficient layer aggregation network combines CSPNet's split–merge design with ELAN's layer aggregation and allows any computational block inside; YOLOv9 uses CSP blocks with planned RepConv as computational blocks [2].

**Training.** Built on YOLOv7 and its anchor-free variant, trained from scratch for 500 epochs with linear warm-up in the first 3 epochs and Mosaic switched off for the last 15 epochs; the auxiliary loss follows YOLOv7's auxiliary-head setting [2].

**Follow the training graph.** The main branch must work after the auxiliary branch is removed. Its predictions therefore cannot depend on an auxiliary activation at inference. The auxiliary loss instead changes the gradients used to learn the main branch's parameters. This is the same distinction that makes ordinary auxiliary heads training-only: supervision influences learned weights without remaining in the final executable.

The information-theoretic inequality also needs a narrow interpretation. Processing cannot increase mutual information about the original input under the stated Markov structure, but useful detection features should discard irrelevant details. Retaining every input bit is not itself the detection objective. In deterministic continuous systems, mutual-information quantities require additional care and can be infinite. The inequality motivates avoiding harmful information loss; AP ablations, rather than an unmeasured mutual-information value, are the practical evidence for this implementation.

#### Results

Table 1 compares train-from-scratch detectors on COCO val by parameters, FLOPs and AP; it does not report latency [2]. YOLOv9-S reaches 46.8% AP with 7.1 M parameters and 26.4 GFLOPs, YOLOv9-C 53.0% with 25.3 M and 102.1 G, and YOLOv9-E 55.6% with 57.3 M and 189.0 G [2]. The paper states that YOLOv9-C matches the anchor-free YOLOv7's 53% AP with 42% fewer parameters and 22% less computation, and that YOLOv9-E improves on YOLOv8-X by 1.7 AP with 16% fewer parameters [2].

#### Ablations

On COCO val [2]:

- **Computational blocks in GELAN** (Table 2, small model): plain convolutions 44.8% AP, residual blocks 44.3%, Darknet blocks 44.5%, CSP blocks 45.5%.
- **Depth** (Table 3): AP grows roughly linearly with ELAN and CSP depth beyond 2, so GELAN is "not sensitive to the depth".
- **PGI against deep supervision** (Table 5): for GELAN-S, deep supervision lowers AP (46.7 → 46.5) while PGI raises it slightly (46.8); for GELAN-C, deep supervision gives 52.5 and PGI 53.0; for GELAN-E, 55.3 and 55.6.
- **Roadmap** (Table 6): YOLOv7 51.2% → anchor-free 53.0% → GELAN 53.2% → a deeper linkage 55.0% → PGI 55.6%.

In these experiments PGI outperforms ordinary deep supervision, including the small model where deep supervision slightly hurts. The results support the training design, while not directly measuring the proposed information-theoretic explanation.

#### Conclusion

YOLOv9 explains auxiliary supervision through information loss and proposes a training-only reversible branch (PGI) plus a flexible aggregation backbone (GELAN), achieving strong accuracy per parameter for train-from-scratch models.

#### Problems

The main comparison reports no speed, so the real-time claim rests on parameter and FLOP counts, which YOLOv4 itself showed to be poor proxies for GPU speed; [YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments) later measured YOLOv9-C at 10.57 ms on a T4 [17]. The information-bottleneck argument motivates the design but is not tested quantitatively, NMS is still required, and PGI makes training more expensive.

### YOLOv10: NMS-free training with dual assignments

*Paper:* A. Wang, H. Chen, L. Liu, K. Chen, Z. Lin, J. Han and G. Ding, "YOLOv10: Real-time end-to-end object detection," NeurIPS 2024 [17]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [label assignment](#label-assignment-several-anchors-per-object) and [post-processing](#post-processing-nms-and-diou-nms).

#### Challenge

YOLOs are trained with one-to-many assignment (YOLOv4's several anchors per object is one form), so several predictions fire on each object and NMS must remove the duplicates; NMS adds latency, depends on thresholds and requires an extra post-processing stage [17]. One-to-one assignment, as in DETR [59], removes NMS but gives weak supervision, which slows convergence and lowers accuracy. Separately, the paper argues that YOLO architectures had accumulated computational redundancy without a holistic efficiency and accuracy design [17].

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolov10Original} alt="The backbone and PAN feed two training heads. The one-to-many head supplies dense supervision, while inference retains the one-to-one head; the right panel measures matching consistency." caption="Original paper figure 2 — The backbone and PAN feed two training heads. The one-to-many head supplies dense supervision, while inference retains the one-to-one head; the right panel measures matching consistency." />

*Figure source: [17], arXiv:2405.14458v2, PDF page 4. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.14458v2).*

**Dual label assignments.** A second head with the same structure as the usual one-to-many head is added and trained with one-to-one assignment. Both share the backbone and neck, which therefore receive the rich one-to-many supervision; at inference only the one-to-one head is used, so no NMS is needed [17]. The one-to-one match is chosen by "top one selection", which the paper reports performs as well as Hungarian matching [60] with less training time [17].

**Consistent matching metric.** Both heads rank predictions with the task-aligned metric

$$
m(\alpha, \beta) = s \cdot p^{\alpha} \cdot \text{IoU}(\hat{b}, b)^{\beta},
$$

where $p$ is the classification score for the object's class, $\hat{b}$ and $b$ are the predicted and true boxes, $s$ is a spatial prior that is 1 if the prediction's anchor point lies inside the object and 0 otherwise, and $\alpha, \beta$ balance classification against localization [17]; YOLOv8-S uses $\alpha = 0.5$, $\beta = 6$ by default [17]. The paper derives the **supervision gap** between the heads. For an object with largest IoU $u^*$ among the predictions, let $\Omega$ be the one-to-many positives and $i$ the one-to-one match; with task-aligned classification targets $t_{o2m,j} = u^* \cdot m_{o2m,j}/m^*_{o2m} \le u^*$ and $t_{o2o,i} = u^*$, the 1-Wasserstein distance between the two classification objectives is

$$
A = t_{o2o,i} - \mathbb{I}(i \in \Omega)\, t_{o2m,i} + \sum_{k \in \Omega \setminus \{i\}} t_{o2m,k},
$$

where $\mathbb{I}$ is the indicator function [17]. The gap is smallest when $i$ is the best positive of the one-to-many head, which holds when $\alpha_{o2o} = r\,\alpha_{o2m}$ and $\beta_{o2o} = r\,\beta_{o2m}$ (then $m_{o2o} = m_{o2m}^r$, and both heads rank predictions in the same order); the paper uses $r = 1$ [17].

The toy snippet places an $8 \times 8$ grid of anchor points on a $100 \times 100$ image with three objects, computes $m$ with these defaults, and compares top-4 one-to-many assignment with Hungarian one-to-one assignment.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

def iou_matrix(a, b):
    x1 = np.maximum(a[:, None, 0], b[None, :, 0]); y1 = np.maximum(a[:, None, 1], b[None, :, 1])
    x2 = np.minimum(a[:, None, 2], b[None, :, 2]); y2 = np.minimum(a[:, None, 3], b[None, :, 3])
    inter = np.clip(x2 - x1, 0, None) * np.clip(y2 - y1, 0, None)
    area = lambda b: (b[:, 2] - b[:, 0]) * (b[:, 3] - b[:, 1])
    return inter / (area(a)[:, None] + area(b)[None, :] - inter)

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
gt = np.array([[20, 20, 60, 70], [55, 30, 95, 80], [10, 80, 40, 100]], float)
# 8 x 8 grid of anchor points (stride 12.5 on a 100 x 100 image), each with a predicted box
ys, xs = np.mgrid[0:8, 0:8]
pts = np.stack([(xs.ravel() + 0.5) * 12.5, (ys.ravel() + 0.5) * 12.5], axis=1)
inside = ((pts[:, None, 0] > gt[None, :, 0]) & (pts[:, None, 0] < gt[None, :, 2]) &
          (pts[:, None, 1] > gt[None, :, 1]) & (pts[:, None, 1] < gt[None, :, 3]))      # spatial prior s
near = inside.argmax(axis=1)                       # the object each point "looks at"
pred = gt[near] + rng.normal(0, 5, (len(pts), 4))  # noisy box predictions
p = np.where(inside.any(axis=1), rng.uniform(0.3, 0.9, len(pts)), rng.uniform(0, 0.2, len(pts)))

alpha, beta = 0.5, 6.0                             # the defaults quoted in the YOLOv10 paper
m = inside * p[:, None] ** alpha * iou_matrix(pred, gt) ** beta   # (points, objects)

# one-to-many: the top-k points by m for every object become positives
k = 4
o2m = {}
for j in range(len(gt)):
    best = np.argsort(-m[:, j])[:k]
    o2m[j] = best[m[best, j] > 0].tolist()
# one-to-one: Hungarian matching on cost -m (each object gets exactly one point)
rows, cols = linear_sum_assignment(-m)
o2o = {int(c): [int(r)] for r, c in zip(rows, cols)}
print("one-to-many positives:", o2m)
print("one-to-one positives: ", dict(sorted(o2o.items())))
print("positives per image: o2m", sum(map(len, o2m.values())), "| o2o", len(o2o))
top1 = m.argmax(axis=0)                            # YOLOv10's cheaper top-1 choice
print("top-1 equals Hungarian here:", all(top1[j] == o2o[j][0] for j in o2o))
```

```text
one-to-many positives: {0: [26, 34, 42, 43], 1: [37, 46, 38, 30], 2: [58, 50, 49, 57]}
one-to-one positives:  {0: [26], 1: [37], 2: [58]}
positives per image: o2m 12 | o2o 3
top-1 equals Hungarian here: True
```

One-to-many gives four times as many positives, and Figure 5 shows their cost: four overlapping boxes per object, all taught to fire. One-to-one keeps the single best point per object, which is also the top-ranked point under one-to-many, the property the consistent metric is designed to guarantee. The cheap per-object top-1 choice agrees with Hungarian matching here because no two objects compete for the same point. This agreement is specific to the toy inputs: competing targets require conflict resolution, and global matching need not equal independent top-1 selection.

Left: four colored positive points per object, each with a dashed predicted box, so every object is covered by four overlapping boxes. Right: one positive point and one dashed box per object." caption="Figure 5 — Label assignment on a toy scene. (b) One-to-one (Hungarian): exactly one positive per object; every other point is trained as background." />

**Efficiency-driven design.** Three changes reduce computation [17]: a **lightweight classification head** (two depthwise-separable $3 \times 3$ convolutions and a $1 \times 1$ convolution), justified by an analysis showing that regression errors matter more than classification errors; **spatial–channel decoupled downsampling**, a pointwise convolution that changes the channels followed by a depthwise stride-2 convolution, instead of one $3 \times 3$ stride-2 convolution, which lowers the cost from $O(\tfrac{9}{2} HWC^2)$ to $O(2HWC^2 + \tfrac{9}{2}HWC)$; and a **rank-guided block design**, which measures the numerical rank of each stage's last convolution (the number of singular values above half the largest), sorts stages from most redundant to least, and replaces their blocks with a cheaper compact inverted block (CIB) as long as accuracy does not drop.

**Accuracy-driven design.** Large-kernel ($7 \times 7$) depthwise convolutions in the CIBs of deep stages, re-parameterized with an extra $3 \times 3$ branch, for small models only; and **partial self-attention (PSA)**, which splits the channels in two after a $1 \times 1$ convolution, applies multi-head self-attention and a feed-forward network to one half only, and is placed once after the lowest-resolution stage [17].

#### Results

Latency is end to end on a T4 GPU with TensorRT FP16, at batch 1, averaged over COCO val, with TensorRT's efficient-NMS plugin appended for models that need NMS and I/O excluded [17]. YOLOv10-S reaches 46.3% AP at 2.49 ms, against 44.9% at 7.07 ms for YOLOv8-S and 46.5% at 4.58 ms for RT-DETR-R18; YOLOv10-X reaches 54.4% AP at 10.70 ms, against 54.3% at 13.71 ms for RT-DETR-R101; YOLOv10-B matches YOLOv9-C's 52.5% AP at 5.74 ms instead of 10.57 ms [17]. The table also reports a forward-only latency ($\text{Latency}^f$) for comparisons without post-processing; do not mix the two columns.

#### Ablations

On COCO val [17]:

- **NMS-free and the architecture** (Table 2): YOLOv10-S goes from 44.9% AP at 7.07 ms (YOLOv8-S with NMS) to 44.3% at 2.44 ms (NMS-free), 44.5% at 2.31 ms (+ efficiency design) and 46.3% at 2.49 ms (+ accuracy design).
- **Dual assignments** (Table 3): one-to-many only, 44.9% AP at 7.07 ms; one-to-one only, 43.4% at 2.44 ms; both heads, 44.3% at 2.44 ms. The latency difference is NMS; the accuracy price of NMS-free inference is 0.6 AP.
- **Matching metric** (Table 4, with $\alpha_{o2m} = 0.5$, $\beta_{o2m} = 6$): the consistent setting $(0.5, 6)$ gives 44.3%; inconsistent settings such as $(0.5, 2)$ give as little as 42.7%; and the proportional settings $(0.25, 3)$ and $(1.0, 12)$, which the theory predicts to be equally consistent, also reach 44.3%.
- **Efficiency components** (Table 5): the lighter classification head, the decoupled downsampling and the rank-guided blocks reduce YOLOv10-S from 11.2 M to 6.2 M parameters and 2.44 to 2.31 ms while AP stays between 44.2% and 44.5%.

#### Conclusion

YOLOv10 brings NMS-free inference to YOLOs without giving up their dense training signal: a second, one-to-one head trained with a metric consistent with the one-to-many head. Combined with a careful efficiency and accuracy redesign, it cuts end-to-end latency substantially at equal accuracy.

#### Problems

The NMS-free head is 0.6 AP below the NMS-based one in its own ablation; the two heads receive equal loss weights throughout training; the box head still uses the distribution focal loss of its YOLOv8 base; and the ablations are single runs. [YOLO26](#yolo26-a-dfl-free-end-to-end-yolo) adds a progressive loss weighting toward the one-to-one head and removes the distribution head; [DEIM](#deim-dense-one-to-one-matching) attacks the same weak one-to-one supervision from the DETR side.

### YOLOv12: an attention-centric YOLO

*Paper:* Y. Tian, Q. Ye and D. Doermann, "YOLOv12: Attention-centric real-time object detectors," NeurIPS 2025 [18]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the modified SAM](#attention-and-fusion-the-modified-sam-and-weighted-residuals), the lightweight attention module considered by YOLOv4. Numbers below are from the NeurIPS version.

#### Challenge

Attention models learn long-range relations well, but YOLOs had stayed convolutional because attention is slower: its cost grows quadratically with the number of tokens, and its memory access is inefficient [18]. Linear attention degrades quality; local window attention (as in Swin-style models) needs input sizes divisible into windows, which conflicts with YOLO's rectangular inference at native aspect ratios [18]. The challenge is attention as the core of a YOLO at real-time speed.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolov12FinalSource} alt="Compare attention regions rather than treating all local attention as identical; area attention groups spatial positions differently from the windowed alternatives." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Compare attention regions rather than treating all local attention as identical; area attention groups spatial positions differently from the windowed alternatives." />

*Figure source: [18], Published paper PDF, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/7103444259031cc58051f8c9a4868533-Paper-Conference.pdf).*

**Area attention ($A^2$).** For a feature map of size $H \times W$, flatten it into $H \times W$ tokens and split the sequence into $l$ equal segments ("areas") of $HW/l$ consecutive tokens; self-attention is computed independently inside each area, which needs only a reshape [18]. With $n$ tokens, $h$ heads of size $d$, vanilla attention costs about $2n^2hd$; area attention divides the quadratic term by $l$. The default is $l = 4$ (or 8), which keeps a large receptive field, and the only constraint is that $HW$ is divisible by $l$ [18]. The memory-access problem is handled with FlashAttention [84].

**R-ELAN.** Adding attention made the larger ELAN-based models fail to converge or train unstably, even with Adam-type optimizers [85] [18]. Residual ELAN adds a shortcut from the block's input to its output, scaled by a factor (default 0.01), and simplifies the aggregation: a transition layer produces one feature map, which is processed by the following blocks and then concatenated, forming a bottleneck [18].

**Architectural adjustments.** The hierarchical YOLO structure is kept; the backbone's last stage keeps a single block; the attention's MLP ratio is reduced from 4 to 1.5 (2 for the N, S and M models); positional encodings are removed and a $7 \times 7$ separable convolution ("position perceiver") supplies position information; and $v$ is projected separately from $q$ and $k$, which speeds up inference by about 10% [18].

**What an area does and does not connect.** Inside one area, every token can directly attend to every other token. Tokens in different areas do not interact through that attention operation. Other blocks, convolutions and feature-fusion paths can subsequently exchange information, but one partitioned layer is not equivalent to global attention. This explains both the reduced cost and the accuracy trade-off in the ablation.

FlashAttention changes how the attention computation is scheduled in memory; it does not turn exact softmax attention into a different linear-attention formula. Area partitioning and an efficient attention kernel solve different bottlenecks and should be evaluated separately. The deployment engine must support the chosen kernel and shapes. A timing result from a recent GPU does not establish an advantage on a CPU or a mobile accelerator, even when the mathematical operation count looks favorable.

#### Results

Latency is measured on a T4 with TensorRT FP16; accuracy on COCO val at 640 × 640; models are trained 600 epochs with SGD [18]. YOLOv12-N reaches 40.5% AP at 1.62 ms, against 38.5% at 1.84 ms for YOLOv10-N and 39.4% at 1.5 ms for YOLO11-N; YOLOv12-S 47.8% at 2.44 ms; YOLOv12-X 55.4% at 10.47 ms, against 54.4% at 10.70 ms for YOLOv10-X [18]. The comparison table takes competitors' numbers from their official reports, and the paper notes that some achieve higher speed in its own reproduction [18].

#### Ablations

- **Area attention** (Table 3, same hardware, without FlashAttention): for YOLOv12-N, global attention reaches 41.7% AP but takes 1.6 ms (FP16, RTX 3080) and 66.3 ms on CPU; area attention reaches 40.5% AP at 1.2 ms and 38.7 ms on CPU; other local attentions (criss-cross, two window schemes) reach 39.5% to 40.3% [18]. Area attention therefore trades about 1 AP of global attention for a large speed gain, and beats the other local schemes.
- **R-ELAN** (Table 4): without the residual shortcut, the L and X models do not converge; with it and scale 0.01 they reach 53.8% and 55.4% AP; for the N model the shortcut is not needed [18].

#### Conclusion

YOLOv12 makes attention the central operator of a YOLO at real-time speed, with a simple area partition, FlashAttention for memory access and a residual aggregation block that keeps large attention models trainable.

#### Problems

The speed relies on FlashAttention, which needs recent GPUs and is not available on every deployment target; global attention remains more accurate in its own ablation; the main table mixes official numbers from other papers; and YOLOv12 still uses NMS. The [YOLOv13](#yolov13-hypergraph-correlation) paper reports a lower YOLOv12-N accuracy in its own reproduction (40.1% AP) [19], and the Jegham et al. survey found that YOLOv12's added complexity did not pay off on its datasets [62].

### YOLOv13: hypergraph correlation

*Paper:* M. Lei, S. Li, Y. Wu, H. Hu, Y. Zhou et al., "YOLOv13: Real-time object detection with hypergraph-enhanced adaptive visual perception," arXiv, 2025 [19]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the PANet neck](#neck-part-2-panet-path-aggregation), the information path that YOLOv13 redesigns.

#### Challenge

Convolutions aggregate local information and self-attention, including YOLOv12's area attention, models pairwise relations between positions. The paper argues that neither captures "many-to-many" high-order correlations, for example several related objects in a cluttered scene, and that the usual backbone → neck → head flow limits how such information reaches the whole network [19].

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolov13Source} alt="Follow multi-scale features through the hypergraph-enhanced aggregation and redistribution paths, then into the detection heads." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Follow multi-scale features through the hypergraph-enhanced aggregation and redistribution paths, then into the detection heads." />

*Figure source: [19], arXiv:2506.17733v2, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.17733).*

**Adaptive hypergraph computation.** A hypergraph has hyperedges that connect any number of vertices. Here every position of a feature map is a vertex $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^C$, $i = 1, \dots, N$, and $M$ hyperedges are learned rather than defined by thresholds [19]. Global average and max pooling give a context vector $f_{\text{ctx}} \in \mathbb{R}^{2C}$; a mapping layer turns it into an offset $\Delta P \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times C}$ that is added to learnable prototypes $P_0$, giving dynamic hyperedge prototypes $P = P_0 + \Delta P$. Each vertex is projected to a query $z_i = W_{\text{pre}} x_i$; with $h$ heads of size $d_h = C/h$, the similarity to prototype $m$ is the average over heads of $\langle \hat{z}^\tau_i, \hat{p}^\tau_m \rangle / \sqrt{d_h}$, and a softmax over vertices gives the continuous participation matrix

$$
A_{i,m} = \frac{\exp(\bar{s}_{i,m})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(\bar{s}_{j,m})},
$$

where $\bar{s}_{i,m}$ is that averaged similarity [19]. Hypergraph convolution then collects vertex features into each hyperedge and sends them back:

$$
f_m = \sigma\Big(W_e \sum_{i=1}^{N} A_{i,m} x_i\Big), \qquad \tilde{x}_i = \sigma\Big(W_v \sum_{m=1}^{M} A_{i,m} f_m\Big),
$$

where $W_e$ and $W_v$ are the hyperedge and vertex projection weights and $\sigma$ an activation [19]. For a fixed number of hyperedges $M$ and feature width, these aggregation steps scale linearly in the number of positions $N$; increasing $M$ or the channel width still adds work.

**HyperACE.** The backbone features $B_3$, $B_4$, $B_5$ are resized to the size of $B_4$ and fused; the result is split into three channel groups: one goes through $K$ parallel C3AH blocks (a CSP-style block around the adaptive hypergraph computation) for global high-order correlation, one through lightweight depthwise-separable blocks for local correlation, and one is a shortcut; the three are concatenated and fused by a $1 \times 1$ convolution [19].

**FullPAD.** The enhanced features are distributed through three "tunnels" to the backbone–neck connection, inside the neck and to the neck–head connection, each added as $F_i + \gamma H_i$ with a learnable scalar $\gamma$ [19].

**Lightweight blocks.** Large-kernel convolutions are replaced by depthwise-separable ones (DS-C3k2 and related blocks) [19]. Models are trained 600 epochs with batch 256 and SGD; the number of hyperedges is 4, 8, 8 and 12 for the N, S, L and X models [19].

**Read the tensor flow.** The participation matrix has shape $N\times M$: rows correspond to spatial positions and columns to learned hyperedges. Gathering sends many spatial features into each hyperedge; scattering lets each position receive information from several hyperedges. The intermediate nonlinearity and image-conditioned prototypes make this different from simply averaging all pixels once.

This is still a model of relationships learned from the detection objective. A hyperedge need not correspond to a labeled semantic group, and a visualization of its weights is not proof that it discovers a particular physical interaction. Likewise, information distributed to multiple pipeline stages does not remove their spatial-resolution constraints. When evaluating a crowded scene, inspect missed objects and localization errors in addition to the aggregate AP. The architecture's intended many-to-many interpretation is a hypothesis supported by its experiments, not an extra annotation supplied to the model.

#### Results

On COCO val, with latency on a T4 with TensorRT FP16, YOLOv13-N reaches 41.6% AP at 1.97 ms and YOLOv13-X 54.8% at 14.67 ms; in the same table, re-trained by the authors on their hardware, YOLOv12-N reaches 40.1% at 1.83 ms and YOLO11-N 38.6% at 1.53 ms [19]. Trained on COCO and tested on PASCAL VOC 2007 [71] without fine-tuning, YOLOv13-N reaches 65.3% AP against 64.3% for YOLOv12-N [19].

#### Ablations

On YOLOv13-S [19]: FullPAD distribution to all three locations raises AP from 47.1% (no distribution) to 48.0%, each single location giving 47.6% to 47.8%; the number of hyperedges changes AP from 47.8% ($M = 2$) to 48.1% ($M = 16$); replacing the depthwise-separable blocks by ordinary ones keeps AP (48.0% against 48.0%) but raises FLOPs from 20.8 G to 27.1 G.

#### Conclusion

YOLOv13 adds a learned hypergraph module for high-order feature correlation and distributes its output through the whole network, improving accuracy per FLOP, especially for the smallest models.

#### Problems

It is an arXiv paper; its accuracy gains come with higher latency than the models it beats (1.97 ms against 1.83 ms for the re-trained YOLOv12-N), its comparisons re-train some baselines and take others from official numbers, and it still uses NMS. Whether hyperedges capture "high-order" relations beyond what attention does is argued with visualizations rather than measured.

### YOLO26: a DFL-free end-to-end YOLO

*Paper:* G. Jocher, J. Qiu, M. Liu, S. Lyu, F. C. Akyon and M. E. Kalfaoglu (Ultralytics), "Ultralytics YOLO26: Unified real-time end-to-end vision models," arXiv, June 2026 [20]; the software was released in January 2026. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [label assignment](#label-assignment-several-anchors-per-object), [post-processing](#post-processing-nms-and-diou-nms) and the [training schedule](#training-schedule-cosine-annealing-genetic-search-and-random-shapes).

#### Challenge

The paper lists four limits of current YOLOs [20]: (a) most still need NMS, and YOLOv10's dual heads give both heads the same loss weight throughout training, leaving the inference head under-trained; (b) the distribution focal loss (DFL), used in the Ultralytics head since YOLOv8, expands each box side from one number to $K = 16$ logits, inflating the head (YOLO11n has 2.6 M parameters and 6.5 GFLOPs with DFL and 2.3 M and 5.2 GFLOPs without), and limits the range of each side; (c) training needs about 600 epochs; (d) task-aligned assignment selects candidates whose anchor centres lie inside the box, so tiny objects can receive no positive at all.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperYolo26Original} alt="Read the training pipeline from shared features through dual heads and assignment to progressive loss weights and MuSGD. The two training losses do not mean both heads are required in default inference." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Read the training pipeline from shared features through dual heads and assignment to progressive loss weights and MuSGD. The two training losses do not mean both heads are required in default inference." />

*Figure source: [20], arXiv:2606.03748v1, PDF page 6. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.03748v1).*

**Dual heads, NMS-free by default.** As in YOLOv10, a one-to-one head and a one-to-many head share the backbone and neck and use the same task-aligned assignment with different cardinalities: the one-to-one path forms a candidate set with top-$k = 7$ and then keeps the top 1 per object; the one-to-many head uses top-$k = 10$ [20]. The one-to-one head outputs at most 300 detections per image without NMS; the one-to-many head remains available with NMS when the highest accuracy matters [20].

**No DFL.** DFL [80] predicts a discrete distribution over $K$ bins for each box side and decodes the side as its expected value $d = \sum_{i=0}^{K-1} i \cdot \text{softmax}(z)_i$, so a side can be at most $(K - 1)$ strides long: about 960 pixels of width at stride 32 with $K = 16$ [20]. YOLO26 regresses the box directly with an L1 loss and recovers the lost localization quality with the training changes below [20]. The DFL loss itself, as defined in GFL, is

$$
\hat{y} = \sum_{i=0}^{n} S_i\, y_i, \qquad \text{DFL}(S_i, S_{i+1}) = -\big((y_{i+1} - y)\log S_i + (y - y_i)\log S_{i+1}\big),
$$

where $S_i$ is the softmax probability of the bin value $y_i$, $y$ the true distance and $y_i \le y \le y_{i+1}$ its two neighbouring bins; the minimum puts the mass on the two bracketing bins in proportion to the distances, so that $\hat{y} = y$ exactly [80]. The snippet shows the decoding, the loss and the range limit.

```python
import numpy as np

K, stride = 16, 8                        # 16 bins per box side, a stride-8 level
bins = np.arange(K)                      # support y_0 = 0, ..., y_15 = 15 (in stride units)

def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max())
    return e / e.sum()

def dfl(logits, y):
    """Distribution focal loss for one box side with continuous target y (stride units)."""
    S = softmax(logits)
    i = int(np.floor(y))                 # neighbours y_i <= y <= y_i + 1
    return -((i + 1 - y) * np.log(S[i]) + (y - i) * np.log(S[i + 1]))

y = 5.3                                  # true distance: 5.3 cells = 42.4 pixels
flat = np.zeros(K)                       # untrained head: uniform distribution
sharp = np.full(K, -10.0); sharp[5], sharp[6] = np.log(0.7), np.log(0.3)   # the ideal shape
for name, z in [("uniform", flat), ("ideal", sharp)]:
    S = softmax(z)
    print(f"{name:7s} expected distance {S @ bins:5.2f} cells = {S @ bins * stride:6.1f} px | DFL {dfl(z, y):.3f}")
print("largest distance this head can output:", (K - 1) * stride, "px")
```

```text
uniform expected distance  7.50 cells =   60.0 px | DFL 2.773
ideal   expected distance  5.30 cells =   42.4 px | DFL 0.611
largest distance this head can output: 120 px
```

The ideal distribution decodes to exactly 5.3 cells, but its loss is not zero (0.611 is the entropy of the 0.7 / 0.3 split), and the support is finite: with 16 bins a side can be at most 15 strides long. Figure 6 shows the three cases.

<Figure src={figDfl} alt="Three bar charts over 16 bins. Left: a flat distribution whose expected value is 7.50, far from the true distance 5.3. Middle: probability 0.7 at bin 5 and 0.3 at bin 6, whose expected value is exactly 5.30. Right: a broad bell-shaped distribution centred near bin 6 with expected value 6.01." caption="Figure 6 — How a distribution-based box side is decoded. The dashed line is the expected value (the predicted distance); the yellow line is the true distance 5.3. DFL trains the head toward the middle panel; a broad distribution, as on the right, expresses uncertainty about the edge." />

**Progressive loss.** The total loss shifts weight from the one-to-many to the one-to-one head during training:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \alpha(t)\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{one2many}} + \big(1 - \alpha(t)\big)\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{one2one}}, \qquad \alpha(t) = \max\!\Big(1 - \frac{t}{\max(E - 1, 1)},\, 0\Big)(\alpha_{\text{init}} - \alpha_{\text{final}}) + \alpha_{\text{final}},
$$

where $t$ is the current epoch, $E$ the number of epochs and $\alpha_{\text{init}}, \alpha_{\text{final}}$ the initial and final one-to-many weights [20]. The default starts at weights (0.8, 0.2) for (one-to-many, one-to-one) and ends at (0.1, 0.9) [20].

**STAL (small-target-aware label assignment).** For candidate selection only, every box side shorter than the smallest stride $s_{\min}$ is enlarged to a reference size $s_{\text{ref}}$ (the second pyramid stride), so that at least one anchor centre falls inside a tiny box; the original box is kept for scoring, assignment and regression [20].

**MuSGD.** A hybrid optimizer: for multi-dimensional weights it mixes the SGD-momentum update with the Muon update [86], which orthogonalizes the momentum-based update with a few Newton–Schulz iterations; biases and normalization parameters use plain SGD [20].

The paper also adds task-specific heads for segmentation, pose and oriented boxes, and an open-vocabulary variant; these are outside this chapter.

#### Results

Table 7 lists the released detection models on COCO val at 640 pixels, with T4 TensorRT 10 latency and CPU ONNX latency [20]: YOLO26n reaches 40.9% AP with NMS on the one-to-many head and 40.1% end to end at 1.7 ms; YOLO26s 48.6% / 47.8% at 2.5 ms; YOLO26x 57.5% / 56.9% at 11.8 ms. The end-to-end head trails the NMS head by 0.6 to 0.8 AP across sizes [20]. The released models are pre-trained on Objects365 [87] before COCO fine-tuning [20], so they are not directly comparable with COCO-only models.

#### Ablations

Table 2 goes from YOLO11s to YOLO26s, reporting the NMS-based AP and, from the end-to-end step on, the end-to-end AP [20]: YOLO11s 47.0%; removing DFL 46.4%; + L1 loss 46.6%; + STAL 46.8%; + backbone and neck refinement 47.0%; + end-to-end head 46.4% end to end (47.0% with NMS); + progressive loss 46.7% (47.2%); + MuSGD 47.1% (47.6%); + Objects365 pre-training 47.4% (48.0%); + hyper-parameter search 47.8% (48.6%). A controlled DFL ablation at 640 and 1280 pixels finds that the DFL-free head is better at both, by 0.3 AP (1.0 APL) at 640 and 1.3 AP (2.2 APL) at 1280 [20]. MuSGD reaches 47.4% AP in 500 epochs against 47.0% for SGD in 600 [20]. For the progressive loss, starting at (1.0, 0.0), which gives the one-to-one head no weight at first, does not help (46.3% to 46.4% end to end), while the default schedule reaches 46.7% [20]. For STAL, $s_{\text{ref}} = 16$ is best (46.8% AP, small-object AP 29.6% against 29.0%), while 8 and 32 match or underperform the baseline [20].

#### Conclusion

YOLO26 makes the NMS-free head the default deployment path, removes the distribution-based box head and compensates with training changes aimed at the inference head and at tiny objects, ending where YOLOv4's anchor-based, NMS-dependent design started from the opposite side.

#### Problems

It is an arXiv paper by the company that ships the software, with benchmarks from its own repository; its main numbers use Objects365 pre-training and a hyper-parameter search; each training component adds a few tenths of AP in single runs; and the end-to-end head is still 0.6 to 0.8 AP below the NMS head. The paper itself names broader evaluation beyond COCO-centric benchmarks and learned loss schedules as future work [20].

### RT-DETR: a real-time DETR

Zhao et al.'s CVPR 2024 paper makes direct set prediction competitive with fast YOLO detectors by redesigning the expensive encoder and the initialization of decoder queries [21]. The model first finds useful image features, then refines a small set of candidate objects, rather than generating thousands of dense predictions and suppressing duplicates afterward.

#### Challenge

DETR [59] removes hand-designed NMS through one-to-one matching, but its original form is expensive and slow to train. Multi-scale features improve detection, especially across object sizes, while increasing the number of tokens. Flattening all high-resolution feature maps into a general-purpose attention encoder can spend much of the runtime on interactions that contribute little to the final boxes.

Meanwhile, a YOLO forward pass is only one part of its deployed detector. Thresholding and NMS depend on the image and confidence settings. RT-DETR therefore asks whether an end-to-end detector can match a fast dense detector when post-processing is counted. The claim is tied to its measured hardware and runtime; it is not a theorem that a Transformer is faster than a convolutional detector.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperRtdetrSource} alt="The hybrid encoder combines within-scale interaction and cross-scale fusion before query selection and decoder refinement." caption="Original paper figure 4 — The hybrid encoder combines within-scale interaction and cross-scale fusion before query selection and decoder refinement." />

*Figure source: [21], arXiv:2304.08069v3, PDF page 5. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.08069).*

**Backbone and shapes.** The backbone produces three feature maps, commonly written $S_3,S_4,S_5$, at strides 8, 16 and 32. Project their channel dimensions into a shared width $D$. For a $640\times640$ input, the spatial grids contain $80^2$, $40^2$ and $20^2$ positions. These sizes explain the bottleneck: the finest level contributes most tokens, while the coarsest level carries the strongest semantic abstraction.

**AIFI: within-scale interaction.** Attention-based Intra-scale Feature Interaction applies self-attention only to the coarsest map. Schematically,

$$
X_5=\operatorname{flatten}(S_5),\qquad
F_5=\operatorname{reshape}\bigl(\operatorname{Transformer}(X_5)\bigr).
$$

$X_5\in\mathbb R^{N_5\times D}$ is the coarse feature sequence, $N_5$ its number of spatial locations, and $F_5$ its spatially restored output. Positional information is supplied to attention. Interacting semantically rich coarse tokens captures useful relationships while avoiding all-pairs attention over the much longer concatenated sequence. It does not mean high-resolution information is discarded; the other maps enter the next module.

**CCFF: between-scale fusion.** CNN-based Cross-scale Feature Fusion combines $S_3,S_4,F_5$ through top-down and bottom-up paths. Convolutions adjust channels and fuse adjacent resolutions; RepBlocks provide a training-friendly fusion module with a simpler deployable form. This deliberately assigns different jobs to different operations: attention handles coarse semantic interaction, and convolution handles efficient spatial and scale fusion. The result is a sequence of multi-scale image features for the decoder.

**Query selection.** The encoder predicts classes and boxes at candidate locations. Selecting queries by class confidence alone can choose features that recognize an object but localize it poorly. The paper's *uncertainty-minimal query selection* couples classification supervision to localization quality, seeking features that do both well. A fixed number of high-ranking features initialize decoder content and box positions.

The term “uncertainty” here needs care. The paper motivates it using a discrepancy between classification and localization predictions; it is not a separately measured posterior variance that proves the system knows when it is wrong. The practical mechanism changes the training of the encoder predictions so that the scores used for selection reflect box quality. It is also not an instruction to use ground-truth IoU during deployment: ground truth is available only in training and evaluation.

**Decoder and matching.** Object queries attend to image features and interact with one another, then produce class scores and box refinements at each decoder layer. One-to-one matching pairs predicted objects with ground-truth objects for the training loss. Unmatched predictions learn the background objective. A compact way to express the assignment is

$$
\hat\pi=\arg\min_{\pi\ \mathrm{injective}}
\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal C\bigl(\hat y_{\pi(j)},y_j\bigr).
$$

$M$ is the number of ground-truth objects, $y_j$ an annotated class and box, $\hat y_i$ a query prediction, and $\mathcal C$ the matching cost combining classification and localization terms. An injective map $\pi$ assigns each target a distinct query; the Hungarian algorithm solves the finite assignment [60]. The cost used to choose assignments and the differentiable losses applied after assignment are related but distinct objects.

**Why inference can omit NMS.** Training rewards one query for each object and penalizes redundant foreground predictions through unmatched-query supervision. This encourages a set of unique detections. It does not mathematically prohibit duplicates. Inference still needs score selection, box decoding, coordinate restoration and transfer of outputs; “end to end” does not erase those costs.

**Adjustable decoder depth.** Auxiliary prediction heads supervise intermediate decoder outputs. A trained detector can return an earlier layer to trade some accuracy for latency without retraining. This is early output from an iterative refinement network, not a guarantee that every layer count has the same accuracy. A deployment implementation must actually stop computation at the chosen layer to obtain its speed benefit.

#### Results

The main table reports **53.1 AP / 108 FPS** for RT-DETR-R50 and **54.3 AP / 74 FPS** for RT-DETR-R101 on COCO validation at $640\times640$, using a T4 GPU and TensorRT FP16 with batch one [21]. Keep those conditions attached to the values. The paper compares against selected contemporary YOLO systems under its stated timing protocol, including post-processing; these numbers cannot be directly ranked against YOLOv4's different GPU measurements.

Another important result is that useful accuracy remains when returning intermediate decoder outputs. For the six-layer-trained R50 model, Table 5 gives **53.1 AP at layer 6**, **53.0 at layer 5**, **52.7 at layer 4** and **52.4 at layer 3**, with latency decreasing from **9.3 to 7.9 ms** between the six- and three-layer outputs [21]. These are a measured trade-off for this trained model and runtime, not universal layer costs.

#### Ablations

Table 3 starts with a detector without the proposed encoder and adds different ways of processing scales. Its baseline A gives **43.0 AP / 7.2 ms**. Within-scale interaction raises AP to **44.9** but latency to **11.1 ms**; cross-scale interaction yields **45.6 / 13.3 ms**. Decoupling the operations gives **46.4 / 12.2 ms**. Restricting attention to the coarsest feature map gives **46.8 / 7.9 ms**. The complete hybrid encoder reaches **47.9 / 9.3 ms** [21]. This is a shorter ablation training setting, so its AP should not be substituted into the main table.

The lesson is stronger than “attention improves accuracy.” Where attention is applied determines its cost, and a hybrid can preserve useful interaction while avoiding expensive redundant processing. The final encoder is not the cheapest row, but it provides a better measured accuracy–latency compromise.

Table 4 compares query-selection training: **47.9 AP** for vanilla selection and **48.7 AP** for uncertainty-minimal selection in its ablation setting [21]. The associated score/IoU plots support the explanation that selection improves initialization quality. They do not show that selected queries never miss small objects or that uncertainty is calibrated in a deployment domain.

#### Conclusion

RT-DETR makes the NMS-free alternative concrete: control the encoder's expensive token interactions, fuse scales efficiently, and initialize a compact decoder from useful image features. It gives a clear counterpart to YOLOv4's dense-head recipe. The two systems address the same detection task through different prediction and assignment structures.

#### Problems

The paper explicitly identifies small-object detection as a weakness relative to some contemporary YOLO comparisons. Coarse semantic attention, finite query counts and downsampling do not remove the need to preserve tiny-object evidence. Dense scenes may also contain more annotated objects than a fixed query budget can represent.

Deployment support matters. Deformable sampling in the decoder may be harder to export efficiently than standard convolutions. A fast TensorRT result does not establish the same advantage on a CPU, mobile accelerator or unsupported operator set. RT-DETRv2 directly addresses part of this issue.

Finally, pretrained backbone data, training length, image resolution and precision all affect the comparison. Reproduce AP and latency with the **same exported model artifact**, rather than taking AP from a training framework and latency from a quantized graph whose outputs have not been checked. This lesson becomes especially clear in the later RF-DETR study.

### RT-DETRv2: a bag of freebies for RT-DETR

Lv et al.'s 2024 technical report keeps RT-DETR's basic architecture and asks how to make it more practical to train and deploy [22]. It is a useful example of improving a detector without presenting every gain as a new backbone.

#### Challenge

The first RT-DETR gives a strong speed–accuracy trade-off, but its decoder samples the same number of points at every feature scale, its interpolation operator is not equally convenient on all deployment targets, and different model sizes share training choices that may not suit their pretrained backbones. These are separate problems: flexibility of feature extraction, operator support, and optimization.

Calling a change a “freebie” also requires a timing boundary. A training schedule can leave inference unchanged. Replacing a sampling operator changes the deployed computation and may change AP. The report includes both kinds of change, and they should not be conflated.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperRtdetrv2Recipe} alt="Teaching summary — the upper route changes decoder sampling, while the lower route changes training. The optional rounded lookup requires its specific fine-tuning rule." caption="Teaching summary — the upper route changes decoder sampling, while the lower route changes training. The optional rounded lookup requires its specific fine-tuning rule." />

**Unequal sampling budgets.** In decoder deformable attention, each query predicts offsets and attention weights for sampled image features. RT-DETRv2 permits a different number of points at each feature scale. The output remains a weighted sum of sampled features, but computation can be allocated unevenly. The total number of sampled points also depends on the number of queries, attention heads and decoder layers. Reducing only the per-scale setting does not describe the complete sampling cost.

**Optional discrete sampling.** Standard `grid_sample` evaluates predicted fractional coordinates through bilinear interpolation. A discrete alternative rounds sampling offsets and looks up nearby discrete feature positions. This can remove an inconvenient interpolation operator from the deployed graph, but rounding is non-differentiable. The report first trains with continuous sampling, then switches to discrete sampling for fine-tuning while stopping gradients to the offset-prediction parameters. It does not pretend ordinary rounding provides useful coordinate gradients.

The distinction can be written for a one-dimensional illustration as $v_{\mathrm{disc}}=F[\operatorname{round}(u)]$, versus an interpolation of neighboring values for $v_{\mathrm{cont}}$. Here $F$ is a feature array and $u$ a predicted coordinate. Real attention uses two-dimensional, bounded feature maps and learned weights; this expression explains the operator choice, not a replacement implementation of the entire decoder.

**Dynamic augmentation.** Strong augmentation is useful early in training, but the final model should fit the distribution it will encounter at evaluation. The proposed schedule disables random photometric distortion, zoom-out, IoU crop and multi-scale input in the last two epochs. This is a specified schedule change, rather than a blanket rule that augmentation should always disappear near the end of every training run.

**Scale-dependent optimization.** The backbone learning rate depends on model size and pretrained feature quality. Table 1 uses backbone learning rates from $10^{-4}$ for the ResNet18 model down to $10^{-6}$ for the ResNet101 model, while keeping the detector learning rate at $10^{-4}$ [22]. A stronger pretrained backbone can benefit from a gentler update. Those values describe the report's experiments, not a universal scaling law.

#### Results

Table 2 fixes the input at $640\times640$ and reports batch-one TensorRT FP16 speed on a T4. The S model improves from **46.5 to 47.9 AP** while retaining the reported **217 FPS**; M improves **48.9 to 49.9** at **161 FPS**; L improves **53.1 to 53.4** at **108 FPS** [22]. The X model remains at **54.3 AP**, demonstrating that the training changes do not improve every aggregate AP result.

Those rows use the report's main sampling configuration. Do not quote their AP as if the optional discrete operator had no accuracy cost. A model's reported family name must be accompanied by the actual sampling choice used in its exported graph.

#### Ablations

Table 3 reduces the S model's total sampled points from **86,400 to 64,800, 43,200 and 21,600**. AP changes from **47.9 to 47.8, 47.7 and 47.3** [22]. This shows a gradual trade-off in that experiment; it does not establish that all feature scales are interchangeable or that the smallest budget is always preferable.

Table 4 tests discrete sampling. The S model gives **47.4 AP / 64.8 AP50**, compared with the main continuous configuration's **47.9 / 64.9**. The L model gives **52.9 AP / 71.3 AP50**, compared with **53.4 / 71.6** [22]. AP50 changes little, but the stricter aggregate AP still falls. This is why “no noticeable AP50 reduction” must not be rewritten as “no accuracy loss.”

The report's experiments support the practical benefits of its recipe, but do not fully isolate every augmentation and learning-rate interaction. A careful reproduction should retain the exact schedule rather than assume the headline gain comes from one ingredient.

#### Conclusion

RT-DETRv2 extends YOLOv4's recipe perspective into end-to-end detection: training choices and deployment operators deserve explicit design. It makes the decoder's sampling budget adjustable and offers a concrete workaround for interpolation support, while preserving the hybrid encoder and set-prediction structure.

#### Problems

Discrete sampling reduces flexibility and needs fine-tuning; it is not a lossless algebraic conversion. Hardware may still lack fast kernels for other decoder operations. A supported graph can be slower than expected if the runtime moves unsupported subgraphs onto another device.

The most useful next experiment is therefore practical: export both sampling variants, validate their boxes and AP with the same precision, then measure the target device. The operator that looks simpler on paper need not be the fastest operator in a particular compiler.

### D-FINE: boxes as refined distributions

Peng et al.'s ICLR 2025 paper revisits what each decoder layer predicts [23]. Rather than repeatedly outputting only four box coordinates, it refines a distribution of possible corrections for each edge and uses the final layer to teach earlier layers during training.

#### Challenge

A box edge can be ambiguous: an object may be occluded, blurred or hard to separate from its background. A single coordinate hides the alternatives. Distributional regression gives the optimizer a richer intermediate representation, but a fixed, uniformly spaced distribution predicted in one shot can still be too coarse. A DETR decoder already refines objects through several layers, making it a natural place to refine these distributions as well.

The practical challenge is to improve localization without turning a real-time detector into a large teacher–student pipeline at deployment. D-FINE therefore combines a more informative box representation with self-distillation inside the training network.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperDfineSource} alt="Compare initial and refined box-side distributions; the weighting operation turns finer intermediate distributions into updated localization." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Compare initial and refined box-side distributions; the weighting operation turns finer intermediate distributions into updated localization." />

*Figure source: [23], arXiv:2410.13842v1, PDF page 5. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13842).*

**Reference box.** The first decoder layer predicts an initial box with centre $(x_0,y_0)$, width $W_0$ and height $H_0$. It also initializes distributions for the four edges. Subsequent layers predict corrections relative to that initial geometry, rather than treating every layer's box as an unrelated output.

**Residual distribution refinement.** For one edge, let $z^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb R^{N+1}$ be its logits at decoder layer $\ell$. The refinement has the form

$$
z^{(\ell)}=z^{(\ell-1)}+\Delta z^{(\ell)},\qquad
p_n^{(\ell)}=\operatorname{softmax}(z^{(\ell)})_n.
$$

$\Delta z^{(\ell)}$ is the newly predicted residual vector, $N+1$ the number of bins, and $p_n^{(\ell)}$ the probability assigned to bin $n$. Residuals are added to **logits**, before normalization; adding probability vectors would not implement this operation.

**Nonuniform corrections.** The expected correction is $r^{(\ell)}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}w_n p_n^{(\ell)}$, where $w_n$ is the signed correction represented by bin $n$. D-FINE uses a nonuniform weighting function so that the representation can describe small adjustments finely while retaining a wider correction range. The corresponding edge distance is the initial distance plus the expected correction scaled by $H_0$ for a vertical edge or $W_0$ for a horizontal edge. These dimensions make the correction relative to object size.

Four edge distributions therefore require $4(N+1)$ logits per query, rather than four scalar box outputs. The final boxes are still ordinary rectangles obtained from the expected corrections. The distributions are an optimization representation; their entropy is not automatically a calibrated measure of real-world uncertainty.

**Global Optimal Localization Self-Distillation.** GO-LSD transfers refined localization information from the last decoder layer to earlier layers using distributional supervision. Earlier layers learn from a richer target than a hard coordinate alone. Improved early predictions also leave smaller residual errors for later layers, which explains the intended mutual reinforcement. The final layer is a teacher inside the same training network; an additional external teacher does not run at inference.

**Efficient detector around the head.** The paper also simplifies projections, fusion and decoder-related operations, and adjusts sampling points. These changes reduce the cost of the underlying real-time DETR, sometimes at an accuracy cost. FDR and GO-LSD are then added to recover and improve accuracy. Consequently, the final model's speed difference from RT-DETR cannot be attributed solely to predicting distributions.

#### Results

In the manuscript's main COCO results, D-FINE-L reaches **54.0 AP at 124 FPS**, and D-FINE-X reaches **55.8 AP at 78 FPS**, measured on a T4 in the paper's TensorRT FP16 setting [23]. Objects365 pretraining raises the corresponding AP results to **57.1 and 59.3**. These are separate data regimes, not four equivalent COCO-only experiments.

The useful comparison is the full curve of accuracy and latency at the desired model size. A stronger pretrained detector is not evidence that distribution refinement alone contributes the entire gap. Likewise, results from the later RF-DETR evaluation use a different export and timing protocol and should retain their own context.

#### Ablations

Table 3 traces the efficiency modifications and head additions. After the listed architecture and training changes, the baseline reaches **53.0 AP**. Adding FDR gives **53.5**, then GO-LSD gives **54.0** [23]. The incremental changes support both components, while the earlier rows show that the recipe includes much more than the box head.

Table 4 varies the distribution and distillation hyperparameters. With **4, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128** bins in that experiment, AP is **53.3, 53.4, 53.5, 53.7, 53.6 and 53.6** [23]. More bins do not monotonically improve AP. Table 5 compares distillation strategies under its own settings: its baseline is **53.0**, baseline plus FDR **53.8**, and GO-LSD **54.5**. Those values come from a distinct experiment; do not splice its 54.5 into Table 3's progression.

#### Conclusion

D-FINE uses the iterative structure of a DETR decoder to refine a richer geometric representation. The important idea is the combination of residual distribution updates and training-time localization supervision, while keeping the final deployed prediction an ordinary box.

#### Problems

More expressive distributions require extra logits and careful numerics. Nonuniform bin design, correction scales and distillation temperature become choices to validate. A sharply peaked distribution can still be confidently wrong under domain shift, and an occluded object's annotation may itself be uncertain.

Finally, exporting and quantizing the actual distributional head deserves explicit verification. Softmax, expectations and residual accumulation can behave differently at lower precision. Validate AP after export instead of assuming that a graph which runs quickly preserves the training-framework result.

### DEIM: dense one-to-one matching

Huang et al.'s CVPR 2025 paper increases the amount and usefulness of positive supervision while retaining one-to-one set prediction [24]. Its central move is simple: create more annotated object instances in a training image, rather than assign many predictions to each original object.

#### Challenge

A dense detector can train several predictions for one target. A DETR-style one-to-one assignment gives each target one matched query in a matching group. That structure helps avoid NMS, but it also means a training image with few objects supplies few positive assignments. Matching may be especially unhelpful early in training, when predicted boxes are poor.

DEIM separates **quantity** from **quality**. Dense O2O increases target instances. Matchability-Aware Loss changes how matched predictions are trained. Neither component replaces the matching structure with ordinary one-to-many detection.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperDeimSource} alt="Dense one-to-one matching changes how positive examples are obtained, while the lower plots compare losses for different match qualities." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Dense one-to-one matching changes how positive examples are obtained, while the lower plots compare losses for different match qualities." />

*Figure source: [24], arXiv:2412.04234v3, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04234).*

**Dense O2O through augmentation.** Mosaic combines images spatially; Mixup overlays image content. Their transformed annotations create more target instances in the resulting training example. Matching remains one-to-one for those instances. Four targets may produce four positive matches, rather than four predictions being assigned to a single target. This distinction is why denser supervision does not by itself reintroduce the need for NMS.

All geometric transformations must also transform boxes and remove invalid or excessively cropped annotations consistently. More targets are not useful if their coordinates are wrong. The augmentation probability and when augmentation is disabled are part of the method, not incidental loader details.

**Matchability-Aware Loss.** MAL aims to provide better learning signals for low-quality matched boxes. For a class probability $p\in(0,1)$, matched-box IoU $q\in[0,1]$ and a positive-match indicator $y$, its form is

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MAL}}(p,q,y)=
\begin{cases}
-q^\gamma\log p-(1-q^\gamma)\log(1-p), & y=1,\\
-p^\gamma\log(1-p), & y=0.
\end{cases}
$$

$\gamma$ controls the target transformation and negative modulation. A positive prediction receives the soft target $q^\gamma$; an unmatched negative receives a focal-style penalty. Compared with the Varifocal Loss used in its baseline [73], MAL changes both the positive target and the weighting. It should not be described as ordinary binary cross-entropy with every positive target set to one.

The intended effect is better supervision for imperfect matches. IoU is used during training, when a target box is available; the detector does not need ground truth to score its deployment predictions. As with other quality-aware losses, the score's interpretation depends on training and is not guaranteed calibration.

**Schedule and implementation.** The paper combines its loss with an augmentation schedule and a flat-cosine learning-rate schedule. Efficient cached Mosaic and within-batch Mixup limit the overhead of constructing denser images. Strong augmentation is disabled partway through training to return the model toward the evaluation distribution. The final inference graph remains that of the underlying detector.

#### Results

The paper evaluates DEIM on both RT-DETRv2 and D-FINE rather than introducing one fixed architecture. In Table 6, the RT-DETRv2-R50 baseline reaches **53.4 AP in 72 epochs**, while the combined DEIM recipe reaches **53.9 in 36 epochs**. For D-FINE-L, the corresponding values are **54.0 in 72 epochs** and **54.6 in 36 epochs** [24]. The comparison is deliberately about faster convergence as well as accuracy.

Table 7 also gives training time: one epoch is approximately **1.181 GPU-hours** for the baseline and **1.183** with DEIM on the stated single-4090 setup. A separate comparison reaches **54.3 AP in about 71 hours over 60 epochs**, versus the baseline's **53.4 AP in about 85 hours over 72 epochs** [24]. Epoch count alone would miss this distinction, although these particular per-epoch costs are nearly equal.

#### Ablations

Table 4 varies Mosaic and Mixup. At 12 epochs, the no-augmentation row gives **49.6 AP**; Mosaic alone gives **50.4**, Mixup alone **50.1**, and both **50.4**. At 24 epochs the corresponding results are **51.7, 51.9, 51.5 and 52.5** [24]. Mixup alone can hurt the later result, so the evidence does not support “every way of adding targets always helps.”

Table 6 isolates the loss contribution on top of Dense O2O. At 36 epochs, the R50 model with Dense O2O gives **53.6 AP**, and adding MAL gives **53.9**. D-FINE-L changes from **54.2 to 54.6** in the analogous rows. These support complementary benefits in the tested settings, not a universal gain on every dataset or model.

#### Conclusion

DEIM improves the supervision reaching an existing set predictor. It shows that image construction and loss design can accelerate learning without adding inference modules. That makes it a direct modern counterpart to YOLOv4's training-only recipe improvements.

#### Problems

Artificially dense images can differ from the deployment distribution. Cropping and blending may create unrealistic occlusion, tiny instances or annotation conflicts. Query capacity remains finite: adding targets beyond that capacity cannot create unlimited useful positive matches.

A fair reproduction must report the full augmentation schedule, training cost and underlying architecture. “DEIM” denotes a training method applied to a detector; it is not enough information to determine parameter count, latency or pretrained data.

### RF-DETR: architecture search on a DINOv2 backbone

Robinson et al.'s ICLR 2026 paper treats detector design and benchmarking as connected problems [25]. It adapts a pretrained visual backbone, trains a family of subnetworks through weight sharing and selects configurations that fit deployment constraints.

#### Challenge

A detector that works well on COCO does not necessarily adapt well to a new set of object classes. Conversely, a very large pretrained system may be too slow for the intended device. RF-DETR asks how to preserve useful pretrained visual features while searching for a practical detector architecture.

The paper also challenges a common evaluation shortcut: reporting accuracy from a full-precision training model and latency from a separately exported or quantized model. If the exported model loses accuracy, the two numbers do not describe one deployable detector. This is an important part of the contribution, not merely a benchmarking footnote.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperRfdetrSource} alt="Follow backbone features through the projector and query decoder; the separate search-space choices determine which architecture is timed and evaluated." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Follow backbone features through the projector and query decoder; the separate search-space choices determine which architecture is timed and evaluated." />

*Figure source: [25], arXiv:2511.09554v2, PDF page 4. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.09554).*

**Pretrained backbone.** RF-DETR builds on DINOv2 [66]. The detector uses a vision backbone with a mixture of windowed and non-windowed attention to control computation while retaining broader interactions. A projection stage turns its features into the inputs required by the detection decoder. Gentler optimization and normalization choices aim to preserve pretrained information during detection training.

**Set-prediction head.** Queries interact with image features through a compact decoder and predict classes and boxes. One-to-one matching is used within each training query group. Multiple groups provide additional training supervision, while deployment uses the selected inference structure. The grouping is not a request to run all training groups and suppress duplicates with NMS at test time.

**Weight-sharing search.** Rather than train every architecture from scratch, a shared training model supports different configurations. The search varies choices such as image resolution, patch size, attention-window arrangements and decoder settings. Candidate subnetworks inherit learned weights, allowing accuracy and measured runtime to be assessed at lower search cost than independent full training for every candidate.

Conceptually, the selection is a constrained optimization:

$$
a^*=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal A}\operatorname{AP}(a)
\quad\text{subject to}\quad T_{\mathrm{device}}(a)\leq\tau.
$$

$\mathcal A$ is the supported architecture search space, $a$ one candidate, $T_{\mathrm{device}}$ its latency on the chosen runtime and device, and $\tau$ the deployment budget. This summarizes the design objective; it is not a claim that the paper exhaustively solves a global optimization over all neural networks. Shared weights also make candidate rankings approximate until the selected model is checked.

**Segmentation extension.** The paper extends the detector to instance segmentation with a mask head. Box detection and segmentation share features but have different outputs and costs. A segmentation benchmark must time conversion into usable instance masks, rather than only generating intermediate prototypes.

**Benchmark the artifact.** RF-DETR evaluates compiled models with accuracy and latency measured from the same artifact. It examines precision, NMS settings, export details and GPU power behavior. Its latency protocol inserts a **200 ms buffer between forward passes** to reduce thermal and power-related variation [25]. The paper explicitly says this measures reproducible per-pass latency rather than sustained throughput. That qualification must remain attached to the result.

#### Results

In Table 1, RF-DETR-M gives **54.7 AP / 4.4 ms** in the buffered FP16 evaluation, compared with **54.8 / 20.5 ms** in FP32 [25]. These are not two interchangeable precision modes with identical outputs; both accuracy and latency are measured.

The paper also evaluates transfer across the 100 datasets in its RF100-VL benchmark. Those results address adaptation beyond COCO, while still depending on the selected tasks, fine-tuning settings and evaluation protocol. They do not establish performance on every deployment domain. The benchmark is introduced and documented in the same primary source [25].

#### Ablations

Table 5 is a useful sequence to read closely. Its starting detector gives **52.6 AP**. Gentler hyperparameters reduce that to **51.6**; replacing the backbone with DINOv2 raises it to **53.6**; additional Objects365 pretraining gives **54.3**; weight-sharing architecture search gives **54.6** [25]. The shared training recipe and pretrained data therefore matter alongside the search procedure.

The sequence does not prove that a backbone swap alone will give the same gain under another optimization recipe. Nor should the final model's whole improvement be attributed to search. Later rows alter architecture settings and should be read with their own latency and parameter columns.

Table 1 highlights a separate reproducibility issue: export choices can drastically alter a distributional detector at FP16. The paper corrects its D-FINE export using ONNX opset 17 before drawing the comparison [25]. That is evidence about the tested export path, not a claim that D-FINE inherently cannot operate at half precision.

#### Conclusion

RF-DETR combines reusable pretrained visual features, an efficient set predictor and deployment-aware architecture selection. Its broader lesson is that the architecture and the exported executable must be evaluated together.

#### Problems

Search quality depends on the supported configurations, shared-weight training and the target runtime. A configuration selected on one GPU may not remain optimal on a CPU or another compiler. Pretraining and search also add costs that are absent from inference latency.

Buffered latency does not measure a continuously loaded video pipeline. Reproduce sustained throughput, memory use and end-to-end delay separately when those are the actual product constraints. Finally, “pretrained” does not mean open-vocabulary detection: the supervised class head and adaptation procedure still determine which classes the deployed model recognizes.

### DEIMv2: DINOv3 features for real-time DETRs

Huang et al.'s *Real-Time Object Detection Meets DINOv3* technical report develops a family of detectors spanning larger Transformer backbones and very small convolutional models [26]. The discussion here follows its revised 2026 manuscript; the first preprint appeared in 2025.

#### Challenge

DINOv3 [67] supplies strong pretrained visual features, but a plain vision Transformer naturally produces a single main spatial resolution. Detection needs both semantics and fine detail across object scales. Simply inserting a large backbone does not establish a useful real-time detector, especially for very small deployment budgets.

DEIMv2 therefore has two related challenges: adapt pretrained Transformer features efficiently for the larger models, and design a different, compact path for the smallest models. It does not force every size to use the same backbone family.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperDeimv2Source} alt="DINOv3 features interact with spatial tuning and bidirectional fusion; distinguish adapting the backbone from replacing the detector's matching loss." caption="Original paper figure 2 — DINOv3 features interact with spatial tuning and bidirectional fusion; distinguish adapting the backbone from replacing the detector's matching loss." />

*Figure source: [26], arXiv:2509.20787v5, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.20787).*

**Larger variants.** The L and X configurations use DINOv3 ViT-Small and ViT-Small+ backbones. The S and M configurations use smaller backbones distilled from DINOv3. They retain pretrained semantic structure while changing the hidden width and downstream detector to fit a smaller budget. Pretraining and distillation remain part of the training history, even though only the resulting student runs at inference.

**Spatial Tuning Adapter.** STA combines two information paths. Intermediate Transformer block features at stride 16 are resized to the scales required by detection. A lightweight convolutional path extracts local detail from the image through fast downsampling. The Bi-Fusion operation combines these detail features with resized semantic features using channel projections and lightweight operations.

For example, a feature tensor $F\in\mathbb R^{H/16\times W/16\times D}$ can be resized to $H/8\times W/8$, $H/16\times W/16$ and $H/32\times W/32$ before fusion. $H,W$ are the input dimensions and $D$ the channel count. Resizing itself can be parameter-free, but the **complete adapter is not parameter-free**: its detail path and projections contain learned parameters. Upsampling also does not recreate image details absent from the semantic features; the parallel path supplies complementary evidence.

**Smallest variants.** Nano, Pico, Femto and Atto instead use progressively pruned HGNetv2 convolutional backbones. The report removes stages, reduces block counts and reduces channels as the budget shrinks. Their queries, hidden dimensions and resolutions also differ. Calling every variant a DINOv3-powered detector would therefore misdescribe the actual family.

**Decoder and training.** The report simplifies decoder operations, including its feed-forward and normalization layers, and shares a positional embedding across decoder layers. The motivation is that iterative box refinement often changes query positions only modestly. Training builds on Dense O2O and MAL from DEIM and introduces Copy-Blend to combine inserted objects with existing content rather than fully overwrite regions.

Its full training objective combines classification, localization and distillation terms:

$$
\mathcal L=\lambda_1\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MAL}}
+\lambda_2\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FGL}}
+\lambda_3\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DDF}}
+\lambda_4\mathcal L_{1}
+\lambda_5\mathcal L_{\mathrm{GIoU}}.
$$

The $\lambda_i$ are loss weights; MAL is Matchability-Aware Loss, FGL fine-grained localization supervision, DDF distributional distillation, $\mathcal L_1$ a box-coordinate loss and GIoU the generalized overlap loss [24], [23], [44]. The revised report excludes FGL and DDF for Pico, Femto and Atto because these terms hurt its tested ultra-small models. The equation is a family template, not evidence that all terms are active for every size.

#### Results

Table 1 of the revised manuscript reports **57.8 AP / 50.26 million parameters / 13.75 ms** for X, **56.0 / 32.18 / 10.47** for L, **53.0 / 18.11 / 8.80** for M and **50.9 / 9.71 / 5.78** for S [26]. Preserve the manuscript's model and evaluation settings when using these values. The introduction contains an inconsistent 57.6 figure for X; the values here follow the explicit results table rather than silently mixing versions.

At the smallest end, Table 4 reports **38.5 AP** for Pico at $640\times640$, **31.0** for Femto at $416\times416$ and **23.8** for Atto at $320\times320$ [26]. These different input sizes are part of the comparison. Parameter count alone does not capture the accuracy–latency trade-off.

#### Ablations

This technical report provides configuration and component observations rather than a complete factorial ablation of every change. It reports that removing FGL and DDF helps its ultra-small variants, and that Copy-Blend improves its tested setup. Without a fully controlled table for each ingredient, those statements should remain scoped to the reported experiments.

The scale-specific breakdown also tempers the headline: the report says gains from DINOv3-based models are strongest on medium and large objects, while small-object gains are more limited. A semantic backbone improvement should not automatically be described as recovering fine detail or solving tiny-object detection.

#### Conclusion

DEIMv2 shows how to adapt a strong visual representation while preserving a separate local-detail path, and when to use a different backbone for a much smaller budget. Its family design is more informative than treating a single largest-model AP as the whole contribution.

#### Problems

The report spans multiple manuscript revisions, and configuration names alone may hide changed results. Match the paper version, checkpoint and export settings. The authors also state that latency has not been fully optimized, so the listed timings should not be treated as a hardware-independent ranking.

Finally, distillation, backbone pretraining, adapter design and augmentation all influence the result. Reproducing only the adapter on an unrelated backbone does not reproduce DEIMv2. For a new application, test small-object errors, memory and the actual compiled graph before choosing a variant.
