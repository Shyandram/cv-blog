## The big picture

Most enhancement networks in [DLCV 05](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-05-low-light-enhancement/) are judged by how good their pictures look. This chapter adds a second requirement: the model must run on a phone, inside a camera, or on every frame of a video, often at 4K resolution, within a few milliseconds and a small power budget. That requirement shapes the design more than any loss function does.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Imagine you must recolor a huge mural, and you are allowed only a tiny helper. A slow way is to have the helper repaint every brick. A fast way is to have the helper look at a small photo of the mural and write a short recipe card, which a simple machine then follows brick by brick. Lightweight enhancers differ in the kind of card they write. Some write a brightening curve ("dark values go up a lot, bright values a little"). Some write a map of where the light is weak ("the left wall is in shadow, lift it more"). Some are small all-round painters that are just very quick. And some, borrowed from photo-editing apps, write a color table or a grid of small color recipes. In every case the hard thinking happens once, on something small, and the per-brick work is simple.
</Callout>

Why it matters:

- **Every pixel, every frame.** A phone camera preview or a video pipeline enhances tens of frames per second. A model that is fine for one photo on a server can be far too slow, too hot or too memory-hungry for that.
- **Resolution keeps growing.** A 4K frame has 8.3 million pixels and a 12-megapixel photo 12 million. Most of the cost of a full-resolution convolutional network grows in proportion to the number of pixels, so a network that is cheap at 512 × 512 can be 30 times more expensive on a 4K frame.
- **Enhancement has structure we can exploit.** Unlike denoising or super-resolution, tone and color enhancement mostly *rearranges values that are already there*. That one observation leads to designs that cost a few hundred arithmetic operations per pixel instead of tens of thousands.

This chapter builds on DLCV 05 (Retinex, Zero-DCE curves, low-light datasets and evaluation) and on the classical tools of [IP 03](/en/learn/image-processing/dip-03-intensity-spatial-filtering/) (intensity transformations, the guided and bilateral filters). It does not repeat them; it explains how they become small, fast learned models.

## What "lightweight" means

**Plain version.** "Lightweight" is not one number. A model can be small on disk but slow, fast on a GPU but slow on a phone, or cheap in arithmetic but limited by memory. You need to say which cost you mean, at which resolution and on which device.

**Precise version.** Five costs matter for an enhancement model. The general background (MACs versus FLOPs, why FLOPs are not latency, a latency protocol) is in [ML 10](/en/learn/ml/ml-10-evaluation-deployment/#efficiency-flops-memory-and-latency); here is what each means for enhancement.

1. **Parameters** $P$: the number of learned values. They decide the size of the model file and the weight memory. Zero-DCE++ has about 10K parameters [1]; the U-Net-like design counted below has about 1.9 million.
2. **Arithmetic** per image, counted in multiply–accumulates. For a convolution layer,

$$
\mathrm{MACs} = H_{\text{out}}\, W_{\text{out}}\; k^2\, C_{\text{in}}\, C_{\text{out}},
$$

where $H_{\text{out}} \times W_{\text{out}}$ is the size of the output feature map, $k \times k$ the kernel size, and $C_{\text{in}}$, $C_{\text{out}}$ the numbers of input and output channels. One MAC is one multiplication and one addition, so 1 MAC ≈ 2 FLOPs; state which unit you use.
3. **MACs per pixel** (or per megapixel): the arithmetic divided by the number of *input* pixels $HW$. This is the fairest single number for enhancement, because the same model is applied at very different resolutions. A layer that runs at full resolution contributes $k^2 C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}}$ MACs per pixel; a layer that runs on a fixed-size thumbnail contributes almost nothing per pixel at 4K.
4. **Memory traffic.** Every feature map has to be written to memory and read back by the next layer. A feature map with $C$ channels at full resolution occupies $H W C b$ bytes, where $b$ is the bytes per value (2 for 16-bit floating point, fp16). On phones and embedded chips, moving these bytes often costs more time and energy than the arithmetic.
5. **Latency and energy** on the target device: the measured time from input frame to output frame, and the energy per frame. These are what users feel, and they can only be measured, not computed.

**Designed small, not made small.** The models in this chapter are *designed* to be small: their structure (a curve, an illumination map, a thin network, a table) is chosen so that they are cheap from the start. Making an existing large model smaller after training, by pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation or neural architecture search for a smaller network, is **model compression**, a separate topic covered in [DLCV 01C](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-01c-model-compression/). The two can be combined, but a lightweight enhancer is not necessarily a compressed model.

The code below counts the first four costs for three designs, by pure arithmetic: (1) a seven-layer, 32-channel CNN that runs at full resolution, in the spirit of the DCE-Net of Zero-DCE [2] (the real DCE-Net has skip connections, so its count differs); (2) a U-Net-like restorer [3] with four levels and 32 to 256 channels; and (3) an HDRNet-like design [4] in which a small CNN looks at a fixed 256 × 256 copy of the image and predicts a 16 × 16 × 8 grid of color transforms, followed by a cheap per-pixel step that applies them (explained in the bilateral-grid section).

```python
def conv(cin, cout, k=3, scale=1.0, fixed=None):
    """A k x k convolution on (scale x the input pixels), or on a fixed number of pixels."""
    return dict(params=k * k * cin * cout + cout, macs=k * k * cin * cout, cout=cout, scale=scale, fixed=fixed)

def cost(layers, H, W, per_pixel_apply=0):
    """Parameters, multiply-accumulates (MACs) and the largest feature map (fp16 bytes)."""
    px = [l["fixed"] or H * W * l["scale"] for l in layers]
    macs = sum(l["macs"] * p for l, p in zip(layers, px)) + per_pixel_apply * H * W
    peak = max(2 * l["cout"] * p for l, p in zip(layers, px))
    return sum(l["params"] for l in layers), macs, peak

# (1) a seven-layer, 32-channel CNN at full resolution (in the spirit of DCE-Net)
tiny = [conv(3, 32)] + [conv(32, 32) for _ in range(5)] + [conv(32, 24)]
# (2) a U-Net-like restorer: 4 levels, 32-64-128-256 channels, two convs per level
unet = [conv(3, 32), conv(32, 32), conv(32, 64, scale=1/4), conv(64, 64, scale=1/4),
        conv(64, 128, scale=1/16), conv(128, 128, scale=1/16),
        conv(128, 256, scale=1/64), conv(256, 256, scale=1/64),
        conv(256 + 128, 128, scale=1/16), conv(128, 128, scale=1/16),
        conv(128 + 64, 64, scale=1/4), conv(64, 64, scale=1/4),
        conv(64 + 32, 32), conv(32, 32), conv(32, 3, k=1)]
# (3) HDRNet-like: a CNN on a fixed 256 x 256 copy predicts a 16x16x8 grid of 3x4 affine maps
grid_net = [conv(3, 8, fixed=128**2), conv(8, 16, fixed=64**2), conv(16, 32, fixed=32**2),
            conv(32, 64, fixed=16**2), conv(64, 64, fixed=16**2), conv(64, 64, fixed=16**2),
            conv(64, 96, k=1, fixed=16**2)]
apply = 9 + 48 + 8 * 12 + 12   # guide (3x3 matrix + 16-piece curves), trilinear slice, affine

for H, W, name in [(1080, 1920, "1080p"), (2160, 3840, "4K")]:
    print(f"--- {name}: {H * W / 1e6:.2f} megapixels")
    for label, layers, extra in [("tiny full-res CNN", tiny, 0), ("U-Net-like", unet, 0),
                                 ("low-res grid + slicing", grid_net, apply)]:
        p, m, peak = cost(layers, H, W, extra)
        print(f"{label:23s} params {p:>9,d}  GMACs {m / 1e9:7.1f}  MACs/pixel {m / (H * W):8.0f}"
              f"  largest feature map {peak / 2**20:7.2f} MiB")
```

```text

tiny full-res CNN       params    54,072  GMACs   111.7  MACs/pixel    53856  largest feature map  126.56 MiB
U-Net-like              params 1,946,947  GMACs   336.4  MACs/pixel   162240  largest feature map  126.56 MiB
low-res grid + slicing  params   104,624  GMACs     0.4  MACs/pixel      183  largest feature map    0.25 MiB
--- 4K: 8.29 megapixels
tiny full-res CNN       params    54,072  GMACs   446.7  MACs/pixel    53856  largest feature map  506.25 MiB
U-Net-like              params 1,946,947  GMACs  1345.7  MACs/pixel   162240  largest feature map  506.25 MiB
low-res grid + slicing  params   104,624  GMACs     1.4  MACs/pixel      170  largest feature map    0.25 MiB
```

Three lessons are in these numbers (all from our own arithmetic, not from any paper):

- **Parameters mislead.** The "tiny" CNN has half the parameters of the grid design, yet it needs about 300 times more arithmetic, because every one of its layers runs at full resolution. Parameter count says almost nothing about the cost of a full-resolution network.
- **Resolution multiplies everything at full resolution.** Going from 1080p to 4K multiplies the MACs of designs (1) and (2) by exactly 4, the ratio of pixel counts. The grid design grows only through its small per-pixel step.
- **Memory traffic can be the real limit.** One 32-channel fp16 feature map at 4K is $3840 \times 2160 \times 32 \times 2$ bytes, about 506 MiB, and a full-resolution network writes and reads several of them per frame. The grid design's largest feature map is a quarter of a mebibyte.

## Why enhancement suits small models

**Plain version.** When you brighten or recolor a photo, the edges, textures and objects stay exactly where they were. Only the *values* change, and they change in a smooth, predictable way: similar colors in the same neighborhood get similar treatment. So a model does not have to redraw the image; it only has to describe how to remap it.

**Precise version.** Write the enhanced image as a pixel-wise function of the input,

$$
O(\mathbf{x}) = T_{\mathbf{x}}\big(I(\mathbf{x})\big),
$$

where $\mathbf{x}$ is the pixel position, $I(\mathbf{x}) \in [0, 1]^3$ the input RGB value, $O(\mathbf{x})$ the output value, and $T_{\mathbf{x}} : [0,1]^3 \to [0,1]^3$ a color transform that may depend on the position. Two assumptions make $T$ cheap to represent:

1. **Smooth in space.** $T_{\mathbf{x}}$ changes slowly with $\mathbf{x}$, except across edges of the image. Lighting, exposure and a photographer's local edits are all low-frequency.
2. **Smooth in color.** For a fixed position, $T_{\mathbf{x}}$ is a smooth function of the color, so a few parameters (a curve, a gain, a $3 \times 4$ matrix, a coarse table) describe it.

Under these assumptions $T$ has far fewer degrees of freedom than the image. It can be **predicted at low resolution** by a small network and then **applied at full resolution** by a fixed, cheap rule. The image's fine detail is never processed by the network at all; it passes through $T$ untouched, which is why these methods keep edges sharp even though their networks see only a thumbnail. The total cost of such a predict-then-apply design is

$$
\mathrm{cost} = C_{\text{net}}(h \times w) + c_{\text{apply}} \cdot H W,
$$

where $C_{\text{net}}(h \times w)$ is the cost of the network on an $h \times w$ thumbnail, which does not depend on the full resolution $H \times W$, and $c_{\text{apply}}$ is the per-pixel cost of applying the transform (165 MACs in our HDRNet-like count; for a 3D lookup table, about 24 multiply–adds for the 8-corner blend plus the weights). At 4K the second term dominates, and it is small. The rule to remember: **upsample the transform** (curve parameters, an illumination map, affine coefficients, table weights), **never the output image**.

**When the assumptions fail.** Denoising, deblurring, super-resolution and inpainting must create or remove fine structure, so they are *not* smooth remappings, and these tricks do not apply to them. Low-light enhancement is in between: the tone part is a smooth remapping, but the noise part is not, which is the split between tone enhancement and detail restoration described in [DLCV 05](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-05-low-light-enhancement/#goal-and-difficulty). Keep this limit in mind for every method below: a fast transform can brighten noise, but it cannot remove it.

## A map of lightweight enhancement

**Plain version.** Two questions place any method on the map. *What does the network predict?* A curve, an illumination map, the image itself, or a color table or grid. That is its **design lineage**. *What does it learn from?* Paired photos, rewards, unpaired photos, no reference at all, or a mix. That is its **learning strategy**. The two questions are independent: a curve can be learned with or without references.

**Learning strategy.** The survey of deep low-light image enhancement (LLIE) by Li et al. [5] sorts methods by learning strategy into five groups, and this chapter uses its terms:

- **Supervised learning (SL):** trained on pairs of low-light and reference images. The survey further divides it into *end-to-end* networks, *deep Retinex-based* methods, and *realistic-data-driven* methods (which build new real or more realistic training data).
- **Reinforcement learning (RL):** an agent chooses adjustments and is trained with a reward instead of a reference image.
- **Unsupervised learning (UL):** trained with *unpaired* low-light and normal-light images, for example with an adversarial loss.
- **Zero-shot learning (ZSL):** needs neither paired nor unpaired data; it learns from low-light images alone with non-reference losses, or even from the test image itself. The survey stresses that "zero-shot" here means only this, not its meaning in recognition.
- **Semi-supervised learning (SSL):** combines a supervised stage on paired data with an unsupervised stage.

The survey covers methods up to 2021. For later methods the strategy below comes from the method's own paper, and terms vary between papers: SCI, for example, describes Zero-DCE and RUAS as "unsupervised" [6], while the survey files them under zero-shot learning [5].

**Design lineage.** The main sections of this chapter follow four lineages, in the order of the table below: curve and mapping estimation; Retinex and illumination estimation; lightweight end-to-end networks and Transformers; and real-time retouching transforms (bilateral grids and learnable 3D lookup tables), a line that comes from photo retouching and was later applied to low light.

| Method | Lineage | Learning strategy | What the network predicts | How it is applied at full resolution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zero-DCE [2] | Curve | ZSL (survey) | Per-pixel curve parameters for 8 iterations | Iterated quadratic curve, per pixel |
| Zero-DCE++ [1] | Curve | ZSL (survey) | 3 curve-parameter maps on a downsampled input | Maps resized, curve iterated per pixel |
| ExCNet [7] | Curve | ZSL (survey) | One S-curve per image, fitted on the test image | Curve on a guided-filter base layer, detail added back |
| DeepExposure [8] | Mapping | RL (survey) | An exposure for each sub-image | Exposed versions blended |
| LightenNet [9] | Retinex | SL (survey) | An illumination map | Input divided by the illumination |
| DeepUPE [10] | Retinex | SL (survey) | A 3-channel illumination map, at low resolution | Bilateral-grid upsampling, then division |
| RUAS [11] | Retinex (unrolled) | ZSL (survey) | Illumination and noise through searched, unrolled stages | Retinex division and a denoising stage |
| SCI [6] | Retinex | No references ("unsupervised" in the paper) | An illumination map, with one small block at test time | Input divided by the illumination |
| CPGA-Net [12] | Physical model | SL (paper) | Transmission, light and gamma terms | Scattering-model formula and fusion |
| IAT [13] | End-to-end Transformer | SL (paper) | Per-pixel multiply and add maps; a color matrix and a gamma | Local maps, then global color matrix and gamma |
| HDRNet [4] | Retouching: bilateral grid | Paired (paper) | A 16 × 16 × 8 grid of 3 × 4 affine maps | Slicing with a guide, then a matrix product |
| Image-adaptive 3D LUT [14] | Retouching: 3D LUT | Paired or unpaired (paper) | Weights that fuse a few basis 3D LUTs | One trilinear lookup per pixel |
| AdaInt [15], SepLUT [16] | Retouching: 3D LUT | Paired (papers) | LUT weights plus node spacing, or 1D curves plus a small 3D LUT | Lookup in a non-uniform or two-stage table |
| DPLUT [17] | Curve via a LUT | "Unsupervised" (paper) | A table from color to curve parameters, plus a denoising table | Lookup, then curve |

"Survey" marks a strategy taken from the Li et al. tables [5]; "paper" marks one read from the method's own training setup. HDRNet and the LUT methods are photo-enhancement methods that the survey deliberately leaves out [5], so for them the table states only what the papers train on.

## Curve and mapping estimation

**Plain version.** The cheapest transform is a tone curve: a rule that says "input value 0.1 becomes 0.3". A network only has to choose the curve, once per image or once per pixel, and a trivial rule applies it.

**Precise version.** A **global per-channel curve** (a 1D lookup table, 1D LUT) maps each channel independently, $O_c(\mathbf{x}) = T_c(I_c(\mathbf{x}))$ for $c \in \{R, G, B\}$, where each $T_c : [0,1] \to [0,1]$ is stored as a table of, say, 256 values. The gamma curve and histogram equalization of IP 03 are curves of this kind. Applying it costs one table lookup (or one interpolation) per channel per pixel, independent of how complicated the curve is.

Global curves have two limits. They cannot treat a dark corner differently from a bright window, because they ignore position. And because each channel is mapped on its own, they cannot express transforms that mix channels, such as "make skin tones warmer but leave the sky alone".

**Per-pixel curves: Zero-DCE and Zero-DCE++.** Zero-DCE [2] predicts the parameters of an iterated quadratic curve for every pixel, which makes the curve local; its curve and its non-reference losses are explained in [DLCV 05](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-05-low-light-enhancement/#zero-reference-methods-and-curve-estimation). Its successor Zero-DCE++ [1] is the clearest lightweight design in this lineage. It uses depthwise separable convolutions, predicts only three curve-parameter maps that are reused across iterations, and estimates them on an input **downsampled by a factor of 12** (the default) before resizing the maps back to full resolution. The authors report a network of about 10K parameters and 0.115G FLOPs that runs at 1000 frames per second on a GPU and 11 on a CPU for a 1200 × 900 image [1]. The step "estimate on a thumbnail, resize the *parameter maps*, apply at full resolution" is the core idea of this chapter in its simplest form: the curve parameters are smooth in space, so resizing them loses little, while resizing the *image* would lose its detail.

**Other ways to choose a curve.** Not every curve method is fast or needs no references. ExCNet [7] fits one S-curve per back-lit image by optimizing a network on that very test image; in the survey's runtime comparison (32 images of 1200 × 900 on one NVIDIA 1080Ti GPU) it took about 23 s per image, against about 0.003 s for Zero-DCE, the fastest of the compared methods [5]. DeepExposure [8], the survey's reinforcement-learning example, lets a policy network pick an exposure for each sub-image and blends the results. DPLUT [17] (2025) stores the mapping from an input color to Zero-DCE-style curve parameters in a learned lookup table, trained with unsupervised losses, and adds a second table for noise. Speed comes from one cheap forward pass, not from the curve itself, and how the parameters are learned is a separate choice.

**The lesson of curves.** A curve is a transform that a small network can choose and a cheap rule can apply. Its weakness is expressiveness: a pixel-wise curve brightens, but it cannot tell light from surface color. The next lineage adds exactly that distinction.

## Retinex and illumination estimation

**Plain version.** Retinex theory says that what the camera records is "how much light falls on a surface" times "how much of it the surface reflects". The light part is smooth: it changes slowly across the scene. So a small network can estimate that smooth light map, even from a thumbnail, and the image is brightened by dividing out the weak light. The surface detail, which is the hard part, is never redrawn.

**Precise version.** The Retinex model (see [DLCV 05](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-05-low-light-enhancement/#retinex-theory), where the observed image is called $S$) writes the input as

$$
I(\mathbf{x}) = R(\mathbf{x}) \circ L(\mathbf{x}),
$$

where $I(\mathbf{x}) \in [0, 1]^3$ is the observed RGB value at pixel $\mathbf{x}$, $R(\mathbf{x})$ the reflectance (the surface color, which is what we want to see), $L(\mathbf{x}) \in (0, 1]$ the illumination, and $\circ$ element-wise multiplication. Once $L$ is estimated, a common way to brighten while keeping some of the original lighting is

$$
O(\mathbf{x}) = R(\mathbf{x}) \circ L(\mathbf{x})^{\gamma} = I(\mathbf{x}) \circ L(\mathbf{x})^{\gamma - 1},
$$

where $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ controls how much lighting is kept: $\gamma = 0$ outputs the reflectance alone, $\gamma = 1$ returns the input unchanged. In the language of this chapter, $T_{\mathbf{x}}$ is a per-pixel **gain** $L(\mathbf{x})^{\gamma - 1}$, and the network's only job is to estimate $L$. Because $L$ is smooth in space, it can be estimated at low resolution and resized, exactly like Zero-DCE++'s curve maps.

The snippet below checks that claim without any network. It estimates the illumination in a simple classical way: the bright channel (the per-pixel maximum of R, G and B), which LIME [18] uses as its initial estimate, smoothed here with a plain Gaussian instead of LIME's structure-aware refinement. It does so once at full resolution and once on an 8× smaller copy (1/64 of the pixels), and compares two ways of producing the full-resolution result.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter
from skimage import data, img_as_float
from skimage.metrics import peak_signal_noise_ratio as psnr
from skimage.transform import resize

dark = (0.15 * img_as_float(data.coffee()) ** 2.2) ** (1 / 2.2)   # 400 x 600, 15% of the light

def illumination(x, sigma):
    """A simple illumination estimate: the bright channel (max of R, G, B), smoothed."""
    return np.clip(gaussian_filter(x.max(axis=-1), sigma), 0.02, 1)

def relight(x, L, gamma=0.4):
    """Retinex relighting: O = R * L**gamma = I * L**(gamma - 1)."""
    return np.clip(x * L[..., None] ** (gamma - 1), 0, 1)

reference = relight(dark, illumination(dark, sigma=15))           # estimate L at full resolution
small = resize(dark, (50, 75), anti_aliasing=True)                # 8x smaller in each direction
L_small = illumination(small, sigma=15 / 8)                       # estimate L on 1/64 of the pixels
results = {
    "L at 1/8 size, resized up": relight(dark, resize(L_small, dark.shape[:2], order=1)),
    "relight at 1/8 size, resize output": resize(relight(small, L_small), dark.shape, order=1),
}
print(f"mean brightness {dark.mean():.2f} -> {reference.mean():.2f}")
for name, out in results.items():
    print(f"{name:36s} PSNR vs full-resolution estimate {psnr(reference, out, data_range=1):5.2f} dB")
```

```text
mean brightness 0.16 -> 0.35
L at 1/8 size, resized up            PSNR vs full-resolution estimate 54.57 dB
relight at 1/8 size, resize output   PSNR vs full-resolution estimate 22.87 dB
```

Resizing the *map* is almost indistinguishable from the full-resolution estimate (above 54 dB on our toy), while resizing the *output* loses about 32 dB, because the image's detail is gone. It works because this light map is very smooth; one with sharp shadow boundaries suffers more, which is why some methods upsample it with an edge-aware step.

**Lightweight Retinex-style networks.** Several methods learn $L$ with a very small network:

- **LightenNet** [9] (2018), a supervised method, estimates the illumination map with four convolutional layers and divides the input by it; in the survey's comparison it has the fewest parameters and FLOPs [5].
- **DeepUPE** [10] (2019) predicts a **three-channel** illumination map (so it can also correct color) from a 256 × 256 copy of an underexposed photo, upsamples it with HDRNet's bilateral-grid module, and divides the input by it. It is trained on 3,000 underexposed photos with expert-retouched references.
- **RUAS** [11] (2021) unrolls the optimization steps of Retinex-style models for illumination estimation and noise removal into network stages, searches a compact architecture for its prior modules, and trains with reference-free losses; the survey describes the searched architectures as lightweight [5].
- **SCI** [6] (2022) learns the illumination with a cascade of weight-sharing stages, each predicting a residual update of the illumination. A self-calibration module, used only during training, makes the stages' results converge, so at test time a **single** stage is enough; the authors describe the inference network as just three $3 \times 3$ convolutions [6].

*How unrolling works and how RUAS fits the wider Retinex family are explained in [DLCV 05](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-05-low-light-enhancement/#retinex-inspired-unrolling); this chapter only adds the efficiency angle.*

**Physical-model variants.** Retinex is not the only image model used this way. CPGA-Net [12] (2025) builds its network on the atmospheric scattering model, noting that this model reduces to the Retinex model when its light term is zero. Its local branch estimates a transmission-like term and a light term with the help of dark and bright channel priors, its global branch estimates a gamma correction, and a fusion module combines the two results. It is trained on paired data and has about 25K parameters [12]. Classical image-formation knowledge replaces part of the capacity that a larger network would need.

**The lesson of illumination estimation.** A light map has a physical meaning, so priors (smoothness, a lower bound on light) can be built into it. It shares the curve's limit: dividing by a small $L$ multiplies the noise in dark regions by the same large gain.

## Lightweight end-to-end networks and Transformers

**Plain version.** Some methods keep an ordinary network that outputs the image, or the image plus a few global settings, and make that network cheap by its architecture: thin layers, cheap convolutions, few full-resolution layers, and a little attention where global context helps.

**Precise version.** Here the network computes $O = f_\theta(I)$ directly, with parameters $\theta$, possibly predicting a few parameters of a fixed pipeline along the way. Its cost is the cost of $f_\theta$ at the deployment resolution, so the design questions are architectural: which layers run at full resolution, how channels are mixed, and how global information is gathered.

**IAT.** The Illumination Adaptive Transformer (IAT) of Cui et al. [13] splits a simplified camera pipeline into local and global components. A local branch, which replaces attention with depthwise convolutions and keeps the input resolution, predicts a per-pixel multiply map and add map. A global branch uses a few attention queries (nine for a $3 \times 3$ color matrix, one for a gamma value) to predict the parameters of global operations, which are then applied to the locally adjusted image. It is trained on paired data (for low light, the LOL dataset of low/normal-light pairs [19]) and also evaluated on exposure correction and on FiveK. The authors report about 90K parameters and an average inference time of about 0.004 s per image [13]. Its global part is a parametric transform in the sense of this chapter, chosen by attention instead of by a lookup.

The building blocks below recur in such networks, including many entries of recent efficiency challenges [20]. They are design decisions made before training, not compression applied after it.

### Depthwise separable convolutions

A depthwise separable convolution replaces one $k \times k$ convolution from $C_{\text{in}}$ to $C_{\text{out}}$ channels by a $k \times k$ convolution per channel followed by a $1 \times 1$ convolution that mixes channels, which reduces the cost by the factor $1/C_{\text{out}} + 1/k^2$ (MobileNet [21]; derivation in [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-02-image-classification/#mobilenet-factorize-the-convolution) and [ML 03](/en/learn/ml/ml-03-convolutional-networks/#efficient-cnns-separate-spatial-filtering-from-channel-mixing)). Zero-DCE++ [1] and IAT's local branch [13] use it. Two cautions: depthwise layers have low arithmetic intensity (few operations per byte moved), so on some hardware they run slower than their MAC count suggests; and in a very small network the $1 \times 1$ layers dominate, so the saving is smaller than the formula implies for large $C$. MobileNetV2's inverted residual blocks [22] are a common building block in this setting.

### Structural re-parameterization

**Plain version.** Train a block with several parallel branches (which trains well), then merge the branches into one plain convolution for deployment (which runs fast). The merged network computes exactly the same function.

RepVGG [23] trains blocks with a $3 \times 3$ convolution, a $1 \times 1$ convolution and an identity branch, each followed by batch normalization, and sums them. At inference, every branch is linear, so each batch normalization can be folded into its kernel and bias, the $1 \times 1$ kernel and the identity can be written as $3 \times 3$ kernels with zeros around the center, and the three kernels can be added into one. Efficient enhancement networks, including entries in the NTIRE 2026 efficient low-light challenge [20], use this to get multi-branch training with single-branch inference cost.

*Optional: [Structural re-parameterization step by step](#structural-re-parameterization-step-by-step) (in the Supplementary notes). The folding formulas and a NumPy check that the fused convolution matches the three-branch block to about $10^{-15}$.*

### Other recurring design choices

Space-to-depth (pixel unshuffle) at the input folds each $s \times s$ block into channels so that every layer runs at $1/s^2$ of the pixels ([DLCV 04A](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04a-super-resolution/#post-upsampling-and-sub-pixel-convolution)); Exercise 1 shows that it saves only if the channel count does not grow too much. Weight sharing across iterations or stages (Zero-DCE++ reuses one set of curve maps; SCI shares weights across stages) keeps the parameter count low. And global information is gathered cheaply, by a few fully connected layers on a tiny map (HDRNet's global path, below) or a few attention queries (IAT), instead of by deep stacks of convolutions.

## Real-time retouching transforms: bilateral grids and 3D LUTs

**Plain version.** The last lineage comes from photo editing rather than from low-light research. Its goal was to reproduce a photographer's retouching, or a camera's processing pipeline, in real time on full-resolution photos. Its two tools, a grid of small color recipes and a color lookup table, turned out to be among the cheapest ways to apply any smooth color transform, and they were later used for underexposed and low-light images as well.

**Where it comes from.** The methods in this section were trained and evaluated on photo-retouching data, as their own papers state. HDRNet [4] learns from MIT-Adobe FiveK [24] (5,000 raw photos, each retouched by five experts) and from the outputs of photographic operators, among them the HDR+ burst-photography pipeline [25] and Photoshop actions. Image-adaptive 3D LUTs [14] are trained on FiveK (expert C as the target) and on the HDR+ burst-photography dataset, whose finished photos come from a camera's HDR pipeline. AdaInt [15] and SepLUT [16] use FiveK and a large portrait-retouching dataset. The Li et al. survey leaves such general photo-enhancement methods out, as not designed for diverse low-light conditions, and argues that FiveK's references do not suit low-light enhancement [5].

### Bilateral grids and guided upsampling

**Plain version.** Imagine cutting the image into large tiles, and inside each tile, also sorting the pixels into a few brightness levels. For each (tile, brightness level) box, store one small color recipe. A pixel finds its box by *where it is* and *how bright it is*, so two neighboring pixels on opposite sides of an edge (one dark, one bright) use different recipes, and the edge stays sharp even though the tiles are big.

**Precise version.** A **bilateral grid** [26] is a 3D array indexed by two coarse spatial coordinates and one coarse intensity coordinate. Chen, Paris and Durand introduced it to accelerate edge-aware filters such as the bilateral filter [27] by working in this downsampled space, building on the signal-processing view of the bilateral filter by Paris and Durand [28] that is mentioned in the [IP 03 notes](/en/learn/image-processing/dip-03-intensity-spatial-filtering/notes/#the-bilateral-filter-averaging-only-with-pixels-that-look-alike). For enhancement, each grid node stores a color transform. HDRNet [4] stores a $3 \times 4$ affine matrix per node:

$$
A[i, j, k] \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 4}, \qquad i = 0, \dots, G_y - 1,\; j = 0, \dots, G_x - 1,\; k = 0, \dots, G_z - 1,
$$

where $G_y \times G_x$ is the number of spatial nodes and $G_z$ the number of intensity nodes. To process a full-resolution pixel at row $y$ and column $x$, three steps follow.

**1. Guide.** Compute a scalar guide value $g(\mathbf{x}) \in [0, 1]$ at full resolution, for example the luma, or (as in HDRNet) a learned per-pixel function of the RGB value.

**2. Slice.** Map the pixel to continuous grid coordinates $(u_y, u_x, u_z) = \big(y\, s_y,\; x\, s_x,\; g(\mathbf{x})\,(G_z - 1)\big)$, where $s_y$ and $s_x$ convert pixel coordinates to grid coordinates, and interpolate the grid there:

$$
\bar{A}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i, j, k} \tau(u_y - i)\, \tau(u_x - j)\, \tau(u_z - k)\; A[i, j, k], \qquad \tau(t) = \max(1 - |t|, 0),
$$

where $\tau$ is the "tent" (linear interpolation) weight. Only the 8 nodes around the pixel have non-zero weight, so this is a **trilinear interpolation** in (row, column, guide). HDRNet calls this operation **slicing**; it has no learned parameters and is differentiable with respect to both the grid and the guide [4].

**3. Apply.** Multiply the pixel's color by its interpolated matrix:

$$
O(\mathbf{x}) = \bar{A}(\mathbf{x}) \begin{bmatrix} I(\mathbf{x}) \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
$$

where $I(\mathbf{x})$ is the RGB column vector and the appended 1 lets the fourth column of $\bar{A}$ act as an offset. Each output channel is thus an affine combination of the three input channels.

**Why the intensity axis matters.** If the grid had only spatial axes, neighboring pixels would always get nearly the same transform, and a strong edge inside a cell would be blurred into a halo (a bright fringe on the dark side or a dark fringe on the bright side). The intensity axis separates them: a dark pixel and a bright pixel at the same position read different nodes. This is the same "only average with pixels that look alike" idea as the bilateral filter.

**HDRNet.** Gharbi et al. [4] train a CNN on a fixed 256 × 256 copy of the image, with a local path and a global path (fully connected layers that summarize the whole image), that outputs a 16 × 16 × 8 grid with 12 coefficients per node; the guide is a learned pointwise function of the RGB value, and the loss is computed on the full-resolution output, so the grid is learned to look right *after* slicing. The authors report a real-time viewfinder at 1080p on a smartphone [4].

*Optional: [Paper guide: HDRNet](#hdrnet-deep-bilateral-learning-for-real-time-image-enhancement) (in the Paper guides). The architecture, the training data, and what to check in its tables.*

**Bilateral guided upsampling.** The same machinery can accelerate *any* slow operator, learned or not. Chen et al. [29] run the operator on a downsampled image, fit a bilateral grid of affine transforms that maps the downsampled input to the downsampled output, and slice that grid with the full-resolution input. The code below does exactly this, in a simplified form.

**The deep guided filter.** Wu et al. [30] do the same with the guided filter [31] (see the [IP 03 note](/en/learn/image-processing/dip-03-intensity-spatial-filtering/notes/#the-guided-filter-smoothing-that-follows-a-guide-image)) as a differentiable layer: it fits the filter's local linear coefficients between a low-resolution guide and a network's low-resolution output, upsamples the coefficient maps bilinearly, and applies them to the full-resolution guide. It has no intensity axis, so it relies on small windows rather than intensity bins to keep edges.

### Code: fit a grid at low resolution, slice at full resolution

The experiment: a dark coffee image (15% of the light) is the full-resolution input. A "retouching operator" brightens it locally, Retinex-style (darker surroundings get a stronger gamma lift, see [DLCV 05](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-05-low-light-enhancement/#non-deep-learning-llie-and-the-characteristics-of-low-light-images)), and boosts saturation. Pretend this operator is too slow to run at full resolution. We run it only on a 4× smaller copy, then compare three ways of producing the full-resolution result against what the operator would have produced at full resolution: (a) upsample the small output image; (b) fit one global tone curve per channel on the small pair; (c) fit a 9 × 13 × 8 bilateral grid of affine transforms on the small pair and slice it with the full-resolution input.

The fit is weighted least squares per node: each low-resolution pixel $p$ contributes to its 8 surrounding nodes with its trilinear weights $w_p$, and each node solves $\min_A \sum_p w_p \lVert A [I_p; 1] - O_p \rVert^2 + \lambda \lVert A - A_{\text{id}} \rVert^2$, where $A_{\text{id}}$ is the identity transform and the small ridge $\lambda$ keeps empty nodes at the identity.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter
from skimage import data, img_as_float
from skimage.metrics import peak_signal_noise_ratio as psnr
from skimage.transform import resize

luma = lambda x: x @ np.array([0.299, 0.587, 0.114])

def local_brighten(x):
    """The 'expensive' operator to imitate: a Retinex-style local gamma plus a saturation boost."""
    Y = luma(x)
    base = gaussian_filter(Y, x.shape[1] / 40)             # smooth illumination estimate
    Y2 = Y ** (0.35 + 0.9 * base)                           # darker surroundings -> stronger lift
    out = x * (Y2 / np.maximum(Y, 1e-3))[..., None]         # same brightness change on R, G, B
    return np.clip(Y2[..., None] + 1.3 * (out - Y2[..., None]), 0, 1)   # 30% more saturation

full = (0.15 * img_as_float(data.coffee()) ** 2.2) ** (1 / 2.2)   # 400 x 600, 15% of the light
low = resize(full, (100, 150), anti_aliasing=True)          # 4x smaller in each direction
target_low = local_brighten(low)                             # the operator runs only at low resolution
reference = local_brighten(full)                             # ground truth, for scoring only

def corners(x, shape):
    """For every pixel: the 8 surrounding grid nodes (rows, cols, luma) and their trilinear weights."""
    H, W = x.shape[:2]
    gy, gx, gz = shape
    rows, cols = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(0, gy - 1, H), np.linspace(0, gx - 1, W), indexing="ij")
    u = np.stack([rows, cols, luma(x) * (gz - 1)], -1)      # continuous grid coordinates
    i0 = np.minimum(u.astype(int), np.array(shape) - 2)
    d = u - i0
    for c in np.ndindex(2, 2, 2):
        w = np.prod(np.where(c, d, 1 - d), axis=-1)
        yield tuple((i0 + c).reshape(-1, 3).T), w.ravel()

def fit_grid(x, y, shape=(9, 13, 8), lam=1e-4):
    """One 3x4 affine color transform per node, by weighted least squares with a small ridge."""
    X = np.concatenate([x.reshape(-1, 3), np.ones((x[..., 0].size, 1))], 1)   # N x 4: (R, G, B, 1)
    Y = y.reshape(-1, 3)
    XtX, XtY = np.zeros(shape + (4, 4)), np.zeros(shape + (4, 3))
    for idx, w in corners(x, shape):
        np.add.at(XtX, idx, w[:, None, None] * X[:, :, None] * X[:, None, :])
        np.add.at(XtY, idx, w[:, None, None] * X[:, :, None] * Y[:, None, :])
    eye = np.eye(4)[:, :3]                                   # empty nodes fall back to the identity
    return np.linalg.solve(XtX + lam * np.eye(4), XtY + lam * eye)   # (gy, gx, gz, 4, 3)

def slice_apply(A, x):
    """Slice: interpolate the grid at every full-resolution pixel (guide = luma), then apply."""
    X = np.concatenate([x.reshape(-1, 3), np.ones((x[..., 0].size, 1))], 1)
    Ap = sum(w[:, None, None] * A[idx] for idx, w in corners(x, A.shape[:3]))
    return np.clip(np.einsum("nk,nkc->nc", X, Ap).reshape(x.shape), 0, 1)

def fit_curves(x, y, bins=64):
    """Baseline: one global tone curve per channel (mean output for each input level)."""
    curves = []
    for c in range(3):
        k = np.minimum((x[..., c] * bins).astype(int), bins - 1).ravel()
        n = np.bincount(k, minlength=bins)
        mean = np.bincount(k, y[..., c].ravel(), bins) / np.maximum(n, 1)
        centers = (np.arange(bins) + 0.5) / bins
        curves.append((centers[n > 0], mean[n > 0]))
    return lambda z: np.stack([np.interp(z[..., c], *curves[c]) for c in range(3)], -1)

A = fit_grid(low, target_low)
results = {
    "upsample the low-res output": resize(target_low, full.shape, order=1),
    "global tone curve per channel": fit_curves(low, target_low)(full),
    "bilateral grid 9x13x8, sliced": slice_apply(A, full),
}
print(f"grid coefficients: {A.size}   mean brightness {full.mean():.2f} -> {reference.mean():.2f}")
for name, out in results.items():
    print(f"{name:30s} PSNR vs full-resolution operator {psnr(reference, out, data_range=1):5.2f} dB")
```

```text
upsample the low-res output    PSNR vs full-resolution operator 28.48 dB
global tone curve per channel  PSNR vs full-resolution operator 23.02 dB
bilateral grid 9x13x8, sliced  PSNR vs full-resolution operator 36.01 dB
```

### Learnable 3D lookup tables

**Plain version.** A 3D lookup table (3D LUT) is a color cube: for a grid of input colors it stores the output color. Any pixel's color falls inside one small box of the cube, and its output is blended from the box's 8 corners. Fixed, hand-tuned 3D LUTs are widely used for color and tone adjustment in camera imaging pipelines and photo editing tools [14]; the learned version lets a small network choose the table for each photo.

**Precise version.** A 3D LUT with $M$ nodes per axis is an array $\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times M \times M \times 3}$; node $(i, j, k)$ holds the output color for the input color $\big(\tfrac{i}{M-1}, \tfrac{j}{M-1}, \tfrac{k}{M-1}\big)$. For an input color $(r, g, b) \in [0, 1]^3$, compute the continuous lattice coordinates and split them into an integer corner and a fractional offset:

$$
u_r = r\,(M - 1), \quad i = \min\big(\lfloor u_r \rfloor,\, M - 2\big), \quad d_r = u_r - i,
$$

and likewise $j, d_g$ for green and $k, d_b$ for blue, where $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$ rounds down, the cap $M - 2$ keeps the upper corner $i + 1$ inside the table when $r = 1$, and $d_r, d_g, d_b \in [0, 1]$ are the offsets inside the cell. **Trilinear interpolation** blends the 8 corners of the cell:

$$
\Phi(r, g, b) = \sum_{a, b', c \in \{0, 1\}} w_a(d_r)\, w_{b'}(d_g)\, w_c(d_b)\; \Phi[i + a,\, j + b',\, k + c], \qquad w_0(d) = 1 - d,\; w_1(d) = d,
$$

where $a, b', c$ select the lower (0) or upper (1) corner along each axis. The 8 weights are non-negative and sum to 1, so the output is a weighted average of the 8 stored colors. It is the same formula as slicing a bilateral grid, with the axes being (R, G, B) instead of (row, column, intensity).

Middle: the cell drawn as two square faces, one for the lower and one for the upper blue index, each with four labeled corners and the star at the same relative position, with offsets dr and db marked, and the rule that a corner's weight is the product of d or 1 minus d along each axis. Right: the curve x to the power 1 over 2.2 and its piecewise-linear approximations from 5 and 9 nodes; both are close except near zero, where the true curve is much steeper than the first linear piece." caption="Figure 4 — A 3D LUT and trilinear interpolation. (a) The lattice; an input color is located in one cell. (b) The 8 corner weights are products of the offsets. (c) Along one axis, interpolation is piecewise linear, so a curve that bends sharply between nodes (here, a gamma curve near black) is approximated poorly unless the lattice is fine there." />

**Why LUTs are fast.** Applying a LUT costs 8 table reads and about 24 multiply–adds per pixel, *regardless of how complicated the color transform is*. A transform built from dozens of color operations, or one that no formula describes, costs the same as the identity once it is baked into the table. The work is a few memory reads and multiply–adds per pixel with no data-dependent branching, which suits GPUs and fixed-function hardware well. The price is memory and smoothness: the table has $3M^3$ values, and it can only represent transforms that are close to linear inside each cell.

**Image-adaptive 3D LUTs.** Zeng et al. [14] made LUTs learnable and content-dependent. They learn $N$ basis LUTs $\Phi_1, \dots, \Phi_N$ and a small CNN that looks at a 256 × 256 downsampled copy of the image and predicts weights $w_1, \dots, w_N$. The image's own LUT is the weighted sum

$$
\Phi = \sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n\, \Phi_n,
$$

which is applied to the full-resolution image with one trilinear interpolation per pixel. Because the interpolation is linear in the table entries, fusing the tables first and interpolating once gives the same result as interpolating each table and fusing the outputs. In the paper, $M = 33$ and $N = 3$; each LUT has $3 \cdot 33^3 = 107{,}811$ values, the whole model has fewer than 600K parameters, and it processes a 4K image in under 2 ms on one Titan RTX GPU [14]. Training adds two regularizers on the tables: a smoothness term that penalizes differences between neighboring nodes, and a monotonicity term that penalizes outputs that decrease when an input channel increases, so that brightness order is preserved. The authors also state the limits clearly: one LUT per image applies the same color transform everywhere, so local adjustments are impossible, and since each color is transformed independently, noise in a night image is amplified rather than removed [14].

The snippet below implements the trilinear LUT, checks it against direct formulas, and times it against a long color pipeline.

```python
import time
import numpy as np
from skimage import data, img_as_float

img = img_as_float(data.coffee())                   # 400 x 600 x 3, RGB in [0, 1]

def build_lut(f, M=33):
    """Sample a color transform f on an M x M x M lattice: table of shape (M, M, M, 3)."""
    g = np.linspace(0, 1, M)
    r, gr, b = np.meshgrid(g, g, g, indexing="ij")
    return f(np.stack([r, gr, b], axis=-1))

def apply_lut(lut, x):
    """Trilinear interpolation of the table at every RGB value of x."""
    M = lut.shape[0]
    u = x * (M - 1)                                  # continuous lattice coordinates
    i0 = np.minimum(u.astype(int), M - 2)            # lower corner of the enclosing cell
    d = u - i0                                       # fractional offsets in [0, 1]
    out = np.zeros_like(x)
    for cr in (0, 1):
        for cg in (0, 1):
            for cb in (0, 1):
                w = ((d[..., 0] if cr else 1 - d[..., 0]) * (d[..., 1] if cg else 1 - d[..., 1])
                     * (d[..., 2] if cb else 1 - d[..., 2]))
                out += w[..., None] * lut[i0[..., 0] + cr, i0[..., 1] + cg, i0[..., 2] + cb]
    return out

identity = lambda x: x
gamma = lambda x: x ** (1 / 2.2)
print(f"identity, M=33: max error {np.abs(apply_lut(build_lut(identity), img) - img).max():.1e}")
for M in (9, 17, 33, 65):
    err = np.abs(apply_lut(build_lut(gamma, M), img) - gamma(img))
    worst = img.ravel()[err.argmax()]
    print(f"gamma 1/2.2, M={M:2d}: mean error {err.mean():.4f}  max error {err.max():.4f} (at input {worst:.3f})")

def grade(x, n_ops):
    """A long color pipeline: n_ops small rounds of channel mixing and a soft S-curve."""
    mix = np.array([[0.80, 0.15, 0.05], [0.10, 0.80, 0.10], [0.05, 0.15, 0.80]])
    step = np.eye(3) + (mix - np.eye(3)) / n_ops     # n_ops small steps, same total effect
    for _ in range(n_ops):
        x = np.clip(x @ step.T, 0, 1)
        x = x + (0.3 / n_ops) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) / (2 * np.pi)
    return x

def ms(fn, reps=5):
    fn(); t = time.perf_counter()
    for _ in range(reps): fn()
    return 1000 * (time.perf_counter() - t) / reps

for n in (1, 8, 64):
    lut = build_lut(lambda x: grade(x, n))
    err = np.abs(apply_lut(lut, img) - grade(img, n)).max()
    print(f"n_ops={n:2d}: direct {ms(lambda: grade(img, n)):6.1f} ms | apply LUT "
          f"{ms(lambda: apply_lut(lut, img)):5.1f} ms | max difference {err:.4f}")
```

```text
identity, M=33: max error 3.3e-16
gamma 1/2.2, M= 9: mean error 0.0210  max error 0.1098 (at input 0.031)
gamma 1/2.2, M=17: mean error 0.0090  max error 0.0801 (at input 0.016)
gamma 1/2.2, M=33: mean error 0.0041  max error 0.0585 (at input 0.008)
gamma 1/2.2, M=65: mean error 0.0014  max error 0.0427 (at input 0.004)
n_ops= 1: direct   16.5 ms | apply LUT  78.3 ms | max difference 0.0002
n_ops= 8: direct   84.6 ms | apply LUT  83.1 ms | max difference 0.0002
n_ops=64: direct  645.1 ms | apply LUT  87.8 ms | max difference 0.0002
```

(The timings are from one run on a 4-core CPU and will differ on yours; the errors will not.) Three observations:

- **The identity LUT is exact** up to floating-point rounding, because trilinear interpolation reproduces any function that is linear in each channel.
- **Steep curves need fine lattices where they are steep.** The gamma curve has an infinite slope at black, so the worst error is always in the darkest input bin, and doubling $M$ shrinks the *mean* error by a factor of 2 to 3 but the *worst* error much more slowly. This is exactly the problem that non-uniform lattices address (AdaInt, below), and it matters for low light, where most pixels are near black.
- **The cost is flat.** The direct pipeline's time grows with its length; the LUT's time stays about the same for 1, 8 or 64 operations, and its output matches to 0.0002. In NumPy the LUT is slower than a one-step pipeline, because eight gathers are expensive in Python; on hardware with texture interpolation the lookup is nearly free. Building the table costs $33^3 = 35{,}937$ evaluations of the pipeline, about 15% of this image's 240,000 pixels and a vanishing fraction of a 4K frame's.

**Successors.** Later work kept the "small CNN chooses a LUT" idea and attacked its limits; each is described in one line here and in more detail in the notes:

- **Spatial-aware 3D LUTs** (Wang et al. [32]) predict pixel-wise weight maps as well as global weights, so different regions blend the basis LUTs differently; the authors report about 4 ms for a 4K image on one V100 GPU.
- **AdaInt** (Yang et al. [15]) learns **non-uniform** node positions along each axis, so the lattice is dense where the transform bends and sparse where it is nearly linear.
- **SepLUT** (Yang et al. [16]) splits the transform into per-channel 1D LUTs followed by a smaller 3D LUT: the 1D curves spread the input colors over the cube so that more of the 3D LUT's cells are used.
- **4D LUT** (Liu et al. [33]) adds a fourth input, a learned **context** value per pixel, so two pixels with the same RGB but different content (sky versus sea) can be mapped differently; interpolation becomes quadrilinear.
- **LUTs with bilateral grids** (Kim and Cho [34]) bring spatial information into the LUT model through bilateral grids and replace a long decoder with slicing operations.

### From retouching to low light

The retouching tools carry over to underexposed and low-light images. DeepUPE [10] (the Retinex section) upsamples its illumination map with HDRNet's bilateral-grid module and trains on its own underexposed photos rather than on FiveK, which, its authors note, was built for general photo enhancement and contains only a small portion of underexposed photos. DPLUT [17] (the curve section) brings learned tables to low light. And the HDR+ burst dataset used to train image-adaptive 3D LUTs was collected for research on high-dynamic-range and low-light imaging on phones; on its night images, the authors observe that their model brightens the dark areas and amplifies the noise with them [14]. A retouching-style transform is therefore a strong choice for the *tone and color* part of low-light enhancement at high resolution, and a poor choice on its own for dark, noisy captures.

## Measuring efficiency honestly

**Plain version.** Efficiency claims are easy to make and hard to compare. A fair comparison of lightweight designs says exactly what was counted, at which resolution, on which hardware, and includes every step from input frame to output frame.

Dehghani et al. show that parameters, FLOPs, throughput and latency can disagree, and that reporting only one of them gives a misleading picture [35]. For enhancement:

- **Count at the deployment resolution**, and also report MACs per megapixel so that readers can rescale. A FLOP count at 256 × 256 says nothing about 4K for a full-resolution network, and the opposite is true for thumbnail-based designs.
- **Include the full-resolution step.** Slicing, LUT interpolation, curve iteration, guided-filter upsampling and resizing are often custom operators that automatic FLOP counters skip. They are the part of a predict-then-apply design that grows with resolution.
- **Say what a "parameter" includes.** A 33-node 3D LUT is about 108K values, more than the whole of Zero-DCE++ at about 10K parameters [1] or IAT at about 90K [13].
- **Time the whole path on the target device**: fixed device, runtime, numeric precision, batch size 1, resolution and thread count, warm-up, percentiles, GPU synchronization, and color conversion and memory transfers included (the protocol of [ML 10](/en/learn/ml/ml-10-evaluation-deployment/#a-latency-protocol)). Challenges that time on real phones make this concrete: the Mobile AI 2022 learned-ISP challenge, for example, measured runtime on a Snapdragon 8 Gen 1 GPU with TensorFlow Lite [36]. A GPU number from a paper (such as the Titan RTX or V100 timings above) tells you about relative speed on that GPU, not about your phone.
- **Judge quality at full resolution**, with the metrics of [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04-image-restoration/#evaluation-metrics) and the low-light specifics of [DLCV 05](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-05-low-light-enhancement/#evaluation), because halos and banding from coarse grids or tables may vanish when the output is downsampled for scoring.

## Failure modes

**Plain version.** Fast enhancers fail in characteristic ways that follow from their shortcuts: tables are coarse, grids are coarse, transforms ignore noise, and each frame is processed alone.

- **Banding from limited bit depth and coarse tables.** Strong brightening stretches the few gray levels of a dark 8-bit image apart, so a smooth gradient becomes visible steps. A coarse or 8-bit-stored LUT adds its own steps and piecewise-linear kinks. The snippet below brightens a smooth dark ramp after storing it at different bit depths.

```python
import numpy as np

ramp = np.linspace(0, 0.1, 4000)                 # a smooth dark gradient (e.g. a night sky)
curve = lambda x: x ** (1 / 2.2)                 # a strong brightening curve
for bits in (8, 10, 12):
    q = np.round(ramp * (2**bits - 1)) / (2**bits - 1)          # sensor / file bit depth
    out = np.round(curve(q) * 255)                              # 8-bit display output
    levels = np.unique(out)
    print(f"{bits:2d}-bit input: {len(np.unique(q)):4d} input levels -> {len(levels):3d} output levels,"
          f" largest jump {np.diff(levels).max():.0f} display levels")
```

```text
 8-bit input:   27 input levels ->  27 output levels, largest jump 21 display levels
10-bit input:  103 input levels ->  67 output levels, largest jump 11 display levels
12-bit input:  411 input levels ->  84 output levels, largest jump 6 display levels
```

The brightened ramp spans about 90 display levels (from 0 to $0.1^{1/2.2} \approx 0.35$ of 255), but an 8-bit input provides only 27 of them, so the gradient is shown as 27 bands; the largest jump is the first step above black, where the curve is steepest. No enhancer can invent the missing levels without adding noise or dithering, which is one reason raw or higher-bit-depth inputs matter for low light ([DLCV 05](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-05-low-light-enhancement/#deep-learning-llie)).

The top one, from an 8-bit input, shows 27 clearly visible bands; the middle one, from a 10-bit input, shows 67 finer bands; the bottom one, from a 12-bit input, shows 84 bands and looks almost smooth." caption="Figure 5 — Banding after strong brightening (our synthetic ramp). The same curve applied to an 8-, 10- and 12-bit version of one dark gradient; the number of distinct output levels limits how smooth the result can look." />

- **Halos from coarse grids and light maps.** A grid cell spans many pixels; where an edge separates two regions that need different transforms but fall into the same intensity bin, both sides receive a blend, and a fringe appears along the edge. Figure 3's residual errors along the cup rim are this effect in miniature, and a coarsely estimated illumination map does the same at sharp shadow boundaries. Finer intensity bins, a better (learned) guide, or a finer grid reduce it at a cost. HDRNet's authors also note slightly softer outputs from the heavy downsampling [4].
- **Color shifts.** Per-channel curves change hue whenever they treat R, G and B differently; a LUT trained on one dataset may map colors it never saw (saturated neon, skin under colored light) unpredictably, because nodes far from the training colors are constrained only by the smoothness regularizer. Gray-world-style losses push colorful scenes toward gray (see the toy in DLCV 05).
- **Amplified noise.** A transform that brightens shadows multiplies their noise by the local slope of the curve, or by the gain $L^{\gamma - 1}$ of a Retinex division, and pointwise or local-affine transforms cannot average noise away. The 3D LUT authors report exactly this on night images [14]. A fast enhancer for real low light needs a denoiser somewhere (RUAS and DPLUT include one), or a gain that is limited where the signal-to-noise ratio is low.
- **Temporal flicker on video.** When each frame is processed independently, the predicted LUT weights, curve parameters, light maps or grid coefficients jump a little from frame to frame, and the whole image pulses in brightness or color. Smoothing the predicted parameters over time (for example, an exponential moving average of the LUT weights) is cheap because the parameters are few. Lai et al. [37] proposed a general post-processing network that makes per-frame processed video temporally consistent without knowing which operator was applied.

## Modern view

**Surveys and reports.** No survey is devoted only to lightweight enhancement, so three documents together give the overview:

- **Li et al., TPAMI 2022 [5]**, the deep low-light survey already used in DLCV 05, supplies this chapter's learning-strategy taxonomy and a runtime, parameter and FLOP comparison: curve estimation (Zero-DCE) was fastest because a small network estimates only a few curve parameters, the four-layer LightenNet was the smallest, and methods that optimize on each test image were slow. Its discussion adds that supervised methods perform better in most cases but need paired data and more computation, while zero-shot learning is more appealing in practice.
- **Dehghani et al., ICLR 2022 [35]** is about efficiency reporting in general: cost indicators contradict each other, and incomplete reporting leads to wrong conclusions about which model is more efficient.
- **The NTIRE 2026 efficient low-light challenge report [20]** is the most recent snapshot of what works under a hard size limit (models below 1 MB, phone photographs at 3024 × 4032). It groups the submissions into lightweight U-Net or encoder–decoder designs with depthwise separable convolutions, Retinex-style decompositions, small attention modules, color-space decoupling, classical priors such as histogram equalization, and structural re-parameterization or partial convolutions.

*Optional: [Paper guide: the Li et al. low-light survey](#the-li-et-al-low-light-survey-tpami-2022) and [Paper guide: NTIRE 2026 efficient low-light challenge](#ntire-2026-efficient-low-light-image-enhancement-challenge-report) (in the Paper guides). How the taxonomy and the efficiency table were built, how the challenge ranks entries, and what each can and cannot tell you.*

**State of the art (2023–2026).** Retinex-style designs remain central in efficient low-light work: small illumination networks and physical-model variants such as CPGA-Net [12] keep appearing, and Retinex-style decompositions are one of the recurring patterns of the NTIRE 2026 submissions, next to small encoder–decoders with depthwise convolutions, a little attention and re-parameterization [20]. Parametric pipeline-style models such as IAT [13] predict a few interpretable global parameters plus a light local correction. Lookup tables gain locality and context (4D LUTs [33], LUTs with bilateral grids [34]) and reach low light (DPLUT [17]), while curve estimation remains the reference point for speed.

**Open problems.**

- **Local, noise-aware and fast at once.** Transform-based models are fast because they do not touch fine structure, which is also why they cannot denoise. Combining them with a cheap denoiser without losing the speed advantage is unsolved in general, and the survey lists removing unknown real noise as an open issue for LLIE as a whole [5].
- **Generalization of small models.** Small models have little capacity to cover unusual lighting, cameras and scenes; the survey notes that a model trained on FiveK does not enhance LOL images well [5]. Image-adaptive tables and grids help, but behavior far from the training data is poorly understood.
- **Video.** Temporal consistency at a few milliseconds per 4K frame, without optical flow, remains hard; per-frame models flicker and video models are heavier.
- **Fair, comparable efficiency numbers.** Papers time on different GPUs, at different resolutions, with or without the full-resolution step; on-device measurements are rare outside challenges.
- **Which learning strategy for which lineage.** Zero-shot curve and illumination methods avoid paired data, supervised retouching transforms learn a reference style, and the two are rarely compared under the same compute budget and test data.

## Key takeaways

- "Lightweight" has several meanings: parameters, MACs per megapixel, memory traffic and measured latency. For full-resolution networks, arithmetic and memory grow with the pixel count, so a 4K frame costs four times a 1080p frame. Lightweight models are *designed* small; model compression is a separate topic ([DLCV 01C](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-01c-model-compression/)).
- Tone and color enhancement is mostly a **smooth remapping** of the input's values, so a model can **predict a transform at low resolution and apply it at full resolution**. Upsample the transform, never the image.
- The field divides methods by **what the network predicts** (curve, illumination, image, retouching transform) and by **learning strategy** (SL, RL, UL, ZSL, SSL in the Li et al. survey); the two axes are independent.
- **Curve estimation** (Zero-DCE++) and **Retinex illumination estimation** (LightenNet, DeepUPE, RUAS, SCI, and physical-model variants such as CPGA-Net) give some of the smallest low-light models, because a curve or a light map is smooth and cheap to apply.
- **Lightweight end-to-end networks** (IAT and compact encoder–decoders) get their efficiency from architecture: depthwise separable convolutions, structural re-parameterization, few full-resolution layers and cheap global context.
- **Bilateral grids** (HDRNet, bilateral guided upsampling) and **learnable 3D LUTs** (image-adaptive LUTs and successors) come from real-time photo retouching; trilinear interpolation applies them for a fixed, tiny cost per pixel, and they carry over to underexposed images for the tone part.
- Report parameters (including tables), MACs at the deployment resolution including the per-pixel step, peak memory, on-device latency and full-resolution quality; watch for banding, halos, color shifts, amplified noise and flicker.

### Fitting and slicing a bilateral grid in detail

This note writes out the least-squares fit used in the bilateral-grid snippet, the choices it makes, and how they relate to bilateral guided upsampling [29].

**The model.** Each node $n = (i, j, k)$ of a $G_y \times G_x \times G_z$ grid holds a matrix $A_n \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 3}$ (the transpose of the $3 \times 4$ form in the main text, which is convenient for least squares). A pixel $p$ with input color $I_p \in \mathbb{R}^3$ has the augmented vector $\mathbf{x}_p = [I_p^\top, 1]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^4$, a target color $O_p \in \mathbb{R}^3$, and trilinear weights $w_{pn} \ge 0$ for its 8 surrounding nodes ($w_{pn} = 0$ for all other nodes, and $\sum_n w_{pn} = 1$).

**The local fit.** The snippet minimizes, separately for each node,

$$
E_n(A_n) = \sum_{p} w_{pn}\, \big\lVert A_n^\top \mathbf{x}_p - O_p \big\rVert^2 + \lambda\, \lVert A_n - A_{\text{id}} \rVert_F^2,
$$

where $\lVert \cdot \rVert_F$ is the Frobenius norm, $A_{\text{id}} = [\,I_3;\ \mathbf{0}^\top]$ is the identity transform (no change, zero offset) and $\lambda > 0$ is a small ridge weight. Setting the gradient to zero gives the normal equations

$$
\Big(\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_p w_{pn}\, \mathbf{x}_p \mathbf{x}_p^\top}_{S_n \,\in\, \mathbb{R}^{4\times4}} + \lambda I_4\Big) A_n = \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_p w_{pn}\, \mathbf{x}_p O_p^\top}_{T_n \,\in\, \mathbb{R}^{4\times3}} + \lambda A_{\text{id}},
$$

which is exactly what `XtX`, `XtY` and `np.linalg.solve` compute. Accumulating $S_n$ and $T_n$ needs one pass over the low-resolution pixels; solving needs one $4 \times 4$ system per node.

**Why the ridge is needed.** $S_n$ is a weighted scatter matrix of the colors near node $n$. If a node sees no pixels (an intensity bin that does not occur in that region), $S_n = 0$ and the ridge alone gives $A_n = A_{\text{id}}$. If it sees pixels of almost one color, $S_n$ is close to rank 1: the fit can match the target at that color, but the slopes in other color directions are arbitrary. The ridge pulls them toward the identity. Without it, a full-resolution pixel whose color is slightly different from what the node saw can receive an extreme slope, which shows up as speckles or color blotches. Too large a $\lambda$ makes every node close to the identity and the edit too weak.

**Soft versus hard assignment.** The snippet uses the same trilinear weights for fitting and slicing ("soft" binning), so each node's matrix is fitted to the pixels that will later read it. Assigning each pixel only to its nearest node ("hard" binning) is simpler but fits each node to a slightly different set of pixels than slicing uses, which adds error at cell boundaries.

**What bilateral guided upsampling adds.** Chen et al. [29] add a smoothness term on the affine matrices to the data term, which, in their words, prevents false edges and amplified noise. The resulting energy couples neighboring nodes, so empty or poorly constrained nodes are filled from their neighbors instead of falling back to the identity. They either solve it as one global optimization (a large sparse linear system instead of many $4 \times 4$ ones) or approximate it by fitting the matrices locally and then blurring them in grid space; depending on the parameters, that fast approximation reduces to known methods such as the guided filter [31]. They report that fitting takes about 10 ms on mobile devices and that applying the curves at full resolution can be done by a simple GPU shader.

**Grid size.** More spatial cells follow spatial changes of the edit more closely; more intensity bins separate the two sides of an edge better. Both reduce halos, but each node then sees fewer pixels, so the fit becomes noisier and the ridge or smoothness term must do more work. Exercise 4 measures this trade-off on the toy.

**Pitfalls.** The guide must be computed identically at fitting and slicing time (the snippet uses luma for both); a guide in a different color space or gamma encoding shifts every pixel to the wrong intensity bin. The grid's spatial coordinates must map the image corners to the grid corners at both resolutions (the snippet uses `linspace(0, G - 1, H)`), or the edit shifts by a fraction of a cell.

### Learnable LUT variants in detail

This note describes, at the level of their key mechanism, the LUT variants listed in the main text.

**Spatial-aware 3D LUTs** [32]. A two-head weight predictor outputs a weight vector for image-level adaptation and a weight *map* for pixel-wise fusion of several spatial-aware LUTs. Two pixels with the same color can therefore be mapped differently if they lie in different regions, at the cost of fusing tables (or outputs) per pixel instead of once per image.

**AdaInt** [15]. A uniform lattice places nodes at $0, \tfrac{1}{M-1}, \dots, 1$ on every axis. AdaInt predicts, per image and per axis, $M - 1$ positive **intervals** $\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_{M-1}$ that sum to 1 (for example with a softmax), and places node $m$ at the cumulative sum $q_m = \sum_{l \le m} \Delta_l$ with $q_0 = 0$. To look up a value $r$ on that axis, find the interval with $q_i \le r \lt q_{i+1}$ (a search over the sorted node positions) and use the offset

$$
d_r = \frac{r - q_i}{q_{i+1} - q_i},
$$

where $q_i$ and $q_{i+1}$ are the positions of the two nodes around $r$. The 8 corner weights are then formed exactly as in the uniform case. The paper's operator for this lookup, AiLUT-Transform, also passes gradients to the interval sizes, so the lattice positions are learned end to end. The effect is the one our gamma experiment asked for: dense nodes where the transform bends (for example near black for a brightening curve) and sparse nodes where it is nearly linear.

**SepLUT** [16]. The transform is split into a per-channel 1D LUT stage followed by a 3D LUT. The 1D curves are cheap and can be fine, so they handle each channel's tone curve; they also redistribute the input colors so that they spread over more of the 3D LUT's cells. The authors argue that this better **cell utilization** lets the 3D stage be much smaller. Memory goes from $3M^3$ to $3M^3_{\text{small}} + 3K$, where $K$ is the number of entries per 1D LUT.

**4D LUT** [33]. A context encoder predicts a per-pixel context value $c(\mathbf{x})$, and the table is indexed by $(r, g, b, c)$. Interpolation becomes **quadrilinear**: 16 corners, with weights that are products of four offsets. With $M_c$ nodes on the context axis the table has $3M^3 M_c$ values, which is why such tables use few context nodes.

**LUTs with bilateral grids** [34]. Kim and Cho transform bilateral grids into a spatial feature domain to bring spatial information into a 3D LUT model, and use slicing operations in place of the long decoding path of earlier spatially aware designs, which reduces inference time and parameters.

**A common pitfall.** All these methods are trained on photo-retouching data (most often FiveK [24]), whose inputs cover the RGB cube unevenly. Nodes in rarely visited corners of the cube are set only by the smoothness and monotonicity regularizers, so they behave sensibly but are not learned from data. Check outputs on saturated and very dark colors, which are exactly those corners.

### Structural re-parameterization step by step

This note shows why a RepVGG-style block [23] with three branches can be replaced at inference by a single $3 \times 3$ convolution, and checks the claim numerically.

**Batch normalization at inference is affine.** With learned scale $\gamma_o$ and shift $\beta_o$ and stored running mean $\mu_o$ and variance $\sigma_o^2$ for output channel $o$,

$$
\mathrm{BN}(y)_o = \gamma_o \frac{y_o - \mu_o}{\sqrt{\sigma_o^2 + \epsilon}} + \beta_o = s_o\, y_o + (\beta_o - s_o \mu_o), \qquad s_o = \frac{\gamma_o}{\sqrt{\sigma_o^2 + \epsilon}},
$$

where $y_o$ is the convolution output in channel $o$ and $\epsilon$ a small constant. So BN after a convolution with kernel $W$ (no bias) equals a convolution with kernel $s_o W_o$ and bias $\beta_o - s_o \mu_o$.

**Every branch is a $3 \times 3$ convolution.** A $1 \times 1$ kernel is a $3 \times 3$ kernel that is zero except at the center. The identity map is a $3 \times 3$ kernel with a 1 at the center of the $(o, o)$ slice and zeros elsewhere. Convolution is linear in the kernel, so the sum of three convolutions of the same input is one convolution with the summed kernel and the summed bias:

$$
W_{\text{fused}} = \sum_{\text{branch } m} s^{(m)} \odot W^{(m)}, \qquad b_{\text{fused}} = \sum_{m} \big(\beta^{(m)} - s^{(m)} \mu^{(m)}\big),
$$

where $W^{(m)}$ is branch $m$'s kernel written in $3 \times 3$ form, $s^{(m)}, \beta^{(m)}, \mu^{(m)}$ are its BN quantities, and $\odot$ scales each output channel's kernel by its $s_o$. The ReLU comes *after* the sum, so it is not affected.

```python
import numpy as np
from numpy.lib.stride_tricks import sliding_window_view

def conv3x3(x, w, b):
    """x: (C, H, W), w: (Cout, C, 3, 3), b: (Cout,). Zero padding keeps the size."""
    patches = sliding_window_view(np.pad(x, ((0, 0), (1, 1), (1, 1))), (3, 3), axis=(1, 2))
    return np.einsum("chwij,ocij->ohw", patches, w) + b[:, None, None]

def bn(y, gamma, beta, mean, var, eps=1e-5):
    """Batch normalization at inference: a fixed per-channel scale and shift."""
    s = gamma / np.sqrt(var + eps)
    return s[:, None, None] * (y - mean[:, None, None]) + beta[:, None, None]

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
C, H, W = 8, 32, 32
x = rng.standard_normal((C, H, W))
w3, w1 = rng.standard_normal((C, C, 3, 3)) * 0.1, rng.standard_normal((C, C, 1, 1)) * 0.1
bns = [(rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, C), rng.normal(0, 0.1, C), rng.normal(0, 0.1, C), rng.uniform(0.5, 2, C))
       for _ in range(3)]                                   # (gamma, beta, mean, var) per branch
zero_b = np.zeros(C)

# Training-time block: 3x3 conv + BN, 1x1 conv + BN, identity + BN, summed
w1_as_3 = np.pad(w1, ((0, 0), (0, 0), (1, 1), (1, 1)))     # a 1x1 kernel is a 3x3 kernel with zeros
w_id = np.zeros((C, C, 3, 3)); w_id[np.arange(C), np.arange(C), 1, 1] = 1   # identity as a 3x3 kernel
branches = [w3, w1_as_3, w_id]
y_train = sum(bn(conv3x3(x, w, zero_b), *p) for w, p in zip(branches, bns))

# Inference-time block: fold each BN into its kernel, then add the three kernels
w_fused, b_fused = np.zeros_like(w3), np.zeros(C)
for w, (g, beta, mean, var) in zip(branches, bns):
    s = g / np.sqrt(var + 1e-5)
    w_fused += s[:, None, None, None] * w
    b_fused += beta - s * mean
y_infer = conv3x3(x, w_fused, b_fused)
print(f"max difference: {np.abs(y_train - y_infer).max():.1e}")
print("weights: training", w3.size + w1.size, "+ 3 BNs -> inference", w_fused.size, "+ bias")
```

```text
max difference: 2.7e-15
weights: training 640 + 3 BNs -> inference 576 + bias
```

**Edge cases.** The identity branch exists only when the input and output have the same number of channels and the same size (stride 1). Padding must match: the $3 \times 3$ branch uses one pixel of zero padding, and the $1 \times 1$ branch must be padded so that its output aligns, which writing it as a centered $3 \times 3$ kernel does automatically. Folding is exact only with BN in inference mode (fixed running statistics). And the saving is in latency and memory traffic, not in the arithmetic of the $3 \times 3$ branch itself: the inference block does one convolution instead of three plus an addition.

## Paper guides

These guides are optional: each walks through one paper the chapter builds on — the problem it poses, the idea, what it showed and what to check when you read it.

### The Li et al. low-light survey (TPAMI 2022)

**Problem.** By 2021 there were more than a hundred deep low-light enhancement papers, trained on different data, tested on different sets with different metrics, and with no common view of how they relate [5].

**Idea.** Organize the methods by learning strategy (SL, RL, UL, ZSL, SSL), summarize each method's network structure, losses, training and test data, metrics and whether it is Retinex-based in one table, and benchmark a selection of them under one protocol. The survey also contributes a test dataset of low-light images and videos taken with 18 different phone cameras (LLIV-Phone, 120 videos with 45,148 images) and an online platform that runs many published methods on a user's own images.

**What it showed.** No method wins everywhere: rankings change with the test set and the metric. Supervised methods did better on the paired LOL test set, while all methods struggled with the noise of real phone images. In the efficiency comparison (32 images of 1200 × 900, one NVIDIA 1080Ti GPU), Zero-DCE had the shortest runtime, LightenNet the fewest parameters and FLOPs, and per-image optimization methods were slow. The survey also argues that MIT-Adobe FiveK is not appropriate for low-light enhancement because its references do not match the task.

**What to check when you read it.** The learning-strategy labels are the survey's own and depend on what the original papers report; later papers sometimes use other words for the same thing (SCI calls Zero-DCE and RUAS "unsupervised"). The runtime table mixes frameworks (Theano, TensorFlow, PyTorch, MATLAB), so it compares implementations as much as designs, and it was measured on a desktop GPU, not a phone. The survey covers methods up to 2021, so it does not include SCI, IAT or anything later.

**Why it matters here.** It supplies the learning-strategy axis of this chapter's map and the clearest published evidence that curve estimation and tiny illumination networks are among the cheapest low-light designs.

### HDRNet: deep bilateral learning for real-time image enhancement

**Problem.** Phone cameras need complex, content-dependent photographic edits (tone mapping, a retoucher's style) at viewfinder rates on full-resolution frames, which a full-resolution CNN cannot deliver on a phone [4].

**Idea.** Let a CNN see only a 256 × 256 copy of the frame and predict a $16 \times 16 \times 8$ bilateral grid of $3 \times 4$ affine color transforms, combining a local path and a global path (fully connected layers) so that decisions can depend on the whole image. A learned pointwise guide and a parameter-free, differentiable **slicing** layer bring the transforms back to full resolution, where they are applied pixel by pixel. The loss is computed on the full-resolution output.

**What it showed.** The same architecture can imitate many operators, including hand-engineered pipelines, Photoshop actions and expert retouching from FiveK [24], and the authors report a real-time 1080p viewfinder on a phone. Its training is offline from input/output pairs, so it can learn edits for which no algorithm exists.

**What to check when you read it.** Separate the timing of the network (on the thumbnail) from that of slicing and applying (at full resolution), and note on which processor each runs. Read the limitations section: outputs are slightly softer than the reference, and operators that create edges or need non-local reasoning (sharpening, dehazing, matting) fit the local affine model poorly. Note that the global path's fully connected layers fix the input size of the thumbnail.

**Why it matters here.** It is the clearest statement of this chapter's principle, "predict the transform at low resolution, apply it at full resolution", and its bilateral-grid upsampling reappears in later illumination, LUT and grid methods [10], [34].

### Image-adaptive 3D LUTs (Zeng et al., TPAMI 2022)

**Problem.** Learned photo enhancers either gave unsatisfying results or needed too much computation and memory for high-resolution photos of 12 megapixels and more [14].

**Idea.** Learn a few basis 3D LUTs and a small CNN on a 256 × 256 thumbnail that predicts content-dependent weights to fuse them into one LUT per image, trained end to end with pairwise or unpaired learning, with smoothness and monotonicity regularizers on the tables.

**What it showed.** A model with fewer than 600K parameters that processes a 4K image in under 2 ms on one Titan RTX GPU, while scoring well on FiveK and HDR+ benchmarks by fidelity and color-difference metrics [14]. It started a line of work on learnable LUTs (spatial-aware LUTs, AdaInt, SepLUT, 4D LUTs).

**What to check when you read it.** The timing tables are on a desktop GPU with a custom interpolation kernel; check whether a comparison includes data transfer and the thumbnail resize. Check which expert's retouching is the target on FiveK and which split is used, because published numbers differ with both. Read the limitations: one LUT per image cannot make local adjustments, and noise is amplified on night images.

**Why it matters here.** It shows how far a purely global, per-image transform can go, and its stated limits are exactly what the successors in this chapter address.

### NTIRE 2026 efficient low-light image enhancement challenge report

**Problem.** Efficient low-light enhancement had no common benchmark with a hard resource limit on realistic, high-resolution phone photographs [20].

**Idea.** A challenge with phone images at 3024 × 4032 covering dim, extremely dark, backlit, unevenly lit and night scenes, a model-size limit of 1 MB, and a ranking that aggregates each team's ranks over several full-reference and no-reference quality metrics, with fewer parameters breaking ties.

**What it showed.** 207 registered participants, 27 teams with valid entries and 17 with valid fact sheets, and a set of recurring design patterns: lightweight encoder–decoders with depthwise separable convolutions, Retinex-style decomposition, small attention modules, color-space decoupling, classical priors and re-parameterization [20].

**What to check when you read it.** The efficiency constraint is model size, not runtime or FLOPs on a fixed device, so the tables tell you which designs fit in 1 MB, not which run fastest on a phone. Rank aggregation over metrics means a team can win without being best on any single metric; look at the individual metric columns. Training hardware listed by teams is not an efficiency measurement.

**Why it matters here.** It is the most recent independent snapshot of efficient low-light design, and it shows how strongly the measurement protocol shapes what "efficient" means.
