## What you'll learn

- What a pose is: a skeleton graph of named keypoints with coordinates and visibility flags, and how pose estimation differs from detection, segmentation and the generic keypoints of classical feature extraction.
- The two ways a network outputs a keypoint, direct regression and Gaussian heatmaps, and why decoding a heatmap needs sub-pixel refinement, with a tested experiment on quantization error versus heatmap stride.
- How multi-person pose is solved: top-down pipelines (a person detector, then SimpleBaseline, HRNet or ViTPose on each crop), bottom-up pipelines (all keypoints first, then grouping with part affinity fields or associative embedding) and one-stage or set-prediction models.
- How pose is measured: object keypoint similarity (OKS) and OKS-based AP with per-keypoint constants, PCK and PCKh, and MPJPE for 3D, with code.
- How 3D pose is obtained by lifting 2D keypoints, by triangulating them from several cameras or by fitting a parametric body model such as SMPL, and which datasets and failure modes (occlusion, crowds, left–right flips) shape the field.

## The big picture

A detector puts a box around each person ([DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/)). A box says *where* a person is, but not *what they are doing*: standing, waving, falling or kicking a ball all give similar boxes. **Pose estimation** goes one step further. It finds a small set of meaningful points on the body, the **keypoints** (nose, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles and so on), and connects them into a stick figure.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Remember connect-the-dots pictures? Pose estimation is the opposite game. The computer looks at a photo of a person and has to *put the dots back*: one dot on each shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee and ankle, each with the right name. Then it draws lines between the dots and you get a stick figure that moves the way the person moves. The hard parts are the ones you would also find hard: a hand hidden behind someone's back, two friends hugging so you cannot tell whose arm is whose, and remembering which side is *their* left.
</Callout>

**Why it matters.** A stick figure is a compact summary of what a body is doing. Sports analysis, physical therapy, fall detection and fitness apps measure joint angles from it. Animation, games and augmented reality drive avatars with it. Robots and cars use it to anticipate where people will move. Neuroscientists track the joints of mice and flies without attaching markers [1]. And the same ideas, heatmaps, keypoint grouping and keypoint metrics, are reused for faces, hands, animals and object parts.

This chapter builds on [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/) for person detection, non-maximum suppression (NMS) and the AP machinery, and on [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-segmentation/) for dense prediction and upsampling; we link to them instead of repeating them. Tracking poses over time uses the association methods of [DLCV 07](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-tracking/), and 3D triangulation uses the camera geometry of [DLCV 11](/en/learn/deep-learning/3d-reconstruction/). Two surveys frame the field: Zheng et al. [2] cover 2D and 3D pose with deep learning, and Chen et al. [3] organize monocular methods by task. We return to both in the Modern view.

## What a pose is

**Plain version.** A pose is a list of named points on the body, each with a position and a flag that says whether it was visible, plus a fixed list of which points are joined by "bones".

**Precise version.** A **skeleton** is a graph $G = (V, E)$. The vertices $V = \{1, \dots, K\}$ are the $K$ keypoint *types* (for example "left wrist"), and the edges $E \subset V \times V$ are the **limbs** that connect them. A 2D pose of one person is

$$
P = \{(\mathbf{x}_k, v_k)\}_{k=1}^{K}, \qquad \mathbf{x}_k = (x_k, y_k) \in \mathbb{R}^2,
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_k$ is the image position of keypoint $k$ in pixels and $v_k$ is its **visibility flag**. The COCO keypoint format uses $v_k = 0$ for "not labeled", $v_k = 1$ for "labeled but not visible" (occluded, position guessed by the annotator) and $v_k = 2$ for "labeled and visible" [4]. A 3D pose replaces $\mathbf{x}_k$ by a point $\mathbf{X}_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$, usually in millimetres in a camera or world frame.

The COCO person skeleton has $K = 17$ keypoints: the nose, both eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees and ankles [4]. Figure 1 shows them on a synthetic stick figure. Two conventions trip up every beginner:

- **Left and right are the person's, not the viewer's.** A person facing the camera has their left shoulder on the *right* side of the image. Flipping an image horizontally therefore turns every left keypoint into a right one, and data augmentation must swap the labels as well as mirror the coordinates.
- **Not visible is not the same as not labeled.** An occluded wrist ($v = 1$) still has a position and is still evaluated; an unlabeled keypoint ($v = 0$) is simply ignored.

### Pose estimation versus its neighbours

Four related tasks are easy to confuse:

| Task | Output | Where in this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generic keypoint detection | Corners and blobs that are easy to find again in another image, with descriptors; no semantic name | [IP 12](/en/learn/image-processing/feature-extraction/#keypoint-detection-with-difference-of-gaussians) |
| Object detection | One box, class and score per object | [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/) |
| Instance segmentation | One pixel mask per object | [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-segmentation/) |
| Pose estimation | One set of *named* keypoints per object, connected by a fixed skeleton | this chapter |

The keypoints of [IP 12](/en/learn/image-processing/feature-extraction/) are chosen *by the image*: wherever a corner or blob happens to be. The keypoints here are chosen *by the task*: the left elbow must be found even when it lies on a smooth sleeve with no corner at all. That is why semantic keypoints are learned from labeled examples rather than detected by a formula.

The task comes in several flavors. **Single-person 2D** estimation assumes one person per input crop. **Multi-person 2D** estimation must also decide how many people there are and which keypoint belongs to whom. **3D** estimation recovers depth. **Whole-body** estimation adds feet, hands and face landmarks, and **mesh recovery** outputs a full body surface instead of a sparse skeleton [5].

## Two ways to output a keypoint

**Plain version.** A network can either *say the numbers* ("the wrist is at $x = 140$, $y = 128$"), which is regression, or *paint a picture* that glows where the wrist is, which is a heatmap. For most of the deep-learning era, painting won, because convolutional networks are better at painting than at saying numbers.

### Direct regression

DeepPose [6] was an early deep pose estimator built on regression. A convolutional network maps the image to a vector of $2K$ numbers, the keypoint coordinates normalized by the person's box, and is trained with a squared error,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \lVert \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k - \mathbf{x}_k \rVert_2^2,
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k$ is the predicted and $\mathbf{x}_k$ the ground-truth position of keypoint $k$ (in normalized coordinates). DeepPose refined its first guess with a cascade of further regressors applied to higher-resolution crops around each joint.

Regression is simple, fast and has no resolution limit, but the fully connected output throws away the spatial layout that convolutions are good at, and for years regression trailed heatmaps on accuracy.

### Gaussian heatmaps

Tompson et al. [7] instead asked the network to output one image per keypoint type, a **heatmap** that is high near the keypoint and low elsewhere. The target for keypoint $k$ is a Gaussian blob centred on the true position:

$$
H_k^{*}(\mathbf{p}) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{x}_k / s \rVert_2^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
$$

where $\mathbf{p}$ is a heatmap cell (integer coordinates), $s$ is the **output stride** (how many input pixels one heatmap cell covers, typically 4), $\mathbf{x}_k / s$ is the true keypoint position in heatmap units and $\sigma$ is the blob's width in cells. The network is trained with a pixel-wise mean squared error between the predicted heatmaps $\hat{H}_k$ and the targets,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{hm}} = \frac{1}{K\,|\Omega|} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{\mathbf{p} \in \Omega} w_k \big(\hat{H}_k(\mathbf{p}) - H_k^{*}(\mathbf{p})\big)^2,
$$

where $\Omega$ is the set of heatmap cells and $w_k$ is 0 for unlabeled keypoints and 1 otherwise. The stacked hourglass network uses this supervision, following Tompson et al., with $\sigma = 1$ pixel [8].

Why does this work so much better than regression? A heatmap **matches what convolutions do**: it is dense and translation-equivariant (if the person moves, the blob moves), so every output cell answers a local question, "is there a wrist *here*?". It **can express doubt**: two plausible wrist positions become two weaker blobs, where a single regressed coordinate would land in between. And the peak height is a **free confidence score** for each keypoint.

The price is resolution: at stride 4 the peak can only sit on a grid of spacing 4 pixels, and $K$ maps cost far more memory than $2K$ numbers. How dense prediction networks recover resolution is covered in [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-segmentation/#upsample-the-scores-not-the-labels); the next section shows how much the grid costs and how to get most of it back.

### Decoding a heatmap: argmax, quantization error and sub-pixel refinement

**Plain version.** Reading a heatmap is like reading a thermometer with coarse marks. Reporting the nearest mark (the brightest cell) is off by up to half a mark. Looking at how bright the *neighbouring* cells are tells you which way, and how far, to move between the marks.

The simplest decoder takes the brightest cell and maps it back to the image:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = s \cdot \arg\max_{\mathbf{p} \in \Omega} \hat{H}_k(\mathbf{p}).
$$

If the true position falls uniformly at random inside a cell, the error of this decoder in each axis is uniform on $[-s/2, s/2]$, and the expected Euclidean error is about $0.383\,s$ (the mean distance from the centre of a unit square to a random point in it, $(\sqrt{2} + \ln(1 + \sqrt{2}))/6 \approx 0.383$, scaled by $s$). At stride 4 that is about 1.5 pixels of error *before the network makes any mistake*. On a person 100 pixels tall, that is enough to lose points on the strict thresholds of the OKS metric introduced later.

Three refinements are common:

- **The quarter offset.** Move the estimate a quarter of a cell from the peak toward its higher neighbour. The stacked hourglass paper added this to improve results at high precision thresholds [8], and SimpleBaseline uses it as standard practice [9].
- **A Taylor step on the log-heatmap.** The logarithm of a Gaussian is a quadratic (a parabola), so its maximum can be found exactly from the value, slope and curvature at the brightest cell: $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{m} - \mathbf{D}''(\mathbf{m})^{-1} \mathbf{D}'(\mathbf{m})$, where $\mathbf{m}$ is the brightest cell, and $\mathbf{D}'$ and $\mathbf{D}''$ are the gradient and Hessian of $\log \hat{H}_k$ there, estimated by finite differences. This is the decoding step of DARK [10]. DARK also smooths the predicted heatmap with a Gaussian before decoding, because real predictions are not perfect Gaussians, and centres the training targets on the exact (unrounded) keypoint position, as our formula for $H_k^*$ already does.
- **Soft-argmax (integral regression).** Treat the heatmap as a probability distribution and output its *mean*: $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k = s \sum_{\mathbf{p}} \mathbf{p}\, \tilde{H}_k(\mathbf{p})$, with $\tilde{H}_k = \operatorname{softmax}(\hat{H}_k)$ over all cells. Sun et al. [11] showed that this turns the heatmap into a differentiable regression output, so the network can be trained end to end on the coordinates, without quantization.

DARK's ablation makes the size of the effect concrete: for an HRNet-W32 model with a small $128 \times 96$ input on COCO validation, AP was 61.2 with plain argmax, 66.9 with the quarter offset and 68.4 with its Taylor decoding [10]. The decoder alone moved AP by more than 7 points.

The code encodes 500 random keypoints into heatmaps at strides 1, 2, 4 and 8, adds a little noise to imitate an imperfect prediction, and decodes them with each method. All numbers come from this synthetic run.

```python
import numpy as np

def encode(xy, shape, stride, sigma=2.0):
    """Gaussian heatmap of size shape=(H, W) cells for a keypoint at image position xy."""
    cx, cy = xy[0] / stride, xy[1] / stride            # exact (unrounded) center in cells
    ys, xs = np.mgrid[0:shape[0], 0:shape[1]]
    return np.exp(-((xs - cx) ** 2 + (ys - cy) ** 2) / (2 * sigma ** 2))

def peak(h):
    y, x = np.unravel_index(np.argmax(h), h.shape)
    return x, y

def decode_argmax(h, stride):
    x, y = peak(h)
    return np.array([x, y], float) * stride

def decode_quarter(h, stride):
    """Move a quarter cell from the peak toward the higher neighbour on each axis."""
    x, y = peak(h)
    p = np.array([x, y], float)
    if 0 < x < h.shape[1] - 1: p[0] += 0.25 * np.sign(h[y, x + 1] - h[y, x - 1])
    if 0 < y < h.shape[0] - 1: p[1] += 0.25 * np.sign(h[y + 1, x] - h[y - 1, x])
    return p * stride

def decode_taylor(h, stride):
    """One Newton step on log h at the peak: offset = -Hessian^-1 * gradient."""
    x, y = peak(h)
    p = np.array([x, y], float)
    if 1 <= x < h.shape[1] - 1 and 1 <= y < h.shape[0] - 1:
        L = np.log(np.maximum(h[y - 1:y + 2, x - 1:x + 2], 1e-10))
        g = np.array([L[1, 2] - L[1, 0], L[2, 1] - L[0, 1]]) / 2
        hxx = L[1, 2] - 2 * L[1, 1] + L[1, 0]
        hyy = L[2, 1] - 2 * L[1, 1] + L[0, 1]
        hxy = (L[2, 2] - L[2, 0] - L[0, 2] + L[0, 0]) / 4
        Hs = np.array([[hxx, hxy], [hxy, hyy]])
        if np.linalg.det(Hs) > 0 and hxx < 0:          # a proper maximum
            p += -np.linalg.solve(Hs, g)
    return p * stride

def decode_soft(logits, stride):
    """Soft-argmax: the expected position under softmax(logits)."""
    w = np.exp(logits - logits.max()); w /= w.sum()
    ys, xs = np.mgrid[0:w.shape[0], 0:w.shape[1]]
    return np.array([(w * xs).sum(), (w * ys).sum()]) * stride

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
W, H = 192, 256                                    # input crop size in pixels (width, height)
pts = rng.uniform([16, 16], [W - 16, H - 16], (500, 2))

def mean_error(decoder, s, noise=0.01, use_log=False):
    shape, e = (H // s, W // s), 0.0
    for xy in pts:
        h = encode(xy, shape, s)
        h = np.clip(h + rng.normal(0, noise, h.shape), 1e-6, None)   # an imperfect "prediction"
        e += np.linalg.norm(decoder(np.log(h) if use_log else h, s) - xy)
    return e / len(pts)

print("stride  argmax  quarter  taylor   (mean error in input pixels)")
for s in (1, 2, 4, 8):
    print(f"{s:6d}  {mean_error(decode_argmax, s):6.2f}  {mean_error(decode_quarter, s):7.2f}"
          f"  {mean_error(decode_taylor, s):6.2f}")
print(f"soft-argmax, stride 4: clean map {mean_error(decode_soft, 4, 0.0, True):.2f},"
      f" noisy map {mean_error(decode_soft, 4, 0.01, True):.2f}")
```

```text
stride  argmax  quarter  taylor   (mean error in input pixels)
     1    0.39     0.20    0.05
     2    0.80     0.40    0.11
     4    1.54     0.78    0.21
     8    3.20     1.59    0.43
soft-argmax, stride 4: clean map 0.04, noisy map 24.42
```

Four lessons are visible, and Figure 2 plots the first three.

1. **Argmax error grows linearly with the stride** and follows the $0.383\,s$ prediction closely (0.38, 0.77, 1.53, 3.06 for strides 1 to 8).
2. **The quarter offset halves it**, at no cost.
3. **The Taylor step removes most of what remains**, because the log of a Gaussian really is a parabola; what is left comes from the added noise.
4. **Soft-argmax is exact on a clean map but fails badly on a noisy one.** With logits equal to $\log \hat{H}$, the softmax weights are proportional to $\hat{H}$, and a small noise floor spread over thousands of cells outweighs the narrow peak and pulls the mean toward the centre of the map. Integral regression trains the network *through* the soft-argmax, which pushes it to put almost no mass away from the keypoint; applied after the fact to a heatmap trained with MSE, soft-argmax is a poor decoder.

### Other output representations

Two newer representations sit between heatmaps and plain regression. **Coordinate classification** (SimCC [12]) predicts two one-dimensional distributions per keypoint, over $x$ and over $y$, with several bins per pixel, which cuts quantization error without a large 2D map. **Likelihood-based regression** (RLE [13]) keeps direct regression but learns the shape of the error distribution during training, and its paper reports regression beating heatmap methods on multi-person pose. Both are the natural outputs for the one-stage and query-based models later in this chapter.

## Networks for single-person heatmaps

**Plain version.** To place a wrist precisely, the network needs fine detail (high resolution). To know that this blob *is* a left wrist and not a right one, it needs to see the whole body (a large receptive field). Every architecture below is a different way to have both at once.

The input is a **person crop**: a box around one person, enlarged a little, padded to a fixed aspect ratio and resized to a fixed size such as $256 \times 192$ (height × width). The output is $K$ heatmaps at stride 4, $64 \times 48$ in this case.

- **Convolutional Pose Machines (CPM)** [14] stack several stages. Each stage sees the image features *and* the previous stage's heatmaps (belief maps), so later stages can use the positions of other joints as context, for example "an elbow lies between a shoulder and a wrist". Each stage is supervised (**intermediate supervision**), which counters vanishing gradients in a deep stack.
- **Stacked hourglass networks** [8] repeat a symmetric encoder–decoder ("hourglass") module several times, with intermediate supervision after each one. Every hourglass pools down to a very coarse resolution to gather whole-body context and upsamples back with skip connections, the same encoder–decoder idea described for segmentation in [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-segmentation/#encoderdecoders-and-skip-connections).
- **SimpleBaseline** [9] showed that much of this machinery was unnecessary. It takes an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet ([DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/)) and adds three deconvolution (transposed convolution) layers on top of its last stage to produce $64 \times 48$ heatmaps for a $256 \times 192$ input. Its ablation found that three deconvolution layers (heatmaps at $64 \times 48$) beat two (at $32 \times 24$) by 2.5 AP, a direct measurement of the resolution cost discussed above.
- **HRNet** [15] keeps a high-resolution branch alive through the whole network instead of recovering resolution at the end. It adds lower-resolution branches one by one, runs them **in parallel** and repeatedly exchanges information between all resolutions (multi-scale fusion). The heatmaps are read from the high-resolution branch, which has seen coarse context many times. Several later methods in this chapter build on HRNet: DARK reports its decoding results with HRNet models, and HigherHRNet and DEKR extend it to bottom-up pose.
- **ViTPose** [16] replaces the convolutional backbone by a plain, non-hierarchical vision transformer ([ML 05](/en/learn/ml/vision-transformers/)) with $16 \times 16$ patches, followed by a lightweight decoder of a few deconvolution layers (or even a single upsampling layer and a convolution). The paper scales it from 100M to 1B parameters and shows that simple design plus large pretrained transformers sets a new accuracy–throughput trade-off.

*Optional: [Simple Baselines (Xiao et al., 2018)](#simple-baselines-xiao-et-al-2018) (in the Paper guides). What the paper's ablations measure and what to check when you compare its numbers with other methods.*

Two training details matter for every model in this list. **Data augmentation** rotates, scales and flips the crops; a horizontal flip must swap the left and right keypoint channels. **Flip testing** runs the model on the crop and on its mirror image, swaps the channels of the mirrored output back, and averages the heatmaps; the stacked hourglass paper reports about a 1% average improvement on validation from this [8], and later models adopted it as standard practice [9].

## Many people: top-down versus bottom-up

**Plain version.** With several people in a photo there are two orders of work. **Top-down**: first find each person, then find the joints inside each person's box. **Bottom-up**: first find every joint in the picture, then work out which joints belong together. A third family does both at once.

### Top-down: detect, then estimate

A person detector ([DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/)) proposes boxes; each box is cropped, resized and passed to a single-person network such as SimpleBaseline, HRNet or ViTPose; the heatmaps are decoded and the keypoints mapped back to image coordinates. Mask R-CNN [17] builds the same idea into the detector: its keypoint branch predicts, inside each region of interest, $K$ masks of size $m \times m$, each trained as an $m^2$-way softmax whose target is a single "one-hot" pixel at the keypoint (see [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#two-stage-detectors-the-r-cnn-family) for the RoI machinery).

Top-down methods are accurate because every person is resized to the same comfortable scale before the keypoint network sees them, which helps most for small and medium-sized people [18]. Their weaknesses follow from the pipeline:

- **Cost grows with the number of people**, since the keypoint network runs once per box.
- **They fail where the detector fails.** A missed person gets no pose; NMS ([DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#non-maximum-suppression-nms-and-soft-nms)) may delete one of two overlapping people; and a box that contains two people gives the single-person network two left wrists to choose from. The OpenPose authors measured this on a COCO validation subset: the same single-person model scored 62.7 AP with ground-truth boxes but 52.7 AP with boxes from an SSD detector [18].

### Bottom-up: detect all keypoints, then group

A bottom-up model runs once on the whole image and outputs $K$ heatmaps that contain the keypoints of *all* people. Peaks are found with a local-maximum test, which is a per-keypoint NMS. The hard part is **grouping**: deciding which left elbow goes with which left wrist.

**Part affinity fields (PAFs).** OpenPose [18] predicts, besides the heatmaps, one 2D vector field per limb type. At a pixel on limb $c$ of person $k$, the target is the unit vector pointing along that limb; elsewhere it is zero:

$$
\mathbf{L}^{*}_{c,k}(\mathbf{p}) = \begin{cases} \mathbf{v} = \dfrac{\mathbf{x}_{j_2,k} - \mathbf{x}_{j_1,k}}{\lVert \mathbf{x}_{j_2,k} - \mathbf{x}_{j_1,k} \rVert_2} & \text{if } \mathbf{p} \text{ lies on limb } c \text{ of person } k, \\ \mathbf{0} & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
$$

where $j_1$ and $j_2$ are the two keypoint types joined by limb $c$ (for example left elbow and left wrist), $\mathbf{x}_{j,k}$ is the position of keypoint $j$ of person $k$, and "on the limb" means within a distance $\sigma_l$ (the limb width, in pixels) of the segment between them. Where limbs of several people overlap, the targets are averaged. To score a candidate connection between two detected keypoints $\mathbf{d}_{j_1}$ and $\mathbf{d}_{j_2}$, OpenPose integrates the predicted field $\mathbf{L}_c$ along the segment joining them:

$$
E = \int_{0}^{1} \mathbf{L}_c\big(\mathbf{p}(u)\big) \cdot \frac{\mathbf{d}_{j_2} - \mathbf{d}_{j_1}}{\lVert \mathbf{d}_{j_2} - \mathbf{d}_{j_1} \rVert_2} \, du, \qquad \mathbf{p}(u) = (1 - u)\,\mathbf{d}_{j_1} + u\,\mathbf{d}_{j_2},
$$

where $u$ runs along the segment, and the integral is approximated by sampling a few evenly spaced values of $u$. $E$ is high when the field along the segment points the same way as the segment, which happens only if the two keypoints really are the ends of one limb. Finding the best assignment for all limbs at once is NP-hard, so OpenPose relaxes it: it keeps only the edges of a tree skeleton and solves one **bipartite matching** per limb type, for example with the Hungarian algorithm (see [DLCV 07](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-tracking/#association-cost-matrices-and-the-hungarian-algorithm)), then joins limbs that share keypoints into people.

The code builds the ground-truth PAFs of two people standing side by side whose left forearms cross, adds small noise to the keypoint positions, and groups the keypoints limb by limb, once with the PAF score and once with plain distance (the shortest total limb length). The coordinates are synthetic.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

# 13 body keypoints and the 12 limbs of a tree that connects them
J = ["nose", "l_sho", "r_sho", "l_elb", "r_elb", "l_wri", "r_wri",
     "l_hip", "r_hip", "l_knee", "r_knee", "l_ank", "r_ank"]
LIMBS = [(0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 3), (3, 5), (2, 4), (4, 6),
         (1, 7), (2, 8), (7, 9), (9, 11), (8, 10), (10, 12)]

# two people side by side; their left forearms cross (synthetic coordinates)
A = np.array([[40, 20], [52, 38], [28, 38], [70, 50], [24, 62], [100, 74], [22, 86],
              [48, 80], [32, 80], [50, 110], [30, 110], [52, 140], [28, 140]], float)
B = np.array([[72, 24], [84, 42], [62, 42], [98, 58], [56, 64], [74, 76], [52, 86],
              [80, 84], [66, 84], [82, 114], [64, 114], [84, 144], [62, 144]], float)
people = [A, B]
H, W, width = 160, 120, 3.0

def render_paf(c):
    """Ground-truth part affinity field of limb c: unit vectors near each person's limb."""
    ys, xs = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W]
    field, count = np.zeros((H, W, 2)), np.zeros((H, W))
    for P in people:
        a, b = P[LIMBS[c][0]], P[LIMBS[c][1]]
        v = (b - a) / np.linalg.norm(b - a)
        along = (xs - a[0]) * v[0] + (ys - a[1]) * v[1]
        across = np.abs(-(xs - a[0]) * v[1] + (ys - a[1]) * v[0])
        on = (along >= 0) & (along <= np.linalg.norm(b - a)) & (across <= width)
        field[on] += v; count[on] += 1
    return field / np.maximum(count, 1)[..., None]          # average where limbs overlap

def limb_score(field, p, q, n=10):
    """Line integral of the field along the segment p -> q, approximated by n samples."""
    d = (q - p) / np.linalg.norm(q - p)
    u = np.linspace(0, 1, n)[:, None]
    pts = np.round(p + u * (q - p)).astype(int)
    return np.mean(field[pts[:, 1], pts[:, 0]] @ d)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
cands = [np.stack([A[j], B[j]]) + rng.normal(0, 1.0, (2, 2)) for j in range(13)]  # detections

ok_paf = ok_near = 0
for c, (j1, j2) in enumerate(LIMBS):
    field = render_paf(c)
    S = np.array([[limb_score(field, p, q) for q in cands[j2]] for p in cands[j1]])
    D = np.linalg.norm(cands[j1][:, None] - cands[j2][None], axis=2)
    if (j1, j2) == (3, 5):                                    # the crossing forearms
        print("left elbow -> left wrist, PAF scores (rows: elbows A, B; cols: wrists A, B)")
        print(np.round(S, 2))
        print("distances in pixels"); print(np.round(D, 1))
    r, k = linear_sum_assignment(-S)                          # maximize total affinity
    ok_paf += np.sum(k == r)                                  # candidate i belongs to person i
    r, k = linear_sum_assignment(D)                           # baseline: shortest total length
    ok_near += np.sum(k == r)
print(f"correct limbs with PAF scores:     {ok_paf}/{2 * len(LIMBS)}")
print(f"correct limbs with distance only:  {ok_near}/{2 * len(LIMBS)}")
```

**Associative embedding.** Newell et al. [19] avoid limb fields altogether. Alongside each keypoint heatmap the network outputs a **tag map**: one real number per pixel. Training pushes the tags of one person's keypoints to be equal and the tags of different people to be different. With $h_k(\mathbf{x})$ the tag of keypoint type $k$ at pixel $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{x}_{nk}$ the ground-truth position of keypoint $k$ of person $n$, $N$ people and $\bar{h}_n = \frac{1}{K} \sum_k h_k(\mathbf{x}_{nk})$ the mean tag of person $n$, the grouping loss is

$$
\mathcal{L}_g = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n} \sum_{k} \big(\bar{h}_n - h_k(\mathbf{x}_{nk})\big)^2 + \frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{n} \sum_{n'} \exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \big(\bar{h}_n - \bar{h}_{n'}\big)^2\Big),
$$

where the first term pulls each person's tags toward their mean and the second pushes the means of different people apart, with a penalty that fades as the distance between them grows ($\sigma$ sets how far apart is "far enough"). The absolute tag values do not matter, only their differences. At test time, keypoints are added to people joint by joint, matching tags that are close and detections that are confident. CornerNet borrowed exactly this idea to pair box corners ([DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#anchor-free-detectors-points-instead-of-boxes)).

**Scale.** Bottom-up methods see all people at the image's own scale, so small people get few heatmap cells; OpenPose was less accurate than top-down methods on medium-sized people for this reason [18]. HigherHRNet [20] adds higher-resolution heatmaps (HRNet's output upsampled by a transposed convolution) and supervises and aggregates several resolutions; its paper reports results above all top-down methods on the CrowdPose test set, where boxes are unreliable. DEKR [21] returns to **dense regression**, with one branch per keypoint whose adaptive convolutions focus on that keypoint's region, and reports better results than detection-and-grouping methods on COCO and CrowdPose.

### One-stage and set-prediction pose

A third family predicts a whole pose per person directly, without a separate grouping step.

- **Center-based.** CenterNet ("Objects as Points") [22] detects each person as a centre point on a heatmap and regresses the $2K$ offsets from the centre to the keypoints; it then snaps each regressed keypoint to the nearest peak of a separate keypoint heatmap, so the centre acts as the grouping cue.
- **Real-time dense heads.** RTMO [23] puts a pose head into a YOLO-style one-stage detector and represents each keypoint with a pair of one-dimensional heatmaps (coordinate classification), aiming at top-down accuracy at one-stage speed.
- **Set prediction.** Transformer detectors in the DETR style ([DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#set-prediction-with-transformers-detr-and-its-descendants)) output a fixed set of queries matched one-to-one to the ground truth, so no NMS or grouping is needed. ED-Pose [24] applies this to pose with two explicit box-detection stages: a human decoder detects people, and a human-to-keypoint decoder treats each keypoint as a small box to detect. Trained with an L1 regression loss and no heatmaps, it reports results above heatmap-based top-down methods with the same backbone on COCO.

### Choosing a pipeline

| | Top-down | Bottom-up | One-stage / set prediction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main step | Detect people, then estimate each | Detect all keypoints, then group | Predict each person's pose directly |
| Cost vs number of people | Grows linearly | Nearly constant network cost; grouping grows | Nearly constant |
| Small people | Good (each crop is resized) | Harder (one image scale for all) | Depends on the feature pyramid |
| Crowds and overlap | Boxes overlap; NMS can remove people | Grouping can mix people | Queries or centres can collide |
| Typical use | High accuracy when people are few and well separated | Many people at once; real-time systems | End-to-end training; real-time |

## Measuring pose accuracy

**Plain version.** A predicted keypoint is "good" if it is close to the true one, but "close" must depend on the size of the person and on the keypoint: being 5 pixels off on a tiny person's nose is a big mistake; 5 pixels off on a large person's hip is not.

### PCK and PCKh

The **percentage of correct keypoints (PCK)** counts a predicted keypoint as correct if it lies within a threshold distance of the ground truth, where the threshold is a fraction of the person's size. Yang and Ramanan used a fraction of the person's bounding-box size [25]. MPII's **PCKh** [26] uses 50% of the **head segment length** instead, because head size does not change when the person bends or stretches. Formally,

$$
\text{PCKh@}\alpha = \frac{1}{\sum_{n,k} \delta(v_{nk} \gt 0)} \sum_{n,k} \delta(v_{nk} \gt 0)\; \mathbb{1}\!\left[\lVert \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{nk} - \mathbf{x}_{nk} \rVert_2 \le \alpha\, \ell_n\right],
$$

where $n$ indexes people, $k$ keypoints, $\ell_n$ is the head segment length of person $n$, $\alpha$ is the fraction ($\alpha = 0.5$ for the standard PCKh), $\delta(\cdot)$ and $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ are 1 when their condition holds and 0 otherwise, and only labeled keypoints count. PCK assumes the person has been found; it does not penalize missed or invented people, which is why multi-person benchmarks use an AP-style metric.

### Object keypoint similarity and keypoint AP

COCO's metric mirrors box detection ([DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#the-coco-protocol)). In box AP, a detection is matched to a ground-truth box if their IoU passes a threshold. In keypoint AP, a predicted pose is matched to a ground-truth person if their **object keypoint similarity (OKS)** passes a threshold [4]:

$$
\text{OKS} = \frac{\sum_{i} \exp\!\big(-d_i^2 / (2 s^2 \kappa_i^2)\big)\, \delta(v_i \gt 0)}{\sum_{i} \delta(v_i \gt 0)},
$$

where $i$ runs over the keypoints, $d_i$ is the Euclidean distance between the predicted and ground-truth keypoint $i$, $v_i$ is the ground-truth visibility flag, $s$ is the object scale, defined as the square root of the person's segmentation area, and $\kappa_i$ is a per-keypoint constant. Each term is an unnormalized Gaussian of the error with standard deviation $s \kappa_i$, so it is 1 for a perfect keypoint and falls toward 0 as the error grows; OKS averages it over the labeled keypoints. The predicted visibility flags are not used.

**Where the constants come from.** COCO had 5,000 validation images annotated twice and measured, for each keypoint type, how much human annotators disagree relative to the person's scale, $\sigma_i^2 = E[d_i^2 / s^2]$. It then set $\kappa_i = 2\sigma_i$ [4]. The values of $\sigma_i$ are 0.026 for the nose, 0.025 for the eyes, 0.035 for the ears, 0.079 for the shoulders, 0.072 for the elbows, 0.062 for the wrists, 0.107 for the hips, 0.087 for the knees and 0.089 for the ankles [4], [27]. Eyes are easy to place exactly; hips, hidden under clothing, are not, so the metric is more forgiving for hips. With $\kappa_i = 2\sigma_i$, errors of one, two and three annotator standard deviations give similarities of $e^{-1/8} \approx 0.88$, $e^{-1/2} \approx 0.61$ and $e^{-9/8} \approx 0.32$, and human annotators reach a median OKS of about 0.91 against each other [4].

**From OKS to AP.** The rest is the AP of [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#precision-recall-and-ap): rank all predicted poses by their person-level score, match each to the unmatched ground-truth person with the highest OKS, and compute AP at OKS thresholds $0.50, 0.55, \dots, 0.95$. The primary metric, AP, averages these ten values. Companion metrics are $\text{AP}^{50}$, $\text{AP}^{75}$, $\text{AP}^{M}$ for medium people (area between $32^2$ and $96^2$) and $\text{AP}^{L}$ for large ones (area above $96^2$), plus the matching AR values. At most 20 poses per image are scored, and small people have no keypoint labels [4].

The code computes OKS with COCO's constants for a synthetic person about 200 pixels tall.

```python
import numpy as np

NAMES = ["nose", "l_eye", "r_eye", "l_ear", "r_ear", "l_shoulder", "r_shoulder",
         "l_elbow", "r_elbow", "l_wrist", "r_wrist", "l_hip", "r_hip",
         "l_knee", "r_knee", "l_ankle", "r_ankle"]
SIGMAS = np.array([.26, .25, .25, .35, .35, .79, .79, .72, .72, .62, .62,
                   1.07, 1.07, .87, .87, .89, .89]) / 10.0       # COCO per-keypoint constants

def oks(pred, gt, vis, area):
    """pred, gt: (17, 2) pixel coordinates; vis: (17,) COCO flags; area: segment area (s^2)."""
    d2 = ((pred - gt) ** 2).sum(1)
    kappa = 2 * SIGMAS
    ks = np.exp(-d2 / (2 * area * kappa ** 2))                 # per-keypoint similarity
    labeled = vis > 0
    return ks[labeled].mean(), ks

# a standing person about 200 px tall, facing the camera (synthetic coordinates)
gt = np.array([[100, 30], [106, 25], [94, 25], [112, 28], [88, 28], [125, 60], [75, 60],
               [135, 95], [65, 95], [140, 128], [60, 128], [115, 130], [85, 130],
               [117, 175], [83, 175], [118, 220], [82, 220]], float)
vis = np.full(17, 2); vis[3] = 0                                # left ear not labeled
area = 0.6 * (70 * 200)                                         # segment area: ~60% of its box

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
jitter = gt + rng.normal(0, 3, gt.shape)                        # every keypoint off by ~3 px per axis
flipped = jitter.copy(); flipped[[9, 10]] = flipped[[10, 9]]    # left/right wrists swapped
print(f"perfect          OKS = {oks(gt, gt, vis, area)[0]:.3f}")
print(f"3 px jitter      OKS = {oks(jitter, gt, vis, area)[0]:.3f}")
print(f"+ wrists swapped OKS = {oks(flipped, gt, vis, area)[0]:.3f}")

# the same 5-pixel error costs very different amounts on different keypoints
for k in (0, 5, 11):
    p = gt.copy(); p[k, 0] += 5
    print(f"5 px error on {NAMES[k]:10s}: similarity {oks(p, gt, vis, area)[1][k]:.3f}")
```

```text
3 px jitter      OKS = 0.933
+ wrists swapped OKS = 0.810
5 px error on nose      : similarity 0.577
5 px error on l_shoulder: similarity 0.942

```

### Metrics for 3D pose

For 3D pose, the standard metric is the **mean per-joint position error (MPJPE)**, the average Euclidean distance between predicted and ground-truth 3D joints,

$$
\text{MPJPE} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big\lVert \hat{\mathbf{X}}_k - \mathbf{X}_k \big\rVert_2,
$$

reported in millimetres and averaged over all test frames, where $\hat{\mathbf{X}}_k$ and $\mathbf{X}_k$ are the predicted and true 3D positions of joint $k$. Two protocols are common on Human3.6M: errors after aligning the root (central hip) joints of prediction and ground truth, called Protocol #1, and errors after an additional rigid alignment of the predicted skeleton to the ground truth, called Protocol #2 [28]; the second metric is also known as PA-MPJPE or reconstruction error [2]. The second hides errors in global rotation and scale, so the two numbers must never be compared with each other.

### What the numbers do not tell you

Like box AP, keypoint AP mixes several kinds of error. Ronchi and Perona [29] split keypoint localization errors into four types: **jitter** (near the right keypoint but outside the human error margin), **inversion** (at the person's own left–right twin or another of their keypoints), **swap** (at a keypoint of a *different person*) and **miss** (near no keypoint). They found that errors depend most on the number of visible keypoints and on clutter from nearby people. Even COCO's doubly annotated data contain 1–2% outliers, mostly left–right confusions or the wrong person labeled [4]. Diagnose models by error type, not only by AP.

## Datasets and benchmarks

**Plain version.** A handful of datasets defined what "good" means: COCO for many people in everyday photos, MPII for a wide range of activities, CrowdPose for heavy overlap, Human3.6M for 3D, and newer sets for whole bodies, video and animals.

| Dataset | Content (from the papers or official pages) | Metric | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| COCO keypoints [30], [4] | 17 body keypoints on people in everyday photos; small people unlabeled | OKS AP | The main multi-person benchmark |
| MPII Human Pose [26] | 40,522 annotated people in images from YouTube videos, collected with queries for more than 800 activities | PCKh | Diverse activities; single- and multi-person tracks |
| CrowdPose [31] | 20,000 images, about 80,000 people, 14 keypoints, selected for a uniform spread of crowding | OKS AP | Crowded scenes |
| COCO-WholeBody [32] | 133 landmarks per person: 68 face, 42 hand, 23 body and feet | OKS AP | Face, hands and feet together |
| PoseTrack [33] | Videos with person tracks and articulated poses | AP, MOTA | Pose estimation and tracking in video |
| Human3.6M [34] | 3.6 million 3D poses of 11 actors in 17 scenarios, recorded by 4 calibrated cameras with motion capture | MPJPE | The most widely used 3D benchmark |
| AP-10K [35] | 10,015 images of 54 mammal species from 23 families, annotated in COCO style | AP | Animal pose and cross-species generalization |

CrowdPose deserves a closer look because it defines crowding precisely. For person $i$, let $N_a^i$ be the number of its own joints inside its box and $N_b^i$ the number of *other* people's joints inside the same box; the **Crowd Index** of an image with $n$ people is $\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i} N_b^i / N_a^i$ [31]. Common datasets are dominated by uncrowded images; CrowdPose was selected so that this index is spread nearly uniformly between 0 and 1, and its authors found the accuracy of strong models to be about 20 AP lower on crowded images than on uncrowded ones [31].

Two cautions apply when reading results. Human3.6M is an indoor dataset with a few actors [2], [34], so low MPJPE there does not imply good results on outdoor video. And on COCO, results depend on the person detector used by top-down methods (see the [Simple Baselines guide](#simple-baselines-xiao-et-al-2018)), on input resolution and on extra training data, so compare numbers only under the same settings.

## Beyond the human body

**Plain version.** Nothing in a heatmap network is specific to people. Change the list of keypoints and the training data, and the same machinery finds the corners of a face, the joints of a hand, or the paws of a dog.

- **Whole body.** COCO-WholeBody [32] adds feet, hands and dense face landmarks to the body keypoints, which creates a huge range of scales inside one person; its baseline model, ZoomNet, is built around the body's hierarchical structure to handle this. The journal version of OpenPose [36] added foot keypoints.
- **Animals.** AP-10K [35] benchmarks pose across 54 mammal species and tests generalization to unseen animal families. In neuroscience, DeepLabCut [1] uses transfer learning from a pretrained network to track user-defined body parts of mice and flies from about 200 labeled frames in its experiments, replacing reflective markers.
- **Objects.** The same formulation works for any category with consistent landmarks, such as the wheels and lights of cars, provided the keypoints are defined consistently and the category's left–right symmetries are handled like the body's.

## From 2D to 3D pose

**Plain version.** A photo flattens the world: from one image, a hand pointing at the camera and a hand held at the hip can look almost the same. There are three ways to get the depth back: *guess* it from experience (lifting), *measure* it with two or more cameras (triangulation), or *fit* a model of the human body that only allows realistic shapes.

### Lifting 2D keypoints

Martinez et al. [28] showed that a surprisingly large part of monocular 3D pose estimation is solved once the 2D keypoints are known. Their model takes the $2K$ coordinates of a detected 2D pose and maps them with a simple fully connected network (linear layers with batch normalization, dropout and residual connections) to $3K$ coordinates. Trained on Human3.6M, it beat the previous best methods by a large margin when given ground-truth 2D keypoints, which suggests that much of the remaining difficulty lies in the 2D detection rather than in the lifting. Lifting is also *ambiguous*: several 3D poses project to the same 2D pose, and a network with a single output can return only one of them.

VideoPose3D [37] lifts a whole *sequence* of 2D poses with dilated temporal convolutions, so motion over neighbouring frames helps resolve depth and jitter. It also introduced **back-projection**, a semi-supervised loss that estimates 3D poses for unlabeled video and requires them to project back onto the detected 2D keypoints.

### Multi-view triangulation

With two or more calibrated cameras, depth is no longer a guess. Each camera's 2D keypoint defines a ray in space, and the 3D joint is the point where the rays (almost) meet. The geometry is the triangulation of [DLCV 11](/en/learn/deep-learning/3d-reconstruction/#triangulation), applied joint by joint: the camera matrices are the pinhole models of that chapter, and the linear (DLT) solution is derived in its [Supplementary notes](/en/learn/deep-learning/3d-reconstruction/notes/linear-triangulation-dlt/). Learnable triangulation [38] makes this step part of the network: it weights each view by a confidence predicted from the image, so that a view in which the joint is occluded contributes less, and its second variant aggregates 2D features from all views into a 3D volume refined by 3D convolutions.

The code projects a synthetic 13-joint skeleton, standing 4 m from two converging cameras, into both images, adds 2-pixel noise to the 2D keypoints, triangulates every joint with the DLT and reports MPJPE for three distances between the cameras (the **baseline**). The numbers come from this synthetic run, without root alignment.

```python
import numpy as np

def camera(K, angle_deg, center):
    """3x4 projection P = K [R | t] for a camera at `center` rotated about the y axis."""
    a = np.deg2rad(angle_deg)
    R = np.array([[np.cos(a), 0, -np.sin(a)], [0, 1, 0], [np.sin(a), 0, np.cos(a)]])
    return K @ np.hstack([R, -R @ center[:, None]])

def project(P, X):
    x = (P @ np.hstack([X, np.ones((len(X), 1))]).T).T
    return x[:, :2] / x[:, 2:]

def triangulate(Ps, xs):
    """Linear (DLT) triangulation of one point seen in several views."""
    A = []
    for P, (u, v) in zip(Ps, xs):
        A += [u * P[2] - P[0], v * P[2] - P[1]]
    X = np.linalg.svd(np.array(A))[2][-1]
    return X[:3] / X[3]

# a 13-joint 3D skeleton in millimetres, standing 4 m in front of the cameras (synthetic)
skel = np.array([[0, -1650, 0], [170, -1400, 0], [-170, -1400, 0], [250, -1120, 50], [-250, -1120, 50],
                 [280, -860, 120], [-280, -860, 120], [100, -900, 0], [-100, -900, 0],
                 [110, -480, 30], [-110, -480, 30], [110, -60, 0], [-110, -60, 0]], float)
skel += [0, 900, 4000]
K = np.array([[1000, 0, 640], [0, 1000, 360], [0, 0, 1]], float)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for baseline in (200, 1000, 3000):                          # distance between the cameras (mm)
    half = baseline / 2
    Ps = [camera(K, np.degrees(np.arctan2(half, 4000)), np.array([-half, 0, 0.])),
          camera(K, -np.degrees(np.arctan2(half, 4000)), np.array([half, 0, 0.]))]
    errs = []
    for trial in range(200):                                  # 2D detections with 2 px noise
        obs = [project(P, skel) + rng.normal(0, 2.0, (13, 2)) for P in Ps]
        est = np.array([triangulate(Ps, [o[j] for o in obs]) for j in range(13)])
        errs.append(np.linalg.norm(est - skel, axis=1).mean())   # MPJPE of this trial
    print(f"baseline {baseline:4d} mm: MPJPE = {np.mean(errs):6.1f} mm")
```

```text
baseline  200 mm: MPJPE =  183.7 mm
baseline 1000 mm: MPJPE =   37.9 mm
baseline 3000 mm: MPJPE =   17.4 mm
```

With cameras 20 cm apart, a 2-pixel image error becomes about 18 cm in 3D, almost all of it in depth (a per-axis check of the same run gives 182 mm in depth against 9 and 18 mm across): the rays are nearly parallel, so a small shift moves their crossing point far along them. A wider baseline makes the rays cross at a larger angle and cuts the error by a factor of ten.

### Parametric body models and mesh recovery

A skeleton says nothing about body shape, and nothing stops a lifting network from predicting a forearm that changes length from frame to frame. **Parametric body models** fix both problems by describing a body with a small set of parameters. The most widely used is SMPL [39]. It starts from a template mesh with $N = 6890$ vertices and a skeleton with $K = 23$ joints, and generates a posed body as

$$
M(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = W\big(T_P(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \boldsymbol{\theta}),\, J(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\, \boldsymbol{\theta},\, \mathcal{W}\big), \qquad T_P(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \bar{\mathbf{T}} + B_S(\boldsymbol{\beta}) + B_P(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ are **shape** parameters (coefficients of the main modes of body-shape variation), $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are **pose** parameters (an axis–angle rotation for each joint relative to its parent, plus the global orientation: $3 \times 23 + 3 = 72$ numbers), $\bar{\mathbf{T}}$ is the template mesh, $B_S$ and $B_P$ are vertex offsets that model identity and pose-dependent deformations such as bulging muscles, $J(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ are the joint locations regressed from the shaped mesh, $\mathcal{W}$ are the blend weights and $W$ is a standard blend-skinning function that rotates each vertex around the joints. Keypoints are read off the mesh, so a fitted SMPL body always has consistent bone lengths.

**Human mesh recovery (HMR)** [40] regresses SMPL parameters and a camera directly from an image. Its main loss is the **reprojection error** of the model's joints against 2D keypoint labels, which allows training on in-the-wild images; an adversarial prior trained on real 3D bodies rejects implausible parameters. HMR 2.0, part of 4DHumans [41], makes the network fully transformer-based and tracks the reconstructed people through occlusions in video.

## Failure modes and practical advice

**Plain version.** Pose models fail where people would hesitate too: hidden joints, tangled people, mirror-image confusion, bodies cut off by the frame, poses rarely seen in training, and blur.

- **Occlusion.** An occluded joint ($v = 1$) is labeled but not shown, so the network must infer it from context; self-occlusion makes legs especially prone to left–right confusion [29].
- **Crowds.** Overlapping people break detectors, confuse grouping and cause **swap** errors [29]; accuracy falls as the Crowd Index rises [31].
- **Left–right flips.** Front and back views look alike, so a model can mirror a limb or a whole person (an **inversion**); a forgotten channel swap in flip augmentation causes the same error systematically.
- **Truncation.** Joints outside the frame are unlabeled or guessed, and crops of a partial person are poorly centred.
- **Unusual poses.** Gymnastics, dancing or lying people are rare in training data; HMR 2.0's authors single them out as a case where earlier methods struggled [41].
- **Motion blur, low resolution and domain shift.** Fast limbs smear, small people have few pixels per limb, and models trained on adult photos transfer poorly to infants or artwork [2].

Practical advice that follows from this chapter: use an unbiased encoder and a sub-pixel decoder; check that flipping swaps left and right channels; report the detector and input size with top-down results; evaluate crowded and uncrowded images separately; break errors down by type; and in video, smooth or track keypoints over time. Tracking-by-detection with the association methods of [DLCV 07](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-tracking/#the-tracking-by-detection-pipeline) carries over directly: SimpleBaseline, for example, propagates poses to the next frame with optical flow and matches them with an OKS-based similarity [9], and PoseTrack provides the benchmark [33]. Following individual surface points instead of named joints is the point-tracking problem of [DLCV 07](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-tracking/#point-tracking-a-related-trend).

## Modern view

### What the surveys say

**Zheng et al.** [2] review more than 250 deep-learning papers on 2D and 3D pose since 2014, organized as in this chapter: regression versus heatmaps, top-down versus bottom-up, and for 3D, lifting, mesh recovery and multi-view methods. Their future directions include domain adaptation, human–scene constraints and metrics for temporal smoothness.

**Chen et al.** [3] organize monocular methods into four tasks (2D or 3D, single- or multi-person), list the design tricks that proved effective (multi-stage structures, intermediate supervision, multi-scale fusion, body-structure constraints) and name occlusion and crowds as the main unresolved challenges.

**Tian et al.** [5] survey monocular 3D mesh recovery with body models, contrasting optimization-based fitting with regression networks, and point to the lack of in-the-wild 3D labels as the central difficulty.

### The state of the art, 2023–2026

- **Scaled transformers for top-down pose.** ViTPose [16] showed that plain vision transformers with simple decoders scale well (from 100M to 1B parameters in the paper), and its largest model set a new state of the art on the COCO keypoint benchmark at publication.
- **Human-centric foundation models.** Sapiens [42] pretrains vision transformers from 0.3 to 2 billion parameters on over 300 million images of humans with self-supervision, then fine-tunes them for 2D pose, body-part segmentation, depth and surface normals; its authors report that this human-specific pretraining improves all four tasks at the same compute budget.
- **Real-time systems.** RTMPose [43] (top-down, coordinate classification, engineered for CPU, GPU and mobile deployment) and RTMO [23] (one-stage) target the latency budgets of applications rather than leaderboard peaks.
- **End-to-end multi-person models.** Query-based models such as ED-Pose [24] remove NMS, grouping and heatmaps; ED-Pose reports higher COCO accuracy than heatmap-based top-down methods with the same backbone.
- **3D humans in video.** Transformer mesh recovery such as HMR 2.0 [41] reconstructs and tracks full 3D bodies from monocular video, moving the field from sparse skeletons toward complete body surfaces.

### Open problems

1. **Crowds and heavy occlusion** remain the largest accuracy gap [31], [3].
2. **In-the-wild 3D.** Accurate 3D labels are scarce outside the lab [5], and the most widely used 3D benchmark, Human3.6M, is an indoor dataset [2], so monocular 3D methods are trained and tested far from where they are used.
3. **Temporal consistency.** Per-frame metrics such as MPJPE do not measure jitter or physical plausibility over time [2].
4. **Generalization across domains and species**: infants, artwork, unusual viewpoints and unseen animal families [2], [35].
5. **Evaluation.** OKS constants were measured from annotator disagreement on COCO people [4] and do not transfer automatically to other keypoint sets, and AP hides error types such as inversions and swaps [29].

## Key takeaways

- A pose is a skeleton graph of named keypoints with positions and visibility flags; left and right are the person's own, and $v = 1$ (occluded) differs from $v = 0$ (unlabeled).
- Heatmaps (Gaussian blobs, MSE loss) dominate because they suit convolutions and express uncertainty; regression, coordinate classification and soft-argmax trade some of that for speed and end-to-end training.
- Heatmap decoding is limited by the stride: argmax loses about $0.383 s$ pixels on average; the quarter offset halves that and a Taylor step on the log-heatmap removes most of the rest.
- Top-down pipelines (detector plus SimpleBaseline, HRNet or ViTPose) lead on accuracy but scale with the number of people and inherit detector failures; bottom-up pipelines detect all keypoints once and group them with part affinity fields or associative embedding; one-stage and query-based models predict whole poses directly.
- COCO's OKS is a scale- and keypoint-normalized Gaussian similarity, $\kappa_i = 2\sigma_i$ from annotator disagreement; keypoint AP averages over OKS 0.50–0.95. PCKh thresholds at half the head length; MPJPE measures 3D error in millimetres.
- 3D pose comes from lifting 2D keypoints (ambiguous), triangulating them across calibrated views (accurate with a wide baseline) or fitting a body model such as SMPL, whose 72 pose and few shape parameters keep bodies plausible.
- Occlusion, crowds, left–right inversions, truncation, unusual poses and blur cause most errors; diagnose by error type, not only by AP.

Explain the difference.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

In our run of this experiment, with the cameras side by side the reprojection error is about 1 pixel with or without the swap, yet the swapped knee lands more than half a metre from the truth. The two knees are at nearly the same height, and with a horizontal baseline the epipolar lines are nearly horizontal, so swapping the knees moves the observation *along* its epipolar line: the two rays still meet, just at the wrong depth. With the second camera above the first, the epipolar lines become nearly vertical, the swap moves the observation off its line, and the reprojection error jumps to tens of pixels, which flags the error. <details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about what the network sees in the mirrored image: a person facing the camera, so it labels the wrist on the image's right as "left wrist". That wrist is the person's *right* wrist, mirrored. Flipping the map back without swapping channels therefore puts a blob on the true *right* wrist in the left-wrist channel. ### Sub-pixel decoding by a Taylor step

This note derives the sub-pixel refinement used by DARK [10] and implemented in the decoding code, and lists the cases where it fails.

**Setup.** Let $\hat{H}$ be one predicted heatmap and $\mathbf{m}$ the cell with the largest value. Assume that near its peak the heatmap is a scaled Gaussian with an unknown centre $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\Sigma$:

$$
\hat{H}(\mathbf{p}) \approx c \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{2} (\mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top} \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\Big),
$$

where $c \gt 0$ is the peak height and $\mathbf{p}$ is a position in heatmap units. Take the logarithm, $D(\mathbf{p}) = \log \hat{H}(\mathbf{p}) = \log c - \tfrac{1}{2} (\mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top} \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$. This is an exact quadratic in $\mathbf{p}$, with gradient $\mathbf{D}'(\mathbf{p}) = -\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$ and constant Hessian $\mathbf{D}'' = -\Sigma^{-1}$.

**The step.** Evaluate both at the brightest cell and solve for the centre: $\mathbf{D}'(\mathbf{m}) = -\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{m} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \mathbf{D}''(\mathbf{m} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$, so

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbf{m} - \mathbf{D}''(\mathbf{m})^{-1}\, \mathbf{D}'(\mathbf{m}).
$$

This is one Newton step toward the maximum of $D$. Because $D$ is exactly quadratic for a Gaussian, one step is exact; there is no need to iterate. DARK writes the same result as a second-order Taylor expansion of $D$ around $\mathbf{m}$ [10]. SIFT refines its keypoints with the same kind of step on the difference-of-Gaussians function (see the [keypoint localization note of IP 12](/en/learn/image-processing/feature-extraction/notes/keypoint-localization/)).

**Finite differences.** On the grid, with $D_{x,y}$ the log-heatmap at column $x$ and row $y$ and $(x, y) = \mathbf{m}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_x &= \tfrac{1}{2}(D_{x+1,y} - D_{x-1,y}), & D_{xx} &= D_{x+1,y} - 2D_{x,y} + D_{x-1,y}, \\
D_y &= \tfrac{1}{2}(D_{x,y+1} - D_{x,y-1}), & D_{yy} &= D_{x,y+1} - 2D_{x,y} + D_{x,y-1}, \\
D_{xy} &= \tfrac{1}{4}(D_{x+1,y+1} - D_{x-1,y+1} - D_{x+1,y-1} + D_{x-1,y-1}),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $D_x, D_y$ form $\mathbf{D}'$ and $D_{xx}, D_{xy}, D_{yy}$ form the $2 \times 2$ matrix $\mathbf{D}''$. For a quadratic function these central differences are exact, which is why the code's Taylor decoder recovers the true position on a noise-free Gaussian.

**Why the logarithm matters.** Fitting a parabola to $\hat{H}$ itself (not its log) also gives a sub-pixel estimate, but a Gaussian is not a parabola, so that estimate is biased toward the cell centre unless the blob is wide. The log turns the Gaussian into a parabola exactly.

**Edge cases and pitfalls.**

- *Peak on the border.* The central differences need all eight neighbours; at the map border the decoder must fall back to the cell centre (or a one-sided rule), as the code does.
- *Flat or saddle-shaped peaks.* If $\mathbf{D}''$ is not negative definite (determinant not positive, or $D_{xx} \ge 0$), the step can jump far away. The code checks this and keeps the cell centre.
- *Multiple peaks.* Trained networks often produce lumpy maps with several local maxima near the keypoint. DARK first smooths the predicted heatmap with a Gaussian kernel of the training width and rescales it to the original maximum [10], which makes the local shape closer to a single Gaussian.
- *Values near zero.* The log of tiny or negative predicted values is undefined or huge; clip the heatmap to a small positive floor before taking the log.
- *Biased targets.* If the training targets were centred on the *rounded* keypoint position, the network learns to put the peak on the cell centre, and no decoder can recover the lost fraction. DARK therefore centres the target Gaussian on the exact, unrounded position [10].

### Coordinate classification and likelihood-based regression

This note gives the details of the two output representations that the main text mentions between heatmaps and plain regression.

**SimCC: classify each coordinate.** For an input crop of width $W$ and height $H$ pixels, SimCC [12] divides the horizontal axis into $N_x = W \cdot k$ bins and the vertical axis into $N_y = H \cdot k$ bins, where $k \ge 1$ is the **splitting factor** (bins per pixel). A keypoint at $(x, y)$ gets the class labels $c_x \approx k x$ and $c_y \approx k y$. The backbone (a CNN such as HRNet, or a transformer) produces one feature vector per keypoint (for a CNN, the keypoint's $H' \times W'$ feature map flattened), and two single linear layers turn it into scores over the $N_x$ and $N_y$ bins. Training uses a Kullback–Leibler divergence between the predicted and target distributions, and the paper replaces hard one-hot targets by smoothed ones (equal, Laplace or Gaussian label smoothing), because a bin next to the correct one is much less wrong than a bin far away. Decoding takes the highest-scoring bin on each axis and divides by $k$.

*Quantization.* With $k$ bins per pixel the rounding error per axis is at most $1/(2k)$ pixels, compared with $s/2$ pixels for a heatmap at stride $s$. So $k = 2$ gives quarter-pixel resolution without any 2D map, and the cost grows only linearly with $W + H$ instead of with $W \cdot H$.

*Edge cases.* The two axes are classified independently, so when two candidate positions exist (two wrists of overlapping people inside one crop), the $x$ of one can be combined with the $y$ of the other, a failure a 2D heatmap does not have.

**RLE: regression as maximum likelihood.** Suppose the network predicts a mean $\hat{\mu}$ and a scale $\hat{\sigma}$ for a coordinate, and the label is $\mu_g$. If the error is assumed Gaussian, maximizing the likelihood of the label means minimizing

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\log P(\mu_g) \propto \log \hat{\sigma} + \frac{(\mu_g - \hat{\mu})^2}{2 \hat{\sigma}^2},
$$

which becomes the plain L2 loss when $\hat{\sigma}$ is held constant; a Laplace assumption with constant scale gives the L1 loss [13]. Every regression loss is therefore a hidden assumption about the shape of the error distribution. RLE [13] learns that shape instead: it rescales the error as $\bar{\mu} = (\mu_g - \hat{\mu}) / \hat{\sigma}$ and models its density with a normalizing flow that learns only the *residual* (the change) relative to a simple base density, which the paper finds easier to optimize than learning the density from scratch. The flow is used only in the training loss; at test time the network outputs $\hat{\mu}$ as an ordinary regression, so inference costs nothing extra. The paper's ablation on COCO attributes 12.4 AP of improvement over standard regression to the method, of which 7.8 AP come from the residual formulation rather than direct likelihood estimation [13].

*Why it matters for cost.* The RLE paper notes that a standard three-layer deconvolution heatmap head costs 1.4 times the FLOPs of a ResNet-50 backbone, while a regression head costs about 1/20000 of them [13]. Making regression accurate therefore makes cheap heads viable.

### Flip testing and left–right bookkeeping

This note works through the index arithmetic of mirroring heatmaps, the step that flip augmentation and flip testing share.

**Mirroring the image.** For a crop of width $W_{\text{in}}$ pixels, a horizontal flip maps an image column $x$ to $W_{\text{in}} - 1 - x$ (with pixel centres at integer coordinates). Keypoint coordinates are transformed the same way: $x_k \mapsto W_{\text{in}} - 1 - x_k$, with $y_k$ unchanged.

**Swapping the labels.** After mirroring, the person's left side appears where the right side was. Let $\pi$ be the permutation that exchanges each left keypoint with its right twin (left eye with right eye, left shoulder with right shoulder, and so on) and leaves the nose unchanged. A flipped training sample must use the label of keypoint $\pi(k)$ for channel $k$; otherwise the network is trained to call the person's right wrist "left" in half of the images, and it learns to hedge between the two.

**Flip testing.** Given heatmaps $\hat{H}_k$ for the crop and $\hat{H}^{\text{flip}}_k$ for its mirror image, the averaged map for channel $k$ is

$$
\bar{H}_k(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\hat{H}_k(x, y) + \hat{H}^{\text{flip}}_{\pi(k)}(W_{\text{h}} - 1 - x,\; y)\Big),
$$

where $W_{\text{h}}$ is the heatmap width and $(x, y)$ is a heatmap cell. Two operations happen: the mirrored map is flipped back column-wise, and its channels are permuted with $\pi$.

**A subtle offset.** The column mapping $x \mapsto W_{\text{h}} - 1 - x$ is exact only if heatmap cells and image pixels are aligned the same way after flipping. With stride $s$ and image width $W_{\text{in}} = s W_{\text{h}}$, suppose cell $x$ stands for image position $s x$ (its first pixel). Flipping the image sends that position to $W_{\text{in}} - 1 - s x$, but the flipped cell $W_{\text{h}} - 1 - x$ stands for $s(W_{\text{h}} - 1 - x) = W_{\text{in}} - s - s x$. The two differ by $s - 1$ pixels, so averaging the two maps biases every keypoint by about $(s - 1)/2$ pixels. If instead cell $x$ stands for the centre of the pixels it covers, $s x + (s - 1)/2$, the flipped cell stands for $W_{\text{in}} - 1 - \big(s x + (s - 1)/2\big)$, exactly the flipped position, and the bias disappears. Whatever convention a codebase uses, test it with a synthetic heatmap: flip-test a perfect Gaussian and check that the decoded position does not move.

**What goes wrong without the swap.** Exercise 6 shows that forgetting $\pi$ in flip testing averages a left-wrist blob with a right-wrist blob, which produces inversion errors. In augmentation the same mistake is quieter: it does not crash and only lowers accuracy, so it is a classic silent bug.

### Inside OKS: unlabeled people, crowds and the scale s

This note describes the parts of COCO's keypoint evaluation that the OKS formula alone does not show, as specified on the evaluation page [4] and in the official code [27].

**The scale $s$.** It is the square root of the ground-truth person's **segmentation area**, not of the box area and not computed from the keypoints. A partly occluded person has a smaller visible segment, so the same pixel error counts for more. In the code, the term in the exponent is $d_i^2 / (\kappa_i^2 \cdot \text{area}) / 2$, with $\kappa_i = 2\sigma_i$, which is the formula of the main text with $s^2 = \text{area}$.

**Which keypoints count.** Only ground-truth keypoints with $v_i \gt 0$ enter the average, so a predicted keypoint for an unlabeled body part neither helps nor hurts. Each person counts equally in AP, whether they have 3 labeled keypoints or 17; COCO does not weight people by the number of keypoints [4]. A person with only a few labeled keypoints therefore has a noisier OKS.

**People with no labeled keypoints.** Crowd regions and people without any labeled keypoint cannot be scored by distances to keypoints. For them, the code measures how far each predicted keypoint falls *outside* a region around the ground-truth box (the box extended by one box width to the left and to the right, and by one box height above and below), so that predictions inside this region cost nothing. This lenient rule lets such detections match without being counted as false positives, much as ignore regions work for boxes [4], [27].

**Small people and the detection limit.** Small people (segment area below $32^2$) carry no keypoint labels, which is why keypoint AP has only medium and large size ranges. At most 20 predicted poses per image are scored, ranked by their person-level score [4]. The ranking uses the score the model outputs for the whole person, so a method's choice of person score (detector confidence, mean keypoint confidence, or a product of both) can change AP without moving a single keypoint.

**Interpreting thresholds.** Since a keypoint at one annotator standard deviation scores 0.88, an OKS of 0.95 requires almost every keypoint to be well within human disagreement. COCO reports that human performance drops rapidly above OKS 0.95, so the strictest thresholds partly measure annotation noise [4].

### SMPL in more detail

This note explains the terms of the SMPL body model [39] summarized in the main text.

**Template and skinning.** SMPL starts from an artist-made template mesh $\bar{\mathbf{T}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3N}$ with $N = 6890$ vertices in a rest pose, a skeleton of $K = 23$ joints plus a root, and **blend weights** $\mathcal{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$ that say how strongly each joint's rotation moves each vertex. Linear blend skinning $W(\cdot)$ moves every vertex by a weighted average of the rigid transformations of the joints that influence it. On its own, blend skinning produces well-known artifacts, such as collapsing elbows and shoulders when joints bend strongly.

**Shape blend shapes.** $B_S(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{n=1}^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|} \beta_n \mathbf{S}_n$ adds a linear combination of orthonormal principal components $\mathbf{S}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{3N}$ of body-shape variation, learned from registered scans of many people. A few coefficients already capture most of the variation in height, weight and proportions.

**Pose blend shapes.** $B_P(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ adds vertex offsets that depend linearly on the elements of the joints' rotation matrices, computed relative to the rest pose. These offsets correct the blend-skinning artifacts and model pose-dependent deformations. Because the rest-pose rotations are subtracted, $B_P$ is exactly zero in the rest pose [39], which keeps shape and pose effects separate.

**Joints from shape.** People with different shapes have joints in different places, so SMPL computes the rest-pose joint locations from the shaped mesh with a learned linear regressor $\mathcal{J}$: $J(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathcal{J}\big(\bar{\mathbf{T}} + B_S(\boldsymbol{\beta})\big)$ [39]. After posing, the 3D joints (and any keypoints defined as regressed from vertices) follow the mesh, so they always respect the person's bone lengths.

**Why this helps pose estimation.** A network that predicts $(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ can only produce bodies the model can express, and the 72 pose numbers are rotations rather than free 3D positions. Projecting the model's joints into the image with an estimated camera gives 2D keypoints that can be compared with 2D labels, which is the reprojection loss HMR [40] uses to train on images without 3D ground truth.

### Simple Baselines (Xiao et al., 2018)

**The problem.** By 2018, top pose estimators had become complicated: multi-stage hourglasses, cascaded pyramid networks, refinement modules. It was hard to tell which parts mattered. Xiao, Wu and Wei [9] asked how far the simplest reasonable design would go, and offered it as a baseline for evaluating new ideas, for both single-frame pose and pose tracking in video.

**The idea.** Take an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet, remove nothing, and add three deconvolution layers (with batch normalization and ReLU) on top of the last stage; predict $K$ heatmaps with a $1 \times 1$ convolution; train with MSE against Gaussian targets. Use a standard top-down pipeline with a Faster R-CNN person detector, flip testing and the quarter-offset decoder. For tracking, propagate each person's keypoints to the next frame with optical flow to create extra boxes, and link people across frames with a greedy matching based on a flow-based OKS similarity.

**What it showed.** The simple network matched or beat the more complex models of the time on COCO; the paper's best single model reached 73.7 AP on COCO test-dev, and its tracking system was the state of the art on the PoseTrack benchmark at publication.

**How to read its tables.** The ablation (Table 2) changes one factor at a time on COCO val2017: heatmap resolution (three deconvolution layers giving $64 \times 48$ maps beat two giving $32 \times 24$ by 2.5 AP), deconvolution kernel size (a small effect, 0.3 AP between kernel sizes 4 and 2), backbone depth (deeper ResNets help steadily) and input size. The comparison with Hourglass and CPN (Table 3) uses numbers cited from the CPN paper, not re-implemented; the authors say so and note that differences could come from implementation details. Two other settings must match before you compare any top-down numbers: the person detector (the paper's detector has a person AP of 56.4 on COCO val2017, against 55.3 for the detector used by Hourglass and CPN) and the input size ($256 \times 192$ versus $384 \times 288$).

**Limits.** It is a top-down method, so it inherits every detector failure and its cost grows with the number of people. Its heatmaps are still at stride 4, so decoding precision depends on the post-processing described in this chapter. HRNet, by some of the same authors, later showed that keeping high resolution throughout the network does better than recovering it at the end [15].

**Why it matters here.** It is the template of a modern top-down pose estimator, and later systems such as ViTPose explicitly keep its simple deconvolution decoder [16].

### OpenPose: part affinity fields (Cao et al., 2017)

**The problem.** Top-down methods run one network per person, so their runtime grows with the crowd, and they cannot recover from a missed detection. Earlier bottom-up methods grouped keypoints by solving large graph problems that took minutes per image. Cao, Simon, Wei and Sheikh [18] wanted bottom-up grouping that was accurate *and* real-time.

**The idea.** One network with two branches predicts, in several refining stages, confidence maps for the keypoints and **part affinity fields** for the limbs: 2D vector fields that point along each limb. Each stage receives the image features plus both predictions of the previous stage, and every stage is supervised. At test time, candidate limbs are scored by the line integral of the field along them, and a greedy parse solves one bipartite matching per limb of a tree skeleton instead of the full NP-hard problem. The paper argues that the greedy parse works because the fields, computed with a large receptive field, already encode global context.

**What it showed.** The method won the first COCO keypoint challenge in 2016 and clearly improved the state of the art on the MPII multi-person benchmark, with a parsing step whose cost barely depends on the number of people.

**How to read its tables.** The self-comparison on a COCO validation subset (Table 4) is the most instructive: a top-down baseline that uses CPM as the single-person model scores 62.7 AP with ground-truth boxes but 52.7 AP with boxes from an SSD detector, which quantifies how much top-down accuracy depends on the detector; the PAF method alone scores 58.4 AP, and 61.0 AP when each person is refined by CPM. In the challenge table, note the medium-size column: the method is weaker than top-down entries on medium-sized people, which the authors attribute to seeing all people at a single image scale. The runtime figure compares parsing and network time as the number of people grows; the network cost is constant, the parsing cost small.

**Limits.** Accuracy on small and medium people, the greedy parse (limbs are matched locally, so an early mistake propagates), and ambiguous fields where limbs of different people overlap along the same line (Exercise 4). The journal version [36] refines the architecture and adds foot keypoints.

**Why it matters here.** PAFs are the clearest example of learning an explicit *association* signal next to the detections, the same idea that associative embedding implements with tags.

### Guide to the Modern view surveys

This guide expands the survey summary in the Modern view: what each review covers, how it is organized and how to use it.

**Deep Learning-Based Human Pose Estimation: A Survey** (Zheng et al., *ACM Computing Surveys*, 2024) [2] is the broadest of the three. It covers more than 250 papers since 2014, for images and video, 2D and 3D. Its taxonomy follows the input and the inference procedure: 2D single-person (regression versus heatmaps), 2D multi-person (top-down versus bottom-up), and 3D split into single-view single-person (skeleton-only methods, either direct or lifting, versus human mesh recovery), single-view multi-person and multi-view. It also lists datasets and metrics and tabulates reported results. Use it as a map of the literature: find the branch your problem belongs to, then follow its references. Its future directions (domain adaptation, lighter body models, human–scene constraints, temporal metrics) are a good list of open questions.

**Monocular Human Pose Estimation: A Survey of Deep Learning-based Methods** (Chen et al., *Computer Vision and Image Understanding*, 2020) [3] is shorter and earlier. It sorts methods into four tasks (2D and 3D, single- and multi-person) and introduces the three kinds of body model used in the field: skeleton-based, contour-based and volume-based. Its value for a newcomer is its summary of design tricks that proved effective and of the gap between benchmarks and applications (occlusion, crowds, efficiency, data diversity).

**Recovering 3D Human Mesh from Monocular Images: A Survey** (Tian et al., *IEEE TPAMI*, 2023) [5] focuses on mesh recovery with body models such as SMPL. It contrasts optimization-based fitting (data terms plus priors, solved per image) with regression-based networks (trained end to end), reviews training objectives, datasets and metrics, and discusses the remaining difficulties: flexible motion, diverse appearance, complex environments and the shortage of in-the-wild 3D labels. Read it after the 3D section of this chapter if you want to go from skeletons to full bodies.
