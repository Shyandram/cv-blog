## The big picture

A classifier picks one of a fixed list of answers. A face recognition system cannot work that way: the people it will meet next year are not in its training set. Instead it learns a way to **measure how alike two faces are**, and then decides with a threshold.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Imagine a guard at a school gate who has never met most of the visitors. The guard cannot memorize everyone in the world. Instead, each visitor shows a photo card, and the guard asks one question: "Does the face in front of me look enough like the face on this card?" Over many years the guard has learned which differences do not matter (a new haircut, glasses, a shadow) and which do (the shape of the eyes and nose). Face recognition models learn the same skill: turn every face into a short list of numbers, so that two photos of the same person give similar lists and photos of different people give different ones.
</Callout>

This idea, **metric learning**, is much wider than faces. The same recipe (learn an embedding, compare by distance, decide by a threshold) powers person re-identification ([DLCV 07](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-tracking/#person-re-identification)), image retrieval, speaker verification and the contrastive self-supervised methods of [ML 06](/en/learn/ml/representation-learning/#learning-distances-metric-learning). Faces are where the recipe was pushed hardest, because the scale is extreme (millions of identities, error rates of one in a million) and the stakes are high (phone unlocking, border control, police searches).

Face recognition is also a technology with real social costs: errors fall unevenly on different groups, training data was often collected without consent, and some uses are restricted by law. This chapter treats those topics as part of the engineering, factually and with verified sources.

Two surveys frame the field: Wang and Deng's review of deep face recognition [1] and NIST's report on demographic effects in commercial algorithms [2]. We return to both in the Modern view.

## Recognition as comparison: the open-set problem

**Plain version.** Verification asks whether two faces belong to the same person; identification searches stored identities. Both can compare people unseen during training.

### Verification, identification, closed and open set

Let $x$ be a face image and $f_\theta(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ its embedding, produced by a network with parameters $\theta$. A comparison of two images $x_a, x_b$ produces a **similarity score**

$$
s(x_a, x_b) = \cos \angle\big(f_\theta(x_a), f_\theta(x_b)\big) = \frac{f_\theta(x_a)^\top f_\theta(x_b)}{\lVert f_\theta(x_a)\rVert\,\lVert f_\theta(x_b)\rVert} \in [-1, 1],
$$

where $\lVert \cdot \rVert$ is the Euclidean norm. People who have stored reference images are **enrolled**; their stored embeddings (one or several per person) form the **gallery**, and a new image to be recognized is a **probe**. A fixed **threshold** $t$ turns a score into a decision.

| Task | Question | Output | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Verification (1:1) | Is probe $x$ the claimed person $c$? | accept if $s(x, g_c) \ge t$, else reject | phone unlock, passport gate |
| Closed-set identification (1:N) | Which of the $N$ enrolled people is $x$? | $\arg\max_n s(x, g_n)$ | benchmarks; a known, closed group |
| Open-set identification (1:N) | Is $x$ enrolled, and if so, who? | best match if its score $\ge t$, else "not enrolled" | watch lists, photo search |
| Classification ([DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/)) | Which training class is $x$? | one of the classes seen in training | not used for faces at test time |

Here $g_n$ is the gallery embedding of person $n$ and $N$ is the gallery size. Figure 1 draws the three recognition tasks. The important line is the last one: the classes of a face classifier are the *training* identities, and at test time almost every probe belongs to someone else. NIST's description of how deployed systems work makes the same point: a face recognition algorithm has no built-in notion of a particular person; it extracts a feature vector from people it has usually never seen and compares two vectors to produce a score [2].

**Closed versus open set** is a property of the evaluation, not of the network. A closed-set identification test guarantees that every probe has a mate in the gallery, so "pick the best match" is always right in principle. An open-set test includes probes of people who are not enrolled, and then a threshold is unavoidable. Most real searches are open-set. The same distinction appears for generic classifiers as open-set recognition in [PR 10](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/recognition-capstone/#four-tasks-that-are-easy-to-confuse); for faces it is the normal case, not an edge case.

### Why not just train a classifier?

**Plain version.** A classifier has outputs only for training identities. Recognition instead reuses its learned embedding to compare new identities.

A standard classifier trained with softmax and cross-entropy on $C$ identities already learns a useful penultimate layer. DeepFace [6], one of the first deep systems, trained a network on an identity-labeled dataset of more than 4,000 people and showed that the learned representation generalizes to faces in unconstrained photos; the output layer itself is not used for new people. FaceNet [7] instead trained the embedding directly with a triplet loss. The two lines (classification-style training and pair-based training) then merged into the margin-based softmax losses of 2017–2019 that we study below.

What makes a good embedding for open-set use? Two properties, stated by SphereFace as the ideal criterion: the **largest distance within a class** should be smaller than the **smallest distance between classes** [12]. A plain classifier only needs each training sample to fall on the correct side of a decision boundary; it does not care whether the classes are compact. Margins are how we ask for compactness.

## The face recognition pipeline

**Plain version.** Detect the face, locate landmarks, align its crop by rotation and scale, then compute the embedding.

1. **Detection** finds a box around each face.
2. **Landmark localization** finds a few keypoints, usually five: the two eye centers, the nose tip and the two mouth corners.
3. **Alignment** warps the image so that the landmarks match a fixed template, and crops a small, fixed-size image (for example $112 \times 112$ pixels).
4. **Embedding** maps the crop to a vector, usually $L_2$-normalized to unit length.
5. **Comparison** computes cosine similarities against one template (verification) or a gallery (identification) and applies a threshold.

### Detection and landmarks

Face detection is object detection specialized to one class, and the general ideas of [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/) apply. The classical milestone was the Viola–Jones cascade, summarized in DLCV 03's [classical detectors note](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/notes/classical-detectors-in-detail-violajones-hog-and-dpm/). Two deep detectors are common front ends in the face-recognition literature, and both predict the five landmarks together with the box:

- **MTCNN** [4] is a cascade of three convolutional networks that predict face boxes and landmark positions from coarse to fine, trained with an online hard-sample mining strategy.
- **RetinaFace** [5] is a single-stage detector with multi-task heads; its authors manually annotated five landmarks on the WIDER FACE dataset and showed that this extra supervision helps detect hard faces.

These are not historical details: CosFace aligned its faces with MTCNN landmarks [13], and the ArcFace journal version with RetinaFace landmarks [16]. Detector and landmark errors propagate into everything that follows, so a recognition result is always a result *of the whole pipeline*.

### Alignment by a similarity transform

**Plain version.** We want every face to look straight at the camera, at the same size and position. A similarity transform (rotate, scale uniformly, shift) is just flexible enough to do this without distorting the face's shape.

A **similarity transform** maps a point $\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ to

$$
T(\mathbf{p}) = \begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{p} + \begin{bmatrix} t_x \\ t_y \end{bmatrix}, \qquad a = \sigma \cos\phi,\; b = \sigma \sin\phi,
$$

where $\sigma > 0$ is the scale, $\phi$ the rotation angle and $(t_x, t_y)$ the translation. It has four parameters $(a, b, t_x, t_y)$, and the equations are *linear* in them. Given detected landmarks $\mathbf{p}_1, \dots, \mathbf{p}_5$ and template positions $\mathbf{q}_1, \dots, \mathbf{q}_5$, we choose the parameters that minimize $\sum_i \lVert T(\mathbf{p}_i) - \mathbf{q}_i \rVert^2$: ten equations, four unknowns, one least-squares solve. Both CosFace and SphereFace describe their crops as obtained "by similarity transformation" from detected landmarks [12], [13].

```python
import numpy as np

# A toy 5-point template (left eye, right eye, nose tip, left and right mouth
# corners) in a 112 x 112 crop. The coordinates are our own illustration.
template = np.array([[38., 46], [74, 46], [56, 66], [42, 86], [70, 86]])

def fit_similarity(src, dst):
    """Least-squares a, b, tx, ty with dst ~ [[a, -b], [b, a]] @ src + t."""
    n = len(src)
    A = np.zeros((2 * n, 4))
    A[0::2] = np.c_[src[:, 0], -src[:, 1], np.ones(n), np.zeros(n)]
    A[1::2] = np.c_[src[:, 1],  src[:, 0], np.zeros(n), np.ones(n)]
    a, b, tx, ty = np.linalg.lstsq(A, dst.reshape(-1), rcond=None)[0]
    return np.array([[a, -b, tx], [b, a, ty]])

# "Detected" landmarks: the template rotated by 20 degrees, scaled by 1.8,
# shifted into a large photo, plus 1-pixel detector noise.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
ang, sc = np.radians(20), 1.8
R = sc * np.array([[np.cos(ang), -np.sin(ang)], [np.sin(ang), np.cos(ang)]])
detected = template @ R.T + [310, 205] + rng.normal(scale=1.0, size=(5, 2))

M = fit_similarity(detected, template)          # photo -> crop
warped = detected @ M[:, :2].T + M[:, 2]
scale = np.hypot(M[0, 0], M[1, 0])
print(f"scale {scale:.3f} (expect {1 / sc:.3f}), "
      f"rotation {np.degrees(np.arctan2(M[1, 0], M[0, 0])):.1f} deg (expect -20.0)")
print(f"RMS landmark error after alignment: {np.sqrt(((warped - template) ** 2).sum(1).mean()):.2f} px")
```

```text
scale 0.559 (expect 0.556), rotation -19.8 deg (expect -20.0)
RMS landmark error after alignment: 0.56 px
```

The fit recovers the scale and rotation almost exactly, and the remaining error (0.56 pixels) is what the 1-pixel landmark noise leaves after averaging over five points. The matrix `M` is then used to warp the whole image into the crop.

### The embedding and the template

The embedding network is an ordinary image backbone from [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#cnn-architectures-and-the-reasons-behind-them) (a ResNet, or a vision transformer) whose last layer outputs a $d$-dimensional vector instead of class scores. FaceNet used $d = 128$ [7]; the ArcFace experiments use $d = 512$ with $112 \times 112$ crops [16]. When a person is enrolled with several images or a video, the embeddings are combined into one **template**; ArcFace simply uses the feature center (mean) of all images in the template [16]. Since the network's output is normalized before comparison, it is natural to renormalize the mean as well.

## Embeddings on the hypersphere

**Plain version.** If we scale every embedding to length 1, all of them live on the surface of a sphere. Then "how alike are two faces" becomes "how small is the angle between their arrows". Length, which often reflects image quality rather than identity, no longer interferes.

### Why normalize, and why cosine

For unit vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ (that is, $\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert = \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert = 1$),

$$
\lVert \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 - 2\,\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v} = 2 - 2\cos\theta,
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between them. So on the sphere, squared Euclidean distance and cosine similarity are the same ranking, and a threshold on one is a threshold on the other. Normalization removes one degree of freedom that a network can otherwise misuse: with an unnormalized softmax, a network can lower its loss on easy samples simply by making their features longer, without making the classes more compact. NormFace analyzed this and showed that training directly on normalized features needs an extra scale factor to converge [11].

High dimension adds a useful fact: **random directions are almost orthogonal**. If $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are independent and uniformly distributed on the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$, then $\mathbb{E}[\cos\theta] = 0$ and the standard deviation of $\cos\theta$ is $1/\sqrt{d}$. Impostor scores therefore cluster tightly around 0, and the threshold only has to beat the tail of that narrow distribution.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
e = rng.normal(size=(2000, 512))
e /= np.linalg.norm(e, axis=1, keepdims=True)       # 2,000 random unit vectors
a, b = e[0], e[1]
cos = a @ b
print(f"cos = {cos:.4f}   |a-b|^2 = {np.sum((a - b) ** 2):.4f}   2-2cos = {2 - 2 * cos:.4f}")

S = e @ e.T
off = S[np.triu_indices(len(e), 1)]                 # all distinct pairs
print(f"random pairs: mean cos {off.mean():.4f}, std {off.std():.4f}, "
      f"1/sqrt(512) = {1 / np.sqrt(512):.4f}, max {off.max():.3f}")
```

```text
cos = 0.0472   |a-b|^2 = 1.9057   2-2cos = 1.9057
random pairs: mean cos -0.0000, std 0.0442, 1/sqrt(512) = 0.0442, max 0.214
```

Among almost two million random pairs in 512 dimensions, the largest cosine is only 0.214. A trained network does not produce random embeddings, but the picture holds: different people sit at angles near $90^\circ$, and the job of training is to pull each person's images into a small cap around one direction.

## Pair-based losses: contrastive and triplet

**Plain version.** The most direct way to train an embedding is to show the network pairs or triples of images and say "these two should be close, those two should be far". The difficulty is that almost all such examples are already easy, so the network learns little from them unless we search for the hard ones.

### Contrastive loss

The contrastive loss, introduced for face verification with a Siamese network by Chopra, Hadsell and LeCun [8] and developed by Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun [9], looks at one pair at a time:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}(x_a, x_b) = y\, D^2 + (1 - y)\,\big[\, m - D \,\big]_+^2, \qquad D = \lVert f_\theta(x_a) - f_\theta(x_b) \rVert,
$$

where $y = 1$ if the pair shows the same person and $y = 0$ otherwise, $m > 0$ is a margin, and $[z]_+ = \max(z, 0)$. Same-person pairs are pulled together; different-person pairs are pushed apart only until they are $m$ apart. Up to constant factors and the label convention, this squared-hinge form is the one in [9]; [8] introduced the underlying idea of training a Siamese network so that a learned distance separates same-person from different-person pairs.

### Triplet loss and hard-negative mining

The **triplet loss** of FaceNet [7] compares distances instead of thresholding them. Its definition, and its use in re-identification, are in [DLCV 07](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-tracking/#problem-formulation) and [ML 06](/en/learn/ml/representation-learning/#learning-distances-metric-learning); in FaceNet's form, with squared distances on unit-length embeddings,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{tri}}(a, p, n) = \big[\, \lVert f(a) - f(p) \rVert^2 - \lVert f(a) - f(n) \rVert^2 + \alpha \,\big]_+ ,
$$

where $a$ is an anchor image, $p$ a positive (same person), $n$ a negative (another person), $f = f_\theta$ the embedding, and $\alpha > 0$ the margin. What this chapter adds is the **mining** question. For an anchor–positive pair with squared distance $d_{ap}$, a negative at squared distance $d_{an}$ is

- **easy** if $d_{an} \ge d_{ap} + \alpha$ (zero loss, zero gradient),
- **semi-hard** if $d_{ap} \le d_{an} \lt d_{ap} + \alpha$ (farther than the positive, but inside the margin; this is FaceNet's definition [7]),
- **hard** if $d_{an} \lt d_{ap}$ (closer than the positive).

FaceNet selected semi-hard negatives online within large mini-batches, because the very hardest negatives early in training led to bad local minima [7]. A later and widely used alternative, **batch-hard** mining, takes for every anchor in a batch of $P$ identities $\times$ $K$ images the *farthest* positive and the *closest* negative [10].

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
P, Kimg, alpha = 8, 4, 0.2                  # 8 people x 4 images, margin
centres = rng.normal(size=(P, 64))
y = np.repeat(np.arange(P), Kimg)
e = centres[y] + 1.5 * rng.normal(size=(len(y), 64))
e /= np.linalg.norm(e, axis=1, keepdims=True)
d2 = ((e[:, None] - e[None]) ** 2).sum(-1)  # squared distances

easy = semi = hard = 0
for a in range(len(y)):
    for p in np.flatnonzero((y == y[a]) & (np.arange(len(y)) != a)):
        dn = d2[a, y != y[a]]               # distances to all negatives
        hard += (dn < d2[a, p]).sum()
        semi += ((dn >= d2[a, p]) & (dn < d2[a, p] + alpha)).sum()
        easy += (dn >= d2[a, p] + alpha).sum()
tot = easy + semi + hard
print(f"{tot} triplets: easy {easy / tot:.1%}, semi-hard {semi / tot:.1%}, hard {hard / tot:.1%}")

# batch-hard: for each anchor, farthest positive and closest negative
same = y[:, None] == y[None]
hp = np.where(same, d2, -np.inf).max(1)
hn = np.where(~same, d2, np.inf).min(1)
loss = np.maximum(hp - hn + alpha, 0)
print(f"batch-hard loss {loss.mean():.3f}; anchors with non-zero loss: {(loss > 0).sum()} of {len(y)}")
```

```text
2688 triplets: easy 92.8%, semi-hard 5.4%, hard 1.9%
batch-hard loss 0.213; anchors with non-zero loss: 28 of 32
```

In this synthetic batch, 92.8% of all 2,688 valid triplets are easy and contribute nothing. Averaging over all triplets would dilute the few useful ones; batch-hard mining finds a non-zero loss for 28 of the 32 anchors. This is the core weakness of pair-based training at face scale: the number of possible triplets grows with the cube of the dataset size, almost all of them are easy, and the result depends on the sampling strategy and the batch composition.

## Margin-based softmax losses

**Plain version.** Instead of comparing images with each other, give every training identity a learned "anchor direction" on the sphere, and train each image to point at its own identity's direction *by a clear margin*: not just closer than to the other directions, but closer by a fixed angle. One loss term per image then compares it with all identities at once.

### From softmax to normalized softmax

Start from the softmax cross-entropy of [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/). For a feature $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of image $i$ with identity label $y_i \in \{1, \dots, C\}$, a last layer with weight columns $W_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and biases $b_j$ gives

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{softmax}} = -\log \frac{e^{W_{y_i}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b_{y_i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} e^{W_j^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b_j}}.
$$

Set $b_j = 0$ and write the logit as $W_j^\top \mathbf{x}_i = \lVert W_j\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{x}_i\rVert\cos\theta_j$, where $\theta_j$ is the angle between $\mathbf{x}_i$ and $W_j$. Now normalize both: $\lVert W_j \rVert = 1$, and $\lVert \mathbf{x}_i \rVert$ fixed to a constant $s$. Then the logits are $s\cos\theta_j$ and the prediction depends on angles only [11], [13], [16]:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{norm}} = -\log \frac{e^{s\cos\theta_{y_i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} e^{s\cos\theta_j}}.
$$

The **scale** $s > 0$ is necessary because cosines lie in $[-1, 1]$: without it, even a perfect angular arrangement would leave the softmax far from confident, so the loss could not become small. NormFace analyzed this failure to converge [11], and CosFace derived a lower bound for $s$ that grows with the number of classes [13]; both CosFace and ArcFace use $s = 64$ [13], [16].

*Optional: [How large must the scale s be?](#how-large-must-the-scale-s-be) (in the Supplementary notes). CosFace's lower bound with every symbol defined, a worked example, and what goes wrong when $s$ is too small or too large.*

Each column $W_j$ is now a unit vector: a **class center**, or **proxy**, for identity $j$ on the sphere. This is the key structural change compared with pair-based losses. Every image is compared with all $C$ class centers in every step, so there is nothing to mine, and the comparison is global rather than limited to one batch. The class centers exist only for training; at test time the last layer is discarded and only the embedding network is kept [16].

### Three ways to add a margin

The normalized softmax is satisfied when $\theta_{y_i} \lt \theta_j$ for all $j \ne y_i$: the feature is closer in angle to its own center than to any other. That puts the decision boundary exactly halfway between two centers, so samples near the boundary are correct but barely. A **margin** makes the target logit harder to achieve by modifying it from $s\cos\theta_{y_i}$ to $s\,\psi(\theta_{y_i})$ with $\psi(\theta) \lt \cos\theta$, while the other logits stay $s\cos\theta_j$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{margin}} = -\log \frac{e^{s\,\psi(\theta_{y_i})}}{e^{s\,\psi(\theta_{y_i})} + \sum_{j \ne y_i} e^{s\cos\theta_j}}.
$$

The three classic losses differ only in $\psi$:

| Loss | Target logit $\psi(\theta)$ | Margin type | Value in the paper |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SphereFace (A-Softmax) [12] | $\cos(m\theta)$, extended to stay monotone (below) | multiplicative angular | $m = 4$ |
| CosFace (LMCL) [13]; also AM-Softmax [14] | $\cos\theta - m$ | additive cosine | $m = 0.35$, $s = 64$ |
| ArcFace [15], [16] | $\cos(\theta + m)$ | additive angular | $m = 0.5$, $s = 64$ |

Here $m$ is the margin (an integer for SphereFace, a cosine offset for CosFace and an angle in radians for ArcFace), and $\theta = \theta_{y_i}$ is the angle to the sample's own class center; the last column gives the values reported in the papers. Because $\cos(m\theta)$ is not monotone on $[0, \pi]$, SphereFace uses $\psi(\theta) = (-1)^k \cos(m\theta) - 2k$ for $\theta \in [k\pi/m, (k+1)\pi/m]$, $k \in \{0, \dots, m-1\}$ [12]. The original SphereFace normalized only the weights and needed help from an ordinary softmax early in training [16]. AM-Softmax [14], published at about the same time as CosFace, uses the same additive cosine margin.

```python
import numpy as np

def sphereface_psi(theta, m=4):
    """SphereFace's monotone extension of cos(m*theta) to [0, pi]."""
    k = np.floor(theta * m / np.pi)
    return (-1) ** k * np.cos(m * theta) - 2 * k

print(" angle  softmax  SphereFace  CosFace  ArcFace")
for deg in [10, 30, 45, 60, 90]:
    t = np.radians(deg)
    print(f"{deg:5d}   {np.cos(t):6.3f}   {sphereface_psi(t):8.3f}   "
          f"{np.cos(t) - 0.35:6.3f}   {np.cos(t + 0.5):6.3f}")
```

The table shows the target logit (divided by $s$) at five angles. Every margin lowers the logit of the correct class, so the loss stays large until the angle is small. The penalties grow differently: CosFace subtracts the same 0.35 at every angle; ArcFace's penalty is small near $0^\circ$ (0.204 at $10^\circ$) and larger at wide angles; SphereFace with $m = 4$ is very strict at wide angles.

### The geometry on the circle

**Plain version.** Picture two class centers on a circle. Without a margin, the circle is split exactly halfway between them. With a margin, each class claims less than half, and a strip in between belongs to nobody: a sample there still has a loss. Training pushes samples out of that strip, toward their own center.

For two classes with centers $W_1, W_2$ an angle $\varphi$ apart, and a feature on the arc between them, a sample of class 1 is "satisfied" when $\psi(\theta_1) \gt \cos\theta_2$ with $\theta_2 = \varphi - \theta_1$. Solving gives the class-1 boundary:

- normalized softmax: $\theta_1 \lt \varphi / 2$ (no gap);
- SphereFace: $m\,\theta_1 \lt \theta_2$, so $\theta_1 \lt \varphi/(m+1)$; the gap between the two classes' boundaries is $\varphi(m-1)/(m+1)$, which **grows with** $\varphi$;
- CosFace: $\cos\theta_1 - m \gt \cos(\varphi - \theta_1)$; the gap is a fixed amount in *cosine*, so its size in angle depends on $\varphi$;
- ArcFace: $\theta_1 + m \lt \theta_2$, so $\theta_1 \lt (\varphi - m)/2$; the gap is exactly $m$ radians for every $\varphi$.

ArcFace's authors call this a margin equal to the **geodesic distance** on the hypersphere, which is where the name comes from [15], [16]. Figure 2 draws all four cases for $\varphi = 80^\circ$; SphereFace is shown with $m = 2$ so that its zone fits on the same picture.

<Figure src={figMargin} alt="Four unit circles, each with two class-center arrows W1 at 0 degrees and W2 at 80 degrees. The circle is colored blue where class 1's loss is satisfied, orange where class 2's is satisfied and gray where neither is. Normalized softmax has no gray zone; SphereFace with m = 2 has a 27-degree gray zone between the centers and a large one on the far side; CosFace with m = 0.35 has 31 degrees and ArcFace with m = 0.5 has 29 degrees." caption="Figure 2 — Where each loss is satisfied for two classes whose centers are 80° apart. Gray arcs are the margin zones, where a sample of either class still has a loss. The gap between the centers is 0° without a margin, 27° for SphereFace (m = 2), 31° for CosFace (m = 0.35) and 29° (= 0.5 rad) for ArcFace (m = 0.5)." />

One practical detail follows from the formula: $\cos(\theta + m)$ decreases only while $\theta + m \le \pi$. For a sample whose angle to its own center is larger than $\pi - m$ (a badly misclassified or mislabeled sample), the ArcFace target logit would start to *increase* again as the sample moves away. An implementation has to handle this range explicitly, for example by switching to a monotone penalty there. In our toy code below, all angles stay well below that range.

### A toy experiment: softmax versus ArcFace

**Plain version.** We train the same small network on synthetic "faces" twice: once with the ordinary softmax and once with ArcFace. Then we look at how tight the training clusters are and, more importantly, how well the network separates *people it never saw*.

The data mimic the face problem. Each synthetic identity is a random point in a 4-dimensional "identity" subspace of a 16-dimensional input; every image adds a large random "nuisance" component (standing in for pose and lighting) in the other 12 dimensions and small noise everywhere. The network is one hidden layer of 64 ReLU units followed by a linear map to a $d = 4$ embedding. We implement the forward pass and the gradients by hand, including the chain rule through the normalization and through $\cos(\theta + m)$, and use plain gradient descent. We train on 100 identities and test verification on 50 identities that never appear in training. TAR at FAR = 1% (defined in the next section) is the fraction of same-person test pairs accepted when the threshold lets through 1% of different-person pairs.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
D, K = 16, 4                                      # input dim, identity dims
U = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(D, D)))[0]      # random orthonormal basis

def sample(ids, n_per):
    """Identity signal in 4 directions, large 'pose/light' nuisance in 12."""
    y = np.repeat(np.arange(len(ids)), n_per)
    nuis = 1.5 * rng.normal(size=(len(y), D - K))
    x = ids[y] @ U[:, :K].T + nuis @ U[:, K:].T + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=(len(y), D))
    return x, y

unit = lambda v, ax: v / np.linalg.norm(v, axis=ax, keepdims=True)

def train(x, y, C, d=4, margin=None, s=16.0, steps=800, lr=0.2, H=64):
    """One-hidden-layer embedding net + class centres W, plain gradient descent.
    margin=None: ordinary softmax on raw features; otherwise ArcFace logits."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(1)
    W1 = r.normal(size=(D, H)) / 4; b1 = np.zeros(H)
    W2 = r.normal(size=(H, d)) / 8; W = r.normal(size=(d, C))
    Y = np.eye(C)[y]
    for _ in range(steps):
        h = np.maximum(x @ W1 + b1, 0); z = h @ W2          # embeddings
        if margin is None:
            L = z @ W
        else:
            f, w = unit(z, 1), unit(W, 0)
            th = np.arccos(np.clip(f @ w, -1 + 1e-7, 1 - 1e-7))   # angles to centres
            L = s * np.where(Y > 0, np.cos(th + margin), np.cos(th))
        p = np.exp(L - L.max(1, keepdims=True)); p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        g = (p - Y) / len(y)                                  # dLoss/dlogits
        if margin is None:
            gz, gW = g @ W.T, z.T @ g
        else:                                                 # chain rule through cos(theta+m)
            gc = g * s * np.where(Y > 0, np.sin(th + margin) / np.sin(th), 1.0)
            gf, gw = gc @ w.T, f.T @ gc
            gz = (gf - (gf * f).sum(1, keepdims=True) * f) / np.linalg.norm(z, axis=1, keepdims=True)
            gW = (gw - (gw * w).sum(0, keepdims=True) * w) / np.linalg.norm(W, axis=0, keepdims=True)
        gh = (gz @ W2.T) * (h > 0)
        W1 -= lr * x.T @ gh; b1 -= lr * gh.sum(0); W2 -= lr * h.T @ gz; W -= lr * gW
    return lambda xx: unit(np.maximum(xx @ W1 + b1, 0) @ W2, 1)

def intra_angle(e, y):            # mean angle (degrees) to own class mean direction
    cen = unit(np.stack([e[y == j].mean(0) for j in np.unique(y)]), 1)
    return np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip((e * cen[y]).sum(1), -1, 1))).mean()

def tar_at_far(e, y, far=0.01):   # verification on all pairs of unseen people
    i, j = np.triu_indices(len(y), 1)
    sc, same = (e[i] * e[j]).sum(1), y[i] == y[j]
    thr = np.quantile(sc[~same], 1 - far)
    return (sc[same] > thr).mean()

ids = rng.normal(size=(150, K))
xtr, ytr = sample(ids[:100], 10)          # 100 training identities
xte, yte = sample(ids[100:], 10)          # 50 identities never seen in training
for name, m in [("softmax", None), ("norm-softmax", 0.0), ("ArcFace m=0.5", 0.5)]:
    emb = train(xtr, ytr, 100, margin=m)
    print(f"{name:14s} train intra {intra_angle(emb(xtr), ytr):5.1f} deg | "
          f"unseen intra {intra_angle(emb(xte), yte):5.1f} deg | "
          f"unseen TAR@FAR=1% {tar_at_far(emb(xte), yte):.3f}")
```

```text
softmax        train intra  27.1 deg | unseen intra  43.9 deg | unseen TAR@FAR=1% 0.082
norm-softmax   train intra  16.1 deg | unseen intra  30.1 deg | unseen TAR@FAR=1% 0.098
ArcFace m=0.5  train intra  11.6 deg | unseen intra  24.4 deg | unseen TAR@FAR=1% 0.248
```

The script runs in about ten seconds on a CPU. In this toy run the mean angle of a training image to its class center drops from 27.1° (softmax) to 16.1° (normalization alone) to 11.6° (ArcFace), and the clusters of *unseen* people tighten too (43.9° to 24.4°). The verification rate on unseen people at FAR = 1% roughly triples, from 0.082 to 0.248. Two cautions. First, the absolute numbers are low because a 4-dimensional embedding of noisy data is a hard problem; only the comparison matters. Second, these are single runs; with two other random seeds the ordering softmax &lt; normalized softmax &lt; ArcFace stayed the same in our runs, but the exact values changed.

Figure 3 shows the same effect in a 2-D embedding with six identities, where it can be seen directly: softmax spreads each class along a ray (it can lower its loss by lengthening features), while ArcFace packs each class into a short arc.

Margins tighten clusters of the training identities directly. Whether that transfers to new people depends on how many and how varied the training identities are; with very few training identities, a network can separate them perfectly and still learn nothing general. That is one reason why face recognition training sets contain hundreds of thousands to millions of identities (see the datasets section).

### Class centers, sub-centers and adaptive margins

**Plain version.** Once each identity has a center, several refinements follow naturally. They all keep the margin softmax and change one thing about the centers or the margin.

Center loss [17], which predates the angular losses, pulls each feature toward a learned center of its class. Sub-center ArcFace [18], [16] gives each identity several centers, so that noisy or mislabeled images can gather away from the clean ones. MagFace [19] and AdaFace [20] tie the margin to image quality, using the feature magnitude, so that very poor images do not dominate training. Partial FC [21] cuts the cost of millions of centers by updating only a sampled subset of them in each step. The one thing to remember: the class centers are a training device, and these methods tune how strictly and how cheaply images are pulled toward them.

## Evaluating verification

**Plain version.** Compare many pairs of photos of the same person (**genuine** pairs) and many pairs of different people (**impostor** pairs). A good system gives genuine pairs high scores and impostor pairs low scores. Any threshold then trades two errors: letting impostors in, and turning genuine users away.

### Genuine and impostor scores, FAR and TAR

For a threshold $t$ on the similarity score $s$,

$$
\text{FAR}(t) = P(s \ge t \mid \text{impostor}), \qquad \text{FRR}(t) = P(s \lt t \mid \text{genuine}), \qquad \text{TAR}(t) = 1 - \text{FRR}(t).
$$

FAR is the **false accept rate**, FRR the **false reject rate** and TAR the **true accept rate**. NIST's evaluations use the equivalent names **false match rate** (FMR) and **false non-match rate** (FNMR) for one-to-one comparisons [2], [3]. These are exactly the false-positive rate and the false-negative rate of [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#the-binary-case-tp-fp-fn-tn); the "positive" class is "same person".

The two distributions are very unequal in size. With $P$ people and $k$ images each, there are $P\binom{k}{2}$ genuine pairs but about $\binom{Pk}{2} - P\binom{k}{2}$ impostor pairs, which grows with the square of $P$. Because impostor pairs are so many, operating points are stated as **TAR at a fixed FAR**: "TAR@FAR = $10^{-4}$" is the fraction of genuine pairs accepted when the threshold is set so that one impostor pair in ten thousand is accepted. Benchmarks such as IJB-B and IJB-C report TAR@FAR = $10^{-4}$ [16], and NIST's ongoing one-to-one evaluation ranks algorithms by FNMR at FMR = $10^{-6}$ on one of its datasets [3].

A dashed line at 0.23 marks the threshold for FAR 1e-2 and a solid line at 0.36 the threshold for FAR 1e-4. The right panel shows the same data on a log density axis, where the impostor tail reaches beyond 0.3." caption="Figure 4 — Synthetic genuine (blue) and impostor (orange) score distributions. (a) Linear scale. (b) Log scale: the threshold for a small FAR is set by the far tail of the impostor distribution, which is invisible on a linear axis." />

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import roc_curve

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
genuine = np.clip(rng.normal(0.55, 0.13, 5_000), -1, 1)       # same-person cosines
impostor = np.clip(rng.normal(0.02, 0.09, 500_000), -1, 1)    # different-person cosines
y = np.r_[np.ones(genuine.size), np.zeros(impostor.size)]
fpr, tpr, thr = roc_curve(y, np.r_[genuine, impostor])

for far in [1e-2, 1e-3, 1e-4]:
    i = np.searchsorted(fpr, far, side="right") - 1          # last point with FPR <= far
    print(f"FAR <= {far:.0e}: threshold {thr[i]:.3f}, TAR {tpr[i]:.3f}, "
          f"false accepts {int(round(fpr[i] * impostor.size))}")
i = np.argmin(np.abs(fpr - (1 - tpr)))
print(f"EER ~ {(fpr[i] + 1 - tpr[i]) / 2:.4f} at threshold {thr[i]:.3f}")
```

```text
FAR <= 1e-02: threshold 0.231, TAR 0.994, false accepts 4825
FAR <= 1e-03: threshold 0.300, TAR 0.975, false accepts 495
FAR <= 1e-04: threshold 0.356, TAR 0.936, false accepts 49
EER ~ 0.0072 at threshold 0.241
```

The **equal error rate** (EER) is the error at the threshold where FAR equals FRR; it is a convenient single number but describes an operating point that a secure system rarely uses. Notice how the threshold moves: lowering FAR from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-4}$ raises the threshold from 0.231 to 0.356 and costs 5.8 points of TAR. Notice also how few errors the last line is based on: 49 false accepts. A FAR of $10^{-6}$ cannot be measured at all with 500,000 impostor pairs; you need millions of them, and they must be reasonably independent.

### ROC and DET curves

The ROC curve of [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#threshold-free-evaluation-roc-and-precisionrecall-curves) plots TAR against FAR. For face verification it is always drawn with a **logarithmic FAR axis**, because the interesting region is FAR between $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-2}$, which a linear axis squeezes into the left edge. The **detection error trade-off (DET) curve** [22] plots both error rates, FRR against FAR, with both axes transformed by the inverse of the standard normal cumulative distribution function $\Phi^{-1}$ (the "normal deviate" scale). If genuine and impostor scores are both Gaussian, the DET curve is a straight line, and lower-left is better. Martin et al. introduced it for speaker recognition and explained why it tends to be close to linear [22]; it is now standard for biometric systems.

Right: DET curves of the same systems on normal-deviate axes, roughly straight lines; system A lies below and to the left of system B, and a dotted diagonal marks where the two error rates are equal." caption="Figure 5 — (a) ROC with a log FAR axis; dots mark TAR at FAR = 10⁻³. ### Choosing the threshold

The threshold is part of the system, not of the network, and it must be chosen on data that represent deployment. Three rules follow from the formulas above. (1) Fix the FAR you can tolerate from the application (a phone unlock and a forensic search have very different costs), then choose $t$ as the corresponding quantile of impostor scores on a **held-out** set; tuning $t$ on the test set inflates results. (2) Report TAR and FAR *together* at that threshold, never one alone. (3) Check that the impostor scores of your deployment look like those of your calibration set: a new camera, a new population or a new image quality changes the impostor distribution and therefore the real FAR at a fixed $t$. We return to the last point in the fairness section.

## Evaluating identification

**Plain version.** In a search, the probe is compared with every enrolled person. In a closed-set test we ask: was the right person ranked first (or in the top $k$)? In an open-set test we also ask: when the person is not enrolled at all, did the system stay silent?

### Closed set: rank-1 and the CMC curve

For closed-set identification, sort the gallery by similarity to the probe. **Rank-1 accuracy** is the fraction of probes whose mate is ranked first, and the **cumulative match characteristic** CMC($k$) is the fraction whose mate is within the top $k$. The formula and code are in [DLCV 07](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-tracking/#metrics-cmc-rank-kkk-and-map); a face gallery usually holds one template per person, so mAP is rarely used.

The crucial effect is **gallery size**. Each extra enrolled person is one more chance for an impostor to outscore the true mate. MegaFace was built to measure this: it added up to one million distractor photos to the gallery and reported identification and verification as the number of distractors grew from 10 to 1M [24].

### Open set: FNIR and FPIR

In an open-set search, the system returns a candidate only if its score reaches a threshold $t$. Two error rates describe it [2]:

$$
\text{FNIR}(t) = \frac{\#\{\text{mated searches whose mate is not returned with score} \ge t\}}{\#\{\text{mated searches}\}}, \qquad
\text{FPIR}(t) = \frac{\#\{\text{non-mated searches returning any candidate with score} \ge t\}}{\#\{\text{non-mated searches}\}}.
$$

A **mated** search is one whose probe person is enrolled; a **non-mated** search is one whose probe person is not. FNIR (false negative identification rate) and FPIR (false positive identification rate) play the roles of FRR and FAR for one-to-many search; in this simple version a candidate is returned only at rank 1. If every one of the $N$ gallery comparisons of a non-mated probe were an independent trial with false match rate FMR($t$), then

$$
\text{FPIR}(t) \approx 1 - \big(1 - \text{FMR}(t)\big)^N \approx N \cdot \text{FMR}(t) \quad \text{for small } N\cdot\text{FMR}(t),
$$

so at a fixed threshold FPIR grows roughly in proportion to the gallery size. This is why one-to-many search needs far lower one-to-one false match rates than verification.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
dim, noise = 128, 1.1
unit = lambda v: v / np.linalg.norm(v, axis=-1, keepdims=True)

def identify(n_gallery, n_probe=300, thr=0.36):
    people = rng.normal(size=(n_gallery + n_probe, dim))
    gallery = unit(people[:n_gallery] + noise * rng.normal(size=(n_gallery, dim)))
    mated = rng.choice(n_gallery, n_probe, replace=False)             # enrolled people
    p_in = unit(people[mated] + noise * rng.normal(size=(n_probe, dim)))
    p_out = unit(people[n_gallery:] + noise * rng.normal(size=(n_probe, dim)))  # not enrolled
    S_in, S_out = p_in @ gallery.T, p_out @ gallery.T
    rank1 = (S_in.argmax(1) == mated).mean()
    # open set: a search "returns" the top candidate only if its score >= thr
    fnir = 1 - ((S_in.argmax(1) == mated) & (S_in.max(1) >= thr)).mean()
    fpir = (S_out.max(1) >= thr).mean()
    return rank1, fnir, fpir

for n in [300, 3_000, 30_000]:
    r1, fnir, fpir = identify(n)
    print(f"gallery {n:6d}: rank-1 {r1:.3f} | threshold 0.36: FNIR {fnir:.3f}, FPIR {fpir:.3f}")
```

```text
gallery    300: rank-1 0.990 | threshold 0.36: FNIR 0.090, FPIR 0.010
gallery   3000: rank-1 0.953 | threshold 0.36: FNIR 0.113, FPIR 0.043
gallery  30000: rank-1 0.903 | threshold 0.36: FNIR 0.117, FPIR 0.330
```

With the same synthetic embedding quality, growing the gallery from 300 to 30,000 people lowers rank-1 from 0.990 to 0.903, and at a fixed threshold the share of non-enrolled probes that trigger a false hit rises from 1% to 33%. Figure 6 shows the CMC curves and the FPIR-versus-threshold curves of a similar run. An identification result is meaningless without the gallery size, and an open-set system must be re-thresholded when the gallery grows.

## Datasets and benchmarks

**Plain version.** Training sets for faces are huge lists of web photos grouped by person; test sets define pairs or searches with a fixed protocol. Several of the best-known sets were collected from the web without the consent of the people in them, and some have been withdrawn.

| Dataset | Role | What it contains (as described by its source) | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LFW [23] | verification test | unconstrained news photos; about 13K photos of 5K people [24] | saturated; a disclaimer added in 2019 warns against concluding commercial suitability from it [30] |
| IJB-B [27] | verification, identification, clustering | 1,845 subjects; 21,798 still images and 55,026 frames from 7,011 videos, with human-labeled boxes and eye/nose locations | standard test set; media under a Creative Commons license [27] |
| IJB-C [28] | as IJB-B, larger | 3,531 subjects; 31.3K still images and 117.5K frames from 11,779 videos [16] | standard test set [16] |
| MegaFace [24], [25] | identification at scale | 1M distractor photos of more than 690K individuals; a later training set of 4.7M photos of 672,057 identities | **decommissioned**; data no longer distributed [25] |
| MS-Celeb-1M [26] | training | web images for a benchmark of recognizing one million celebrities linked to a knowledge base | **taken down in April 2019** [30] |
| WebFace260M / WebFace42M [29] | training | 4M identities / 260M faces (noisy) and a cleaned 2M identities / 42M faces | described by its authors as the largest public training set [29] |
| DigiFace-1M [31] | training | synthetic faces rendered with a computer-graphics pipeline | synthetic, not photographs of real people |

**LFW** (Labeled Faces in the Wild) [23] defined unconstrained face verification and its standard protocols. Its accuracy is now saturated: state-of-the-art models exceed 99.8% on it [31], so it no longer separates good systems from very good ones. **IJB-B** and **IJB-C** (IARPA Janus Benchmarks) [27], [28] replaced it as the standard academic test: they include video frames, large pose variation and protocols at operationally relevant points such as TAR@FAR = $10^{-4}$, and they evaluate *templates* (sets of images) rather than single images. **MegaFace** [24] made gallery size the central question. **NIST's evaluations** [2], [3] use large operational datasets that are not public and test submitted algorithms directly, so they are the reference for deployment-scale error rates.

**Withdrawn datasets.** MS-Celeb-1M was taken down by Microsoft in April 2019; Peng, Mathur and Narayanan note that the removal followed critical reports, that many of the "celebrities" were fairly ordinary people, that the images had been aggregated without consent, and that Microsoft gave as its reason that the research challenge was over [30]. MegaFace's own site states that the challenge has concluded and that the data are no longer distributed [25]. The same study found that retracted data often keep circulating through copies, cleaned derivatives and pretrained models, and that licenses did little to restrict such use [30]; the DukeMTMC case in [DLCV 07](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-tracking/#datasets) is similar. In practice: do not use withdrawn datasets or their derivatives, record the provenance and license of every training set, and expect reviewers to ask.

**Synthetic training data** is one response. DigiFace-1M renders faces with a computer-graphics pipeline and motivates this with bias, label noise and the lack of consent in web-crawled data [31]; DCFace generates identities and styles with a diffusion model [32]. Boutros et al. survey this direction and its open challenges [33].

## Fairness and demographic differentials

**Plain version.** A system can have the same overall accuracy as another and still make many more errors for some groups of people than for others. Because a deployed system usually uses *one* threshold for everybody, small differences in score distributions between groups can turn into large differences in error rates.

### What the measurements show

The most extensive public measurement is NIST's 2019 report on demographic effects [2]. It processed 18.27 million images of 8.49 million people from four U.S. government datasets through 189 mostly commercial algorithms from 99 developers. Its main findings, in the report's own framing:

- **False positives** differed far more than false negatives: across demographic groups, false positive rates often varied by factors of 10 to beyond 100, while false negatives were more algorithm-specific and often varied by factors below 3.
- The pattern **depended on the algorithm and the data**. For example, with high-quality application photos the highest false positive rates were in West and East African and East Asian people for many algorithms, but with a number of algorithms developed in China the East Asian effect was reversed. False positives were also higher for women than for men and elevated for the elderly and children.
- **Image quality** mattered for false negatives: results differed between well-controlled mugshots and lower-quality border-crossing photos.
- Some highly accurate one-to-many algorithms showed **undetectable** false positive differentials, so differentials are not inevitable.
- The report recommends that every demographic claim state which error (false positive or false negative) is meant, at a fixed threshold, for each group, because many earlier reports did not [2].

Academic studies add complementary evidence. Wang et al. built the Racial Faces in-the-Wild (RFW) test set and measured racial bias in commercial APIs and research algorithms [34]. The widely cited Gender Shades study [35] measured error rates of up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women versus at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men; note that it evaluated commercial **gender classification**, not face recognition, a distinction NIST explicitly asks reporters to make [2]. Drozdowski et al. survey bias estimation and mitigation across biometrics [36].

### A toy: one threshold, two groups

The mechanism is simple enough to simulate. Assume two groups A and B with identical genuine score distributions, but B's impostor scores are slightly higher: different members of B look a little more alike to the network. In this toy we simply assume the shift; in real systems its causes have to be investigated. Set one global threshold for FMR = $10^{-3}$ on the pooled impostors.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_imp, n_gen = 400_000, 4_000
# Same genuine distribution; group B's impostor scores sit slightly higher.
imp = {"A": rng.normal(0.00, 0.09, n_imp), "B": rng.normal(0.06, 0.09, n_imp)}
gen = {"A": rng.normal(0.55, 0.13, n_gen), "B": rng.normal(0.55, 0.13, n_gen)}

pooled = np.r_[imp["A"], imp["B"]]
t_global = np.quantile(pooled, 1 - 1e-3)          # FMR = 0.1% on the pooled data
print(f"global threshold {t_global:.3f}")
for g in "AB":
    print(f"  group {g}: FMR {(imp[g] >= t_global).mean():.5f}, "
          f"FNMR {(gen[g] < t_global).mean():.4f}")
for g in "AB":
    t_g = np.quantile(imp[g], 1 - 1e-3)             # per-group threshold, FMR 0.1% each
    print(f"group {g} own threshold {t_g:.3f}: FNMR {(gen[g] < t_g).mean():.4f}")
```

```text
global threshold 0.322
  group A: FMR 0.00018, FNMR 0.0403
  group B: FMR 0.00182, FNMR 0.0400
group A own threshold 0.279: FNMR 0.0210
group B own threshold 0.337: FNMR 0.0517
```

A shift of 0.06 in the mean impostor score, two-thirds of one standard deviation, makes group B's false match rate 10 times group A's at the same threshold (0.182% against 0.018%), although the two groups' false non-match rates are equal. Choosing a separate threshold per group equalizes FMR, but then group B's FNMR rises to 5.2% while A's falls to 2.1%: one error rate or the other differs. Which trade-off is acceptable is a policy and legal question, not a purely technical one, and group-specific thresholds raise their own concerns. The technical lessons are firmer: measure both error rates per group at the operating threshold, on data like the deployment data, and remember that the overall FAR can hide a tenfold difference (Figure 7).

## Presentation attacks

**Plain version.** A recognition network answers "who is this?", not "is this a real, live face?". A printed photo, a phone screen showing a video, or a mask can be presented to the camera. Detecting such attacks is a separate task.

A **presentation attack** is an attempt to fool the sensor with an artifact; **presentation attack detection** (PAD), also called face anti-spoofing, decides whether the input is *bona fide* (a live person) or an attack. Yu et al. survey deep-learning methods for it [37]: beyond binary "live versus attack" labels, many methods use pixel-wise auxiliary supervision such as pseudo depth maps; the field now emphasizes **domain generalization** (attacks, cameras and lighting not seen in training) and **open-set** attacks of new types; and multi-modal sensors (depth, infrared) or special sensors (light field, flash) help. PAD runs alongside recognition, and the two error types are evaluated separately; a strong recognizer with no PAD is easy to fool with a good photo of the enrolled person. Digital attacks such as morphed passport photos and deepfakes are related but distinct threats.

## Privacy, consent and the law

**Plain version.** A face embedding is a biometric identifier: it can link a person's photos across the internet and cameras, and a person cannot change their face the way they change a password. Many legal systems therefore treat it as sensitive data. This section describes the context; it is not legal advice.

Two points from European law illustrate how regulators view face recognition (rules differ between jurisdictions, and they change):

- Under the EU's General Data Protection Regulation, "biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person" is one of the categories of **sensitive** personal data, which are subject to specific processing conditions (GDPR Article 9) [38].
- The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) lists among its **prohibited practices** the untargeted scraping of the internet or CCTV material to create or expand facial recognition databases, and real-time remote biometric identification for law enforcement purposes in publicly accessible spaces; the prohibitions apply from 2 February 2025 [39]. The Regulation itself defines the exact scope and conditions.

For engineers, the practical consequences are concrete. Know where every training image came from and under what terms; the withdrawn datasets above show that "publicly visible" is not the same as "available for any use". Treat stored templates as sensitive data: limit who can query a gallery, log searches, and delete templates that are no longer needed. Prefer designs that keep templates on the user's device for verification when the application allows it. Document the intended use and the measured error rates per group, as recommended by NIST [2]. Synthetic training data [31], [33] reduces, but does not remove, these concerns, because the deployed system still processes real faces.

## Failure modes and practical advice

**Plain version.** Most failures come from a mismatch between the conditions in training and testing: a face turned away, half covered, much older, blurred, or from a population the system rarely saw. A threshold tuned in one setting silently changes meaning in another.

- **Pose.** Large yaw (profile views) breaks five-point alignment and removes half the face. IJB-B was built with human-labeled face boxes precisely because datasets filtered by a commodity face detector under-represent such poses and occlusions [27], and the ArcFace authors cite pose and age gaps as conditions where plain softmax features degrade [16].
- **Occlusion.** Masks, sunglasses, hands and hair hide parts of the face; detectors may still fire but landmarks drift, and the embedding moves toward the impostor region.
- **Age.** Templates enrolled years earlier match less well; MegaFace evaluated performance with respect to age [24], and NIST found elevated false positives for the elderly and children [2].
- **Image quality.** Low resolution, motion blur, compression and poor lighting raise false non-matches; NIST linked several false-negative differentials to image quality rather than to demographics [2]. Quality-aware methods (MagFace, AdaFace [19], [20]) and quality checks at capture time help.
- **Threshold drift.** A threshold chosen on one dataset gives a different FAR on another camera, population or gallery size (Figures 6 and 7). Recalibrate on deployment-like impostor pairs, and monitor score distributions after deployment, as in [ML 11](/en/learn/ml/evaluation-deployment/#monitoring-after-deployment).
- **Label noise and identity overlap.** Web-collected training sets contain mislabeled images and the same person under two names; sub-center ArcFace [18] and dataset cleaning [29] target this. Overlap between training identities and test identities inflates open-set results, so check it before reporting.
- **Evaluation shortcuts.** Reporting only accuracy on LFW, only rank-1 on a small closed gallery, or only one error rate hides exactly the failures above.

## Modern view

### What the surveys say

Wang and Deng's survey [1] is the standard map of deep face recognition. It sorts the losses into **Euclidean-distance-based** (contrastive, triplet, center loss), **angular/cosine-margin-based** (SphereFace, CosFace, ArcFace) and **softmax variations** (feature and weight normalization), and it traces how Euclidean-distance-based losses dominated the early years and how angular/cosine-margin losses and feature and weight normalization took over from 2017. Drozdowski et al. [36] survey demographic bias across biometrics: how bias is estimated, how it might be mitigated, and the social and technical questions that remain. Yu et al. [37] survey deep face anti-spoofing, including methods designed for domain generalization and open-set attacks. Boutros et al. [33] survey synthetic data for face recognition, organize its use cases and discuss the challenges that remain.

*Optional: [Guide to the Modern view surveys](#guide-to-the-modern-view-surveys) (in the Paper guides). What each survey covers, how it is organized and how to use it as a map.*

### The state of the art, 2023–2026

- **Margin-based softmax losses on very large datasets remain the standard recipe.** The main changes since ArcFace are adaptive margins tied to image quality (MagFace [19], AdaFace [20]), robustness to label noise (sub-center ArcFace [18]) and efficient training over millions of identities (Partial FC [21], applied with both CNN and ViT backbones).
- **Benchmarks moved to low-FAR, template-based and operational evaluation.** LFW is saturated [31]; IJB-B/C at TAR@FAR = $10^{-4}$ became the academic norm [16], and NIST's FRTE (the evaluation known as FRVT until 2023 [3]) tests submitted algorithms on large operational data at false match rates as low as $10^{-6}$.
- **Synthetic and privacy-aware training data** is an active direction, with graphics-rendered (DigiFace-1M [31]) and diffusion-generated (DCFace [32]) datasets, motivated by consent, bias and label-noise problems of web data [33].
- **Fairness is measured and reported**, not assumed: NIST's demographic analysis [2] and dedicated test sets such as RFW [34] made per-group error rates part of evaluation.

### Open problems

1. **Generalization to unseen conditions**: extreme pose, occlusion, very low resolution and long age gaps.
2. **Demographic differentials**: measuring both error types per group on deployment-like data, and reducing differentials without simply moving errors from one group to another.
3. **Training data without consent problems** that still reaches the accuracy of web-scale real data [33].
4. **Presentation and digital attacks** of types not seen in training [37].
5. **Calibrated, quality-aware scores**, so that a threshold keeps its meaning across cameras and populations.
6. **Governance**: dataset stewardship after release [30], and legal limits that differ between jurisdictions [38], [39].

## Key takeaways

- Embeddings support comparison with identities absent from training; classification labels alone do not.
- State the recognition task and gallery size before selecting metrics or thresholds.
- Detection and alignment errors propagate into embedding comparisons.
- Pair-based losses require informative samples; margin losses shape separation around class centers.
- Report verification TAR at a stated FAR and impostor-pair count; use task-specific identification metrics for search.
- Check errors by group and operating condition, verify dataset availability, and protect biometric templates.

### Aligning a face: the least-squares similarity transform

This note derives the alignment fit used in the main text and lists what can go wrong with it.

**The problem.** Given $n$ detected landmarks $\mathbf{p}_i = (u_i, v_i)$ and template positions $\mathbf{q}_i = (u'_i, v'_i)$, find $a, b, t_x, t_y$ minimizing

$$
E(a, b, t_x, t_y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[ (a u_i - b v_i + t_x - u'_i)^2 + (b u_i + a v_i + t_y - v'_i)^2 \Big].
$$

Each landmark contributes two rows to a linear system $A\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{r}$ with $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (a, b, t_x, t_y)^\top$: the row $(u_i, -v_i, 1, 0)$ with right side $u'_i$, and the row $(v_i, u_i, 0, 1)$ with right side $v'_i$. The least-squares solution satisfies the normal equations $A^\top A\,\boldsymbol{\beta} = A^\top \mathbf{r}$, which `np.linalg.lstsq` solves stably.

**A closed form.** Center both point sets: $\bar{\mathbf{p}} = \frac1n\sum_i \mathbf{p}_i$, $\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i = \mathbf{p}_i - \bar{\mathbf{p}}$, and likewise $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i$. Then the optimal $a$ and $b$ are

$$
a = \frac{\sum_i \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i^\top \tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i}{\sum_i \lVert \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i \rVert^2}, \qquad
b = \frac{\sum_i (\tilde u_i \tilde v'_i - \tilde v_i \tilde u'_i)}{\sum_i \lVert \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i \rVert^2},
$$

where $(\tilde u_i, \tilde v_i)$ and $(\tilde u'_i, \tilde v'_i)$ are the coordinates of $\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_i$, and the translation is $\mathbf{t} = \bar{\mathbf{q}} - \begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{bmatrix}\bar{\mathbf{p}}$. Scale and angle follow as $\sigma = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and $\phi = \operatorname{atan2}(b, a)$. The numerator of $b$ is a sum of 2-D cross products, so $b = 0$ exactly when the best rotation is zero.

**Why not a more flexible warp?** DeepFace went the other way and used explicit 3D face modeling with a piecewise affine warp [6], but most later systems settled on the simple 2-D similarity transform. A full affine transform (six parameters) can also shear and stretch the face differently in $x$ and $y$; a projective one (eight parameters) can do more. With only five noisy landmarks, these extra degrees of freedom mostly fit the noise and distort the face's proportions, which are part of what identifies a person. The similarity transform preserves angles and ratios of distances.

**Edge cases.**

- *Reflections.* The parameterization $\begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{bmatrix}$ cannot mirror the image, which is what we want; a mirrored face is a different image (though horizontal flipping is sometimes used as test-time augmentation).
- *Degenerate configurations.* If all landmarks are (nearly) the same point, $\sum_i \lVert \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i \rVert^2 \approx 0$ and the scale is undefined; this happens for tiny faces or failed detections and should be rejected before alignment.
- *Profile faces.* For large yaw, one eye and mouth corner are hidden, and the detector's estimates for them are guesses. A five-point frontal template then pulls the face into an unnatural crop. Systems either use pose-specific templates or accept that alignment quality falls with pose.
- *One bad landmark.* Least squares is sensitive to an outlier: one landmark that is 20 pixels off moves the whole fit. Checking the residual (as the code prints) and rejecting fits with a large residual is a simple safeguard.

### How large must the scale s be?

This note explains why normalized softmax needs the scale $s$ and how CosFace bounds it from below.

**Why a scale at all.** With unit features and unit weights, every logit is a cosine in $[-1, 1]$. Even in the best case, the correct class has logit 1 and every other class has logit at least $-1$, so the softmax probability of the correct class is at most $e^{1}/(e^{1} + (C-1)e^{-1})$. For $C = 10{,}000$ classes this is about $7 \times 10^{-4}$: the network cannot make the loss small however well it arranges the angles, and training stalls. NormFace identified this problem and showed that a scale parameter after normalization is necessary [11].

**CosFace's lower bound.** Suppose the learned features of each class lie around their class center, and let $P_W$ be the desired expected minimum posterior probability of a class center (the probability the softmax assigns to class $j$ for a feature exactly at its center $W_j$). CosFace shows that this requires [13]

$$
s \ge \frac{C - 1}{C} \, \ln \frac{(C - 1)\, P_W}{1 - P_W},
$$

where $C$ is the number of classes and $\ln$ the natural logarithm. The bound grows with $C$ (logarithmically) and with the desired confidence $P_W$.

**A worked example.** For $C = 10{,}000$ and $P_W = 0.9$: $\frac{9{,}999}{10{,}000}\ln\frac{9{,}999 \times 0.9}{0.1} = \ln(89{,}991) \times 0.9999 \approx 11.4$. For $C = 100{,}000$ and $P_W = 0.99$ the bound is about 16.1. The value $s = 64$ used by CosFace and ArcFace [13], [16] is far above these bounds.

```python
import numpy as np

def s_lower_bound(C, P):
    return (C - 1) / C * np.log((C - 1) * P / (1 - P))

for C, P in [(10_000, 0.9), (100_000, 0.99)]:
    print(f"C = {C:7,d}, P_W = {P}: s >= {s_lower_bound(C, P):.1f}")
```

```text
C =  10,000, P_W = 0.9: s >= 11.4
C = 100,000, P_W = 0.99: s >= 16.1
```

**Too small or too large.** If $s$ is below the bound, the loss cannot get close to zero, and even well-separated samples keep receiving gradients that push them around. If $s$ is very large, the softmax becomes nearly a hard maximum: the probabilities saturate at 0 or 1 early, most samples stop contributing gradients, and training can become unstable. In practice $s$ is a hyperparameter fixed for a given number of classes; the scale and the margin interact, because $s \cdot m$ sets how much the target logit is lowered.

### Margin losses side by side: one formula and its edge cases

This note writes the three margins as one formula, derives their two-class boundaries, and gives the gradient used in the toy code.

**One formula.** The ArcFace journal paper writes SphereFace, ArcFace and CosFace as special cases of a **combined margin** target logit [16]

$$
\psi(\theta) = \cos(m_1 \theta + m_2) - m_3,
$$

where $m_1$ is SphereFace's multiplicative angular margin, $m_2$ ArcFace's additive angular margin and $m_3$ CosFace's additive cosine margin. $(m_1, m_2, m_3) = (1, 0, 0)$ is normalized softmax; $(1, 0.5, 0)$ is ArcFace; $(1, 0, 0.35)$ is CosFace; and the paper also tries combinations of the three [16].

**Two-class boundaries.** Let the centers $W_1$ and $W_2$ be an angle $\varphi$ apart and consider a feature on the arc between them, at angle $\theta_1$ from $W_1$ and $\theta_2 = \varphi - \theta_1$ from $W_2$. Class 1 is satisfied when $\psi(\theta_1) \gt \cos\theta_2$.

- *ArcFace:* $\cos(\theta_1 + m) \gt \cos(\varphi - \theta_1)$. Both arguments lie in $[0, \pi]$ (when $\theta_1 + m \le \pi$), where cosine is decreasing, so the condition is $\theta_1 + m \lt \varphi - \theta_1$, that is $\theta_1 \lt (\varphi - m)/2$. By symmetry class 2 is satisfied when $\theta_1 \gt (\varphi + m)/2$, so the zone where neither is satisfied has width exactly $m$, independent of $\varphi$.
- *SphereFace* (on the first branch of $\psi$): $m\theta_1 \lt \varphi - \theta_1$, so $\theta_1 \lt \varphi/(m+1)$; the gap is $\varphi - 2\varphi/(m+1) = \varphi(m-1)/(m+1)$. Close centers get a small margin and distant centers a large one.
- *CosFace:* $\cos\theta_1 - \cos(\varphi - \theta_1) \gt m$. Using $\cos A - \cos B = -2\sin\frac{A+B}{2}\sin\frac{A-B}{2}$, this is $2\sin\frac{\varphi}{2}\sin\big(\frac{\varphi}{2} - \theta_1\big) \gt m$, so $\theta_1 \lt \frac{\varphi}{2} - \arcsin\!\big(\frac{m}{2\sin(\varphi/2)}\big)$. The angular gap is $2\arcsin\!\big(\frac{m}{2\sin(\varphi/2)}\big)$: larger for close centers, and undefined (no satisfiable region on the arc) if $2\sin(\varphi/2) \lt m$. For $\varphi = 80^\circ$ and $m = 0.35$ it is about $31^\circ$, as in Figure 2.

**The gradient of the ArcFace logit.** Training uses the derivative of the target logit with respect to the cosine $c = \cos\theta$, because the cosine is what the network computes. With $\theta = \arccos c$,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial c}\, s\cos(\theta + m) = s\,\sin(\theta + m)\,\frac{1}{\sin\theta},
$$

since $\frac{d}{d\theta}\cos(\theta + m) = -\sin(\theta + m)$ and $\frac{d\theta}{dc} = -1/\sin\theta$. This is the factor `np.sin(th + margin) / np.sin(th)` in the toy code. It is larger than 1 whenever $\sin(\theta + m) \gt \sin\theta$, that is for $\theta \lt (\pi - m)/2$, so the margin *amplifies* the gradient on samples that are already close to their center, until the softmax probability saturates. Two edge cases follow. Near $\theta = 0$ the factor divides by $\sin\theta \approx 0$, so implementations clip the cosine away from $\pm 1$ (the toy code clips at $1 - 10^{-7}$). For $\theta + m \gt \pi$ the factor becomes negative and the logit is no longer monotone, as noted in the main text; such samples need a fallback penalty.

**Why easy samples stop contributing.** The gradient with respect to the logits is $p_j - [j = y_i]$, where $p_j$ is the softmax probability. When $s\,\psi(\theta_{y_i})$ is far above every $s\cos\theta_j$, $p_{y_i} \to 1$ and all these differences go to zero. The margin moves the point where this happens: a sample must be inside its class's satisfied region by a clear amount before its gradient vanishes, which is exactly how margins create compact clusters.

### Beyond one center per class: sub-centers, quality-aware margins and Partial FC

This note describes, at the level of the papers' own summaries, how four extensions change the basic margin loss and what each one is for.

**Sub-center ArcFace** [18], [16]. Each identity $j$ gets $K$ unit vectors $W_{j,1}, \dots, W_{j,K}$ instead of one. For a feature $\mathbf{x}$, the similarity to class $j$ is the largest of its $K$ cosines, $\cos\theta_j = \max_k \cos\angle(\mathbf{x}, W_{j,k})$, and the usual ArcFace loss is applied to these class-level cosines. A sample therefore only has to be near *any* of its class's sub-centers. In web-collected data, most clean images of a person gather around one dominant sub-center, while mislabeled or very unusual images form small non-dominant ones; the authors use this to automatically clean noisy training data [16]. Pitfall: with large $K$ and little noise, the sub-centers of one person can drift apart and the class is no longer compact, which is why the journal version describes dropping non-dominant sub-centers after cleaning [16].

**MagFace** [19]. Instead of fixing the feature length, MagFace lets the magnitude $\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert$ vary within a range, makes the angular margin depend on it and adds a regularizer on it, such that the magnitude increases monotonically with how likely the face is to be recognized. The magnitude then doubles as a **face-quality score**, which can be used to reject poor images before matching. The paper also describes an adaptive within-class structure: easy samples are pulled toward the class center and hard samples pushed away, which reduces overfitting to noisy, low-quality images [19].

**AdaFace** [20]. AdaFace starts from the observation that a margin loss emphasizes hard (misclassified) samples, but a hard sample may be hard because it is unrecognizable (very low quality) rather than informative. It makes the margin a function of image quality, approximated by the feature norm, so that the emphasis on hard samples is strong for good-quality images and weak for very poor ones [20]. Its experiments target low-quality benchmarks such as IJB-S and TinyFace as well as IJB-B and IJB-C [20].

**Partial FC** [21]. The last layer of a margin softmax holds one center per identity; with millions of identities it dominates memory and computation. Partial FC keeps all centers but, in each iteration, computes the loss only over the positive centers of the batch and a random subset of negative centers, and updates only those. This cuts computation and also reduces two side problems of huge web datasets: conflicts between classes that are actually the same person, and rare updates of tail classes [21]. Pitfall: the sampled subset changes the softmax denominator, so the sampling rate is a hyperparameter that affects results, not just speed.

### Measuring very small error rates

This note explains how many comparisons are needed to support a claimed FAR, and why counting pairs overstates the evidence.

**The rule of three.** Suppose each of $n$ impostor comparisons independently gives a false accept with probability $p$, and you observe zero false accepts. The probability of that outcome is $(1 - p)^n$. The largest $p$ that is still consistent with zero errors at the 95% level solves $(1 - p)^n = 0.05$, so $n\ln(1 - p) = \ln 0.05 \approx -3.0$, and for small $p$, $\ln(1 - p) \approx -p$, giving

$$
p_{95} \approx \frac{3}{n}.
$$

So zero false accepts in 30,000 impostor pairs supports only "FAR below about $10^{-4}$", and a claim of $10^{-6}$ needs at least three million impostor comparisons with no errors.

**With some errors.** If you observe $k \gt 0$ false accepts in $n$ trials, the estimate is $\hat p = k/n$, and an exact (Clopper–Pearson) interval can be computed from the beta distribution, for example with `scipy.stats.beta.ppf`. The relative uncertainty is roughly $1/\sqrt{k}$: with $k = 49$ false accepts (as in the main text's FAR = $10^{-4}$ line) the estimate is good to about $\pm 30\%$ at the 95% level; with $k = 4$ it is good to about a factor of two.

**Pairs are not independent.** Impostor pairs are usually formed by comparing every image with every other image. If one person's image is unusual (blurred, half-occluded, or simply similar to many others), it takes part in thousands of pairs and can produce many false accepts at once. The effective number of independent trials is then closer to the number of *people* or *images* than to the number of pairs. Resampling by person (a bootstrap over identities, as in the bootstrap section of [ML 11](/en/learn/ml/evaluation-deployment/#the-bootstrap)) gives more honest intervals than the binomial formula.

### Before deep learning: eigenfaces and Fisherfaces

This note connects the chapter to the classical subspace methods for faces.

**Eigenfaces.** Turk and Pentland represented a face image as a long vector of pixel values and applied principal component analysis to a training set of aligned faces [40]. The leading principal components, viewed as images, are the "eigenfaces"; a face is described by its coordinates in the subspace they span, and recognition is nearest-neighbor matching in that subspace. The method is PCA exactly as in [PR 05](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/features-dimensionality/#principal-component-analysis). Its weakness is the one PR 05 points out for PCA in general: the directions of largest variance in face images are often due to lighting and pose, not identity.

**Fisherfaces.** Belhumeur, Hespanha and Kriegman replaced PCA by Fisher's linear discriminant analysis, which finds directions that maximize between-class scatter relative to within-class scatter [41] ([PR 05](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/features-dimensionality/#fishers-linear-discriminant-analysis)). Because the within-class scatter matrix of high-dimensional pixel vectors is singular when there are few images per person, they first reduce dimension with PCA, then apply LDA in the reduced space [41].

**The link to this chapter.** Both methods learn a linear embedding and compare faces by distance, which is the same structure as a deep face system. Fisherfaces already optimize a ratio of between-class to within-class spread, the same goal that margins pursue; deep networks replace the linear map by a learned nonlinear one and the scatter ratio by a margin softmax.

These guides are optional: each walks through one paper the chapter builds on — the problem it poses, the idea, what it showed and what to check when you read it.

### ArcFace (Deng et al., CVPR 2019; TPAMI 2022)

#### Challenge

By 2018 several losses added margins to the softmax (SphereFace, CosFace), with different geometric meanings and training difficulties. The authors wanted a margin with a clear geometric interpretation that is also easy to train at scale [15].

<Figure src={paperArcfaceSource} alt="Normalized features and class weights produce an angle; the target-class angular margin changes the training logit before rescaling and cross-entropy." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Normalized features and class weights produce an angle; the target-class angular margin changes the training logit before rescaling and cross-entropy." />

*Figure source: [15], Published paper PDF, PDF page 3. [Original PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2019/papers/Deng_ArcFace_Additive_Angular_Margin_Loss_for_Deep_Face_Recognition_CVPR_2019_paper.pdf).*

#### Method

Normalize features and class weights, rescale by $s$, and add an angular margin $m$ to the angle between a feature and its own class center: the target logit becomes $s\cos(\theta + m)$. Because the margin is added to the angle, it equals a geodesic distance on the hypersphere [15]. The journal version [16] extends the work with sub-center ArcFace for noisy web data, a combined-margin analysis that places SphereFace, CosFace and ArcFace in one formula, and further experiments (including inverting the model to synthesize faces).

#### Results

The experiments compare losses with the backbone and training data held fixed, written as [training set, network, loss] [16]. Read the tables in that spirit: compare rows that differ only in the loss; a change of training set (for example a cleaned versus a raw web set) moves results by more than many loss changes. The main test sets are LFW and similar pair-based sets, MegaFace, and IJB-B/IJB-C; for IJB the reported number is TAR at FAR = $10^{-4}$ on template pairs, where a template's embedding is the mean of its images' embeddings [16]. The experimental setup (five RetinaFace landmarks, $112 \times 112$ crops, 512-D embeddings, $s = 64$, $m = 0.5$) is documented in the paper [16] and has become a common default.

#### Problems

The gains on LFW are tiny because LFW is saturated; the informative comparisons are at low FAR on IJB-B/C and on large galleries. Several training sets used in this line of work have since been withdrawn or changed [30], which makes exact reproduction difficult. The margin assumes labels are correct; with heavy label noise the plain loss suffers, which is what sub-centers address.

#### Conclusion

ArcFace is the reference form of the margin-based softmax in this chapter, and its geometric argument (constant geodesic margin) is the one Figure 2 illustrates.

### NIST FRVT Part 3: Demographic Effects (Grother, Ngan and Hanaoka, 2019)

#### Challenge

Public claims about bias in face recognition were often based on small studies, on different tasks (such as gender classification), or on a single error type. NIST set out to quantify demographic differentials of contemporary algorithms on large operational datasets [2].

<Figure src={paperNistProtocol} alt="Teaching protocol diagram — split genuine and impostor comparisons, apply the stated threshold and report errors for defined groups. The diagram is not a reproduction of a NIST result plot." caption="Teaching protocol diagram — split genuine and impostor comparisons, apply the stated threshold and report errors for defined groups. The diagram is not a reproduction of a NIST result plot." />

#### Method

Run many submitted one-to-one and one-to-many algorithms on four large U.S. government image collections with sex, age and race or country-of-birth metadata, and report false positive and false negative rates per group at fixed thresholds [2].

#### Results

The executive summary's main result is that false positive differentials were much larger than false negative ones, often by factors of 10 to beyond 100 across groups, and that the size and even the direction of effects depended on the algorithm and the dataset; image quality explained several false negative differences; and some accurate one-to-many algorithms showed undetectable false positive differentials [2].

#### Reading the evidence

Always check three things for any number you quote: which error (FMR or FNMR, FPIR or FNIR), at which threshold, and on which dataset. The report gives results per algorithm in extensive annexes, so "face recognition is biased by X" is not a statement the report supports; "algorithm A on dataset D at threshold t has false match rates that differ by X between groups" is. The datasets are not public, so results cannot be reproduced outside NIST, and the algorithms were prototypes submitted at the time, not necessarily deployed products [2].

#### Problems

Country of birth is used as a proxy for race in three of the four datasets [2]; the report measures differentials but does not decide which differences are acceptable; and algorithms have changed since 2019, so current per-algorithm results should be taken from NIST's ongoing evaluations [3].

#### Conclusion

It is the basis for the fairness section's main claims and for the advice to report both error types per group at the operating threshold.

#### Keep the operating point explicit

For one-to-one verification, suppose larger similarity score $S$ means a stronger match and acceptance occurs when $S\ge t$. For the report's defined comparison stratum $g$,

$$
\operatorname{FMR}_g(t)=\Pr(S\ge t\mid\text{different identities},g),\qquad
\operatorname{FNMR}_g(t)=\Pr(S<t\mid\text{same identity},g).
$$

$t$ is the threshold; $g$ must specify how the pair/population is grouped. A cross-group pair and a same-group pair need not be the same stratum. Both errors depend on the threshold and the image population. Changing to a separate threshold for every group answers a different operating-policy question from comparing groups at one common threshold. Neither rate is itself a complete definition of fairness [2].

One-to-many search additionally depends on the gallery and search decision, so its FPIR/FNIR cannot simply be substituted for the two verification rates. Read uncertainty and small comparison counts alongside an observed differential; an undetected difference is not proof that all populations and capture conditions behave identically [2], [3].

### Guide to the Modern view surveys

Choose a reading by topic: Shape, Local keypoints, Texture, Matching.

<Figure src={paperReadingMap} alt="Face systems: recognition, fairness and spoof resistance" caption="Teaching reading map — the cards compare questions addressed by different papers. They are complementary categories, not a performance ranking or a diagram copied from one source." />

#### Deep face recognition: A survey

(Wang and Deng, *Neurocomputing*, 2021) [1]. Coverage: architectures, loss functions, face processing, datasets and protocols, and application scenes. Organization: losses in three families (Euclidean-distance-based, angular/cosine-margin-based, softmax variations); face processing in two ("one-to-many augmentation", which generates many images with pose variation from one image, and "many-to-one normalization", which recovers a canonical frontal view); scenes such as cross-factor, heterogeneous, multiple-media and industrial recognition. Use it as a map when you meet an unfamiliar loss or benchmark: find its family, then read the original paper.

#### Demographic bias in biometrics: A survey on an emerging challenge

(Drozdowski et al., *IEEE Transactions on Technology and Society*, 2020) [36]. Coverage: algorithmic bias in biometrics generally, the literature on estimating and mitigating it, and the related technical and social issues. Use it to see how fairness is measured in different studies, which makes it easier to compare their claims.

#### Deep learning for face anti-spoofing: A survey

(Yu et al., *IEEE TPAMI*, 2023) [37]. Coverage: deep PAD with binary and pixel-wise supervision, domain generalization and open-set protocols, and multi-modal or specialized sensors. Use it to understand what the PAD benchmarks test and which protocols are hardest.

#### Synthetic data for face recognition: Current state and future prospects

(Boutros et al., *Image and Vision Computing*, 2023) [33]. Coverage: a taxonomy of use cases of synthetic face data, recent recognition models trained on it, and the remaining challenges. Use it to place DigiFace-1M, DCFace and later synthetic datasets.

#### Reading these papers critically

Treat these as separate claims about a face system. Identity discrimination does not establish resistance to presentation attacks, and high average recognition accuracy does not establish equal error rates across groups. Synthetic training data can reduce dependence on particular collections without automatically removing demographic bias, privacy concerns or evaluation leakage. The surveys are useful precisely because they expose these different questions.

Read each result with its protocol: verification versus identification, the operating threshold, false-match versus false-nonmatch errors, attack types and whether the test domains were seen during training. A change in population composition can change an average without improving every group. Anti-spoofing evaluated on known attack media does not establish open-set resistance. For synthetic-data studies, inspect the identity source, generator training provenance and independence of the recognition test set. Use the recognition survey for losses and pipelines, the bias survey for measurement choices, the PAD survey for attack protocols and the synthetic-data survey for data construction; one benchmark cannot replace all four.
