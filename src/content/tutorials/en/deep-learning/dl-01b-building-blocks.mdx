## The big picture

A vision network looks complicated in a paper figure, but it is built from a small number of block types, repeated and connected. Two networks that use the same blocks can behave very differently because they are **composed** differently: where the resolution changes, where the skips go, where normalization sits, how the output is formed. This chapter is about those composition decisions.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Think of a toolbox: saws, brushes, glue. A furniture maker uses them to turn a big log into one small chair leg: cut away most of the wood, keep only what matters. A picture restorer uses the same tools very differently: the painting must come back the same size, every brushstroke in place, just cleaner. A classifier is the furniture maker: it throws away pixels until only the answer ("cat") is left. A restoration network is the restorer: it must give back every pixel. Same tools, opposite plans.
</Callout>

**Why it matters.** Practical work is rarely inventing a block. It is choosing a backbone, adapting it to a new task, or deciding which of a paper's ten changes actually helped. A classifier backbone used unchanged for denoising throws away detail, and a block that helped in one paper may not help in your design space.

**Where the basics are.** The blocks themselves are taught elsewhere on this site, and this chapter links there instead of repeating them: convolution, receptive field and residual blocks in [ML 03](/en/learn/ml/convolutional-networks/#receptive-field) and [DLCV 01](/en/learn/deep-learning/deep-learning-for-image-processing/#resnet-2015-and-why-residual-connections-work); the Transformer block, Swin and efficient attention in [ML 05](/en/learn/ml/vision-transformers/#the-transformer-block); the classification backbones in [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#resnet-learning-residuals); encoder–decoders and ASPP in [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-segmentation/#encoderdecoders-and-skip-connections); restoration backbones and upsampling in [DLCV 09](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-restoration/#general-backbones) and [DLCV 09A](/en/learn/deep-learning/super-resolution/#decision-2-how-to-upsample-transposed-or-sub-pixel-convolution). ## Anatomy of a network

**Plain version.** Almost every vision network has three parts: a **stem** that turns pixels into features, a **body** of repeated blocks, and a **head** that turns features into the answer. What differs between tasks is mostly what happens to the image size along the way.

**Precise version.** Write a feature map as a tensor of shape $C \times H \times W$, where $C$ is the number of channels (the **width**) and $H \times W$ the spatial size. The common parts are:

- **Stem**: the first one or two layers. ResNet uses a $7 \times 7$ convolution with stride 2 and a $3 \times 3$ max-pool with stride 2, so the body starts at $1/4$ of the input resolution [1]. ConvNeXt replaces this with a "patchify" $4 \times 4$ convolution with stride 4 [2]; ViT cuts the image into $16 \times 16$ patches [3]. Restoration networks use a single $3 \times 3$ convolution at full resolution, which SwinIR calls **shallow feature extraction** [4].
- **Stage**: a run of blocks that share one resolution and one width. A **block** is the repeated unit (a residual block, a Transformer block).
- **Downsampling layer** (transition): halves $H$ and $W$, usually while doubling $C$. It can be a strided convolution inside the first block of a stage (ResNet), a separate $2 \times 2$ convolution with stride 2 (ConvNeXt, NAFNet), patch merging (Swin), or pixel unshuffle (Restormer).
- **Neck**: an optional module that mixes features from several stages, such as the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) used by detectors [5].
- **Head**: global average pooling and a linear layer for classification; a final convolution, and for super-resolution an upsampler, for restoration.

The **output stride** of a stage is the ratio between the input size and the stage's feature-map size. ResNet's four stages have output strides 4, 8, 16 and 32 [1]; so do Swin's [6]. Networks that keep one resolution and width throughout, like ViT, are **isotropic**; those with stages at decreasing resolution are **hierarchical**.

ResNet also states the most copied rule of macro design: when the feature map is halved, the number of filters is doubled "so as to preserve the time complexity per layer" [1]. A $3 \times 3$ convolution with $C$ input and output channels on an $H \times W$ map costs $9 H W C^2$ multiply-accumulates (MACs); after halving the map and doubling the width it costs $9 \cdot \tfrac{H}{2} \cdot \tfrac{W}{2} \cdot (2C)^2 = 9 H W C^2$, the same. ## The block catalogue

**Plain version.** A block has two jobs: mix information across **space** (neighboring pixels or tokens) and mix information across **channels** (features at one position). Every block in the catalogue does both, in a different proportion and at a different cost. Around them sit three helpers: skips, normalization and nonlinearities.

The MetaFormer paper makes this split explicit: a Transformer block is a **token mixer** (attention) followed by a **channel mixer** (the MLP), each in a residual branch with normalization, and replacing attention by a simple spatial pooling operator still gave competitive accuracy on ImageNet [7], which suggests that the general structure carries much of the performance [8]. ### Residual connections and residual scaling

**Plain version.** A residual block adds its input back to its output, so the block only has to learn a correction, and the identity path carries signal and gradient through any depth. The open questions are where to put normalization and activations relative to the addition, and how large the correction should be at the start.

**Precise version.** The general form is

$$
y = x + \alpha \, F(x),
$$

where $x$ is the block input, $F$ the residual branch (convolutions, attention, normalization), $y$ the output and $\alpha$ a scale on the branch. Three placements of the nonlinearity are common:

- **Post-activation** (original ResNet): $y = \mathrm{ReLU}(x + F(x))$, with BN inside $F$ [1].
- **Pre-activation**: BN and ReLU move inside the branch, before each convolution, so the shortcut is a pure identity; He et al. found this easier to train at very large depth [11]. The pre-norm Transformer block, $y = x + \mathrm{MHA}(\mathrm{LN}(x))$, is the same idea ([ML 05](/en/learn/ml/vision-transformers/#the-transformer-block)).
- **No activation after the addition**: EDSR, MobileNetV2, ConvNeXt, NAFNet and Restormer all end the block with the addition.

Several designs control $\alpha$ explicitly. EDSR found training numerically unstable once the number of feature maps grew large, and fixed it with a constant residual scaling of 0.1 in its final model of 32 blocks with 256 channels [12]. ConvNeXt uses Layer Scale [13], a learnable per-channel $\alpha$, initialized to $10^{-6}$ [2]. NAFNet's code multiplies each branch by learnable per-channel parameters initialized to zero, so every block starts as the identity [14]; zero-initializing the last BN scale of a ResNet branch does the same ([DLCV 01A](/en/learn/deep-learning/training-practice/#initialization-and-normalization-in-practice)). The shared reason: a deep stack of identity blocks is easy to optimize, and each block can then grow its correction.

Skips also come at two scales. **Short skips** wrap one block; **long skips** wrap a group of blocks or the whole body. RCAN's "residual in residual" structure uses both: residual groups with long skip connections, each containing blocks with short skip connections [15].

Training and inference can also use different block graphs: RepVGG [54] trains with additional affine branches and converts them into one convolution for deployment.

### Bottlenecks, inverted residuals and depthwise separable convolutions

**Plain version.** Spatial filtering and channel mixing need not happen in one expensive layer. Bottlenecks run the expensive $3 \times 3$ on fewer channels; depthwise convolutions filter each channel alone and leave mixing to cheap $1 \times 1$ layers. These blocks change cost by large factors, so it pays to count.

**Precise version.** A dense $k \times k$ convolution from $a$ to $b$ channels has $k^2 a b$ weights and, at stride 1, costs $k^2 a b$ MACs per output pixel. A depthwise $k \times k$ convolution on $a$ channels has $k^2 a$ of each. The ResNet bottleneck reduces $C$ to $C/4$, filters, and expands back [1]. The MobileNetV2 **inverted residual** does the opposite: it expands $C$ to $tC$ with a $1 \times 1$ convolution (expansion factor $t = 6$ in the paper's main experiments), filters with a $3 \times 3$ depthwise convolution, and projects back with a **linear** $1 \times 1$ convolution, without an activation, because the authors argue that a ReLU in the narrow layer destroys information [10]. The paper gives its cost as $h \cdot w \cdot d' \cdot t\,(d' + k^2 + d'')$ multiply-adds for an $h \times w$ input with $d'$ input and $d''$ output channels [10]. The snippet counts weights and MACs per output pixel for the blocks of Figure 2 at $C = 64$, on a $128 \times 128$ map, and checks the inverted residual against that formula.

```python
# Weights (no biases or norm parameters) and MACs per output pixel of common blocks,
# all with C input and C output channels at stride 1. A dense k x k conv from a to b
# channels has k*k*a*b weights and costs k*k*a*b MACs per pixel; a depthwise one k*k*a.
def blocks(C, n_tokens):
    g = int(2.66 * C)                                  # Restormer GDFN hidden width
    return {                                           # name: (weights, MACs per pixel)
        'basic residual (2 x 3x3)':     (18 * C * C, 18 * C * C),
        'bottleneck (1x1, 3x3, 1x1)':   (2 * C * C // 4 + 9 * (C // 4) ** 2,) * 2,
        'depthwise separable 3x3':      (9 * C + C * C,) * 2,
        'inverted residual, t = 6':     (12 * C * C + 54 * C,) * 2,
        'ConvNeXt (7x7 dw, 4x MLP)':    (49 * C + 8 * C * C,) * 2,
        'NAFNet block':                 (7 * C * C + 18 * C, 6 * C * C + 18 * C),  # SCA acts on a pooled vector
        'SE add-on, r = 16':            (2 * C * C // 16, C),                       # one multiply per pixel
        'ViT block, global attention':  (12 * C * C, 12 * C * C + 2 * n_tokens * C),
        'Swin block, 8 x 8 windows':    (12 * C * C, 12 * C * C + 2 * 64 * C),
        'Restormer block (MDTA+GDFN)':  (4 * C * C + 27 * C + 3 * g * C + 18 * g,
                                         4 * C * C + 27 * C + 2 * C * C // 4 + 3 * g * C + 18 * g),  # 4 heads
    }

C, side = 64, 128
for name, (p, m) in blocks(C, side * side).items():
    print(f"{name:30s} {p:7d} weights  {m:8d} MACs/pixel")

# Check the inverted residual against MobileNetV2's formula h*w*d'*t*(d' + k^2 + d'') per pixel.
t, k = 6, 3
assert blocks(C, 1)['inverted residual, t = 6'][1] == C * t * (C + k * k + C)
```

Four lessons come out of these numbers.

1. **At equal width, the blocks differ by more than 10 times in cost.** The bottleneck and the separable block are about 16 to 17 times cheaper than the basic block. Real networks therefore never compare blocks at equal width: MobileNetV2 uses an inverted residual on thin tensors, ResNet-50 uses bottlenecks on wide ones. Compare blocks at equal cost, not at equal $C$.
2. **Global attention over pixels is out of reach for restoration.** With every pixel of a $128 \times 128$ map as a token, the ViT block costs about 37 times more per pixel than a Swin block with $8 \times 8$ windows, and the gap grows with image size. This is why restoration Transformers use windows (SwinIR) or attention across channels (Restormer); see [ML 05](/en/learn/ml/vision-transformers/#cost-and-efficient-attention) for the general cost argument.
3. **Attention add-ons are cheap in MACs.** An SE gate adds $C^2/8$ weights and one multiply per channel and pixel; its cost is memory traffic, not arithmetic.
4. **MACs are not latency.** Depthwise layers do few operations per byte moved, so they can run slower than their MAC count suggests; [ML 03](/en/learn/ml/convolutional-networks/#efficient-cnns-separate-spatial-filtering-from-channel-mixing) and the [DLCV 02 efficiency section](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#efficiency) discuss this. Measure on the target hardware.

Whether a cheaper block is also a better one depends on the design space. RegNet's population study found that its best models used neither a bottleneck nor an inverted bottleneck, and that depthwise convolution did even worse than the inverted bottleneck in its setting [16], while ConvNeXt found the inverted bottleneck slightly better within its own sequence of changes [2]. Both can be true: the comparison depends on the other choices held fixed.

### Attention inside a CNN: SE and CBAM

**Plain version.** A gate looks at a summary of the whole feature map and decides which channels (or positions) to turn up or down for this particular image. It is a cheap way to give a convolutional network some content-dependent, image-wide behavior.

**Precise version.** Squeeze-and-excitation (SE) computes a channel gate $s = \sigma(W_2\,\delta(W_1\,\bar u))$ from the vector $\bar u$ of channel means, where $W_1$ and $W_2$ are the weights of a two-layer MLP that shrinks the dimension by a ratio $r$ and restores it, $\delta$ is a ReLU and $\sigma$ the sigmoid, and multiplies each channel $u_c$ of the feature map by $s_c$; the formula and code are in the [DLCV 02 note](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/notes/squeeze-and-excitation-attention-inside-a-cnn/) [17]. CBAM extends it in two ways [18]: its channel gate pools each channel by both average and maximum and passes both vectors through one shared MLP, and a second, **spatial** gate pools across channels and applies a $7 \times 7$ convolution, so the block can also turn whole regions up or down. CBAM's ablations found the sequential channel-then-spatial order better than running the two gates in parallel [18].

*Optional: [CBAM's channel and spatial gates](#cbams-channel-and-spatial-gates) (in the Supplementary notes). The two formulas with every symbol defined, and how they relate to SE and to NAFNet's simplified channel attention.*

**Where it goes.** Both SE and CBAM sit on the residual branch, before the addition: SE applies the gate to the non-identity branch "before summation with the identity branch" [17], and CBAM applies its module to the convolution outputs of each ResNet block [18]. RCAN brought channel attention into super-resolution [15]; NAFNet later simplified it to $X \cdot W\,\mathrm{pool}(X)$, a single linear map of the pooled vector with no ReLU or sigmoid, and lost nothing in its ablation [19]. The Modern view's attention survey places SE in the "channel attention" family and CBAM in "channel and spatial" [20].

### Token mixers: self-, window- and channel attention

**Plain version.** Attention mixes information between tokens with weights computed from the content, instead of fixed weights as in a convolution. The design question is **which tokens talk to which**: all of them (global), those in the same local window, or, in Restormer's twist, the channels instead of the pixels.

**Precise version.** Global multi-head self-attention over $N$ tokens of width $C$ costs about $2N^2C$ MACs for the scores and the weighted sum, on top of $4NC^2$ for the projections; window attention restricts it to $M \times M$ windows, giving $2M^2NC$ ([ML 05](/en/learn/ml/vision-transformers/#swin-and-hierarchical-transformers)) [6]. Restormer's **multi-Dconv head transposed attention** (MDTA) instead computes attention between channels [21]:

$$
\hat X = W_p\,\mathrm{Attention}(\hat Q, \hat K, \hat V) + X, \qquad
\mathrm{Attention}(\hat Q, \hat K, \hat V) = \hat V \cdot \mathrm{Softmax}\big(\hat K \cdot \hat Q / \alpha\big),
$$

where $X$ is the input feature map and $\hat X$ the output, $Q = W_d^Q W_p^Q Y$ (and likewise $K$, $V$) is computed from the layer-normalized input $Y$ by a $1 \times 1$ convolution $W_p^Q$ followed by a $3 \times 3$ depthwise convolution $W_d^Q$, the hats denote reshaped matrices $\hat Q \in \mathbb{R}^{HW \times C}$, $\hat K \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times HW}$, $\hat V \in \mathbb{R}^{HW \times C}$, $\alpha$ is a learnable temperature, and $W_p$ without a superscript is the output $1 \times 1$ convolution. The attention map $\hat K \hat Q$ has size $C \times C$ instead of $HW \times HW$, so the cost grows linearly with the number of pixels, and every output pixel still depends on the whole image through the channel statistics. The depthwise convolutions put local context back in. As in ordinary multi-head attention, the channels are split into heads with separate maps.

Restormer's feed-forward part (GDFN) is covered with the gates below. The trade-off between the mixers returns in the restoration sections: window attention mixes space strongly but locally, channel attention reaches globally at low cost, and a comparative study argued that Restormer's spatial mapping is weaker than SwinIR's for super-resolution [22].

### Normalization: BN, LN, GN, and when to leave it out

**Plain version.** A normalization layer rescales features to a standard size and then lets the network learn its preferred scale. The layers differ in **which numbers share statistics**. In classification the choice mostly affects training stability; in restoration the wrong choice can change the brightness and contrast of the output.

**Precise version.** All the common layers compute $y = \gamma (x - \mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon} + \beta$ over some set of values ([ML 02](/en/learn/ml/neural-networks/#normalization-layers) gives the template). For a mini-batch of shape $N \times C \times H \times W$:

| Layer | Statistics computed over | Depends on the batch? |
|---|---|---|
| BatchNorm (BN) [23] | $N, H, W$ for each channel | yes; running averages at test time |
| LayerNorm (LN) [24] | the features of one sample (for images, implementations differ: all of $C, H, W$, or $C$ at each position) | no |
| InstanceNorm (IN) [25] | $H, W$ for each sample and channel | no |
| GroupNorm (GN) [26] | $H, W$ and a group of channels, for each sample | no; $G = 1$ gives LN, $G = C$ gives IN |

BN is the default in classification CNNs and works well with large batches. Its known failure is small batches: GN's authors report a ResNet-50 on ImageNet with 10.6% lower error than its BN counterpart at a batch size of 2 [26]. What BN computes, and why training and inference differ, is in the [DLCV 01 note](/en/learn/deep-learning/deep-learning-for-image-processing/notes/batch-normalization-standardize-inside-the-network/).

**Why restorers dropped BN.** EDSR removed BN from its residual blocks, arguing that because BN normalizes the features it removes "range flexibility" from the network; the change improved accuracy and cut training memory by about 40% compared with SRResNet, the residual SR network of the SRGAN paper [27], [12]. ESRGAN added that BN tends to introduce artifacts when training and test statistics differ, especially in deeper networks trained with a GAN loss, and removed it as well [28]. This is not a law: DnCNN combined residual learning with BN for Gaussian denoising and reported that the two help each other [29]. The snippet shows the mechanism with a fixed, untrained residual branch $r(x)$ (convolution, normalization, ReLU, $1 \times 1$ convolution) and the global residual $y = x + r(x)$. The same mid-gray image $A$ is restored inside two different batches.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter, correlate

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def image(level, size=32):
    """A smooth synthetic image with mean brightness `level` (values roughly in [0, 1])."""
    t = gaussian_filter(rng.standard_normal((size, size)), 2)
    return level + 0.15 * t / t.std()

C = 4
W1 = rng.normal(0.1, 0.2, (C, 3, 3))           # 3x3 conv, 1 -> C channels (fixed random weights)
w2 = rng.normal(0.0, 0.05, C)                  # 1x1 conv, C -> 1 channel

def features(batch):                           # (N, H, W) -> (N, C, H, W)
    return np.stack([[correlate(x, W1[c], mode='nearest') for c in range(C)] for x in batch])

def branch(batch, norm, running=None):
    """Residual branch: conv -> normalize -> ReLU -> 1x1 conv."""
    f = features(batch)
    if norm == 'bn-train':                     # per channel, over the whole batch
        mu, var = f.mean((0, 2, 3), keepdims=True), f.var((0, 2, 3), keepdims=True)
    elif norm == 'bn-eval':                    # per channel, stored training statistics
        mu, var = running
    else:                                      # 'instance': per sample and channel
        mu, var = f.mean((2, 3), keepdims=True), f.var((2, 3), keepdims=True)
    h = np.maximum((f - mu) / np.sqrt(var + 1e-5), 0)
    return np.tensordot(w2, h, axes=(0, 1))    # (N, H, W)

train = np.stack([image(rng.uniform(0.3, 0.7)) for _ in range(64)])
f = features(train)
running = (f.mean((0, 2, 3), keepdims=True), f.var((0, 2, 3), keepdims=True))

A = image(0.5)
dark = np.stack([image(0.15) for _ in range(7)])
bright = np.stack([image(0.85) for _ in range(7)])
for norm in ['bn-train', 'bn-eval', 'instance']:
    for name, others in [('dark', dark), ('bright', bright)]:
        batch = np.concatenate([A[None], others])
        out = A + branch(batch, norm, running)[0]          # global residual: y = x + r(x)
        print(f"{norm:8s}  A with 7 {name:6s} images: output mean {out.mean():.3f}")

# Per-sample normalization forgets each image's level and contrast:
B = 0.5 * A + 0.3
r_A, r_B = branch(A[None], 'instance')[0], branch(B[None], 'instance')[0]
print("instance-norm branch, A vs 0.5*A + 0.3: max |r_A - r_B| =", np.abs(r_A - r_B).max().round(4))
print("outputs with the global residual: means", (A + r_A).mean().round(3), (B + r_B).mean().round(3))
```

In training mode, BN gives the same image (input mean 0.492) an output brightness of 0.629 or 0.482 depending only on its batch companions (Figure 3). In evaluation mode the batch no longer matters, but the stored statistics come from other images, so the network behaves differently at test time from how it was trained: the mismatch EDSR and ESRGAN describe. Per-sample normalization is batch-independent but blind in the other direction: the branch gives the same output for $A$ and for $0.5A + 0.3$ (up to $\epsilon$), so only the identity path carries the image's own level and contrast. That is why restorers that do normalize (NAFNet, Restormer and SwinIR use LayerNorm) keep it **inside** residual branches with a global residual [19], [21], [4]. The weights are random and untrained; training can partly compensate, but the batch dependence is structural.

### Activations and gates

**Plain version.** A nonlinearity is what lets stacked layers do more than one linear map. The classic choice applies a fixed function to every number (ReLU, GELU). A gate instead **multiplies** two learned features, so one feature decides how much of the other passes. Modern restoration blocks use gates and, in NAFNet's case, nothing else.

**Precise version.** ReLU, GELU and the SwiGLU feed-forward block [33] are defined in [ML 02](/en/learn/ml/neural-networks/#swiglu-a-gated-feed-forward-block). The gated linear unit (GLU) of Dauphin et al. computes $f(X) \odot \sigma(g(X))$ with two linear maps $f$, $g$ and a sigmoid $\sigma$ [34]. NAFNet's **SimpleGate** drops the sigmoid and the second map [19]:

$$
\mathrm{SimpleGate}(X) = X_1 \odot X_2, \qquad X = [X_1;\, X_2],
$$

where $X$ is a feature map with $2C$ channels, $X_1$ and $X_2$ are its first and second $C$ channels, and $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication. The product of two linear functions of the input is quadratic, so the block is nonlinear without any activation function; the authors note that GELU itself, $x\,\Phi(x)$ with $\Phi$ the standard normal CDF [35], is a special case of a gate. Restormer's GDFN uses $\phi(W_d^1 W_p^1\,\mathrm{LN}(X)) \odot W_d^2 W_p^2\,\mathrm{LN}(X)$ with $\phi$ the GELU, two paths of $1 \times 1$ and $3 \times 3$ depthwise convolutions, and a channel expansion $\gamma = 2.66$ [21]. In NAFNet's ablation, replacing GELU by SimpleGate gave +0.08 dB on SIDD and +0.41 dB on GoPro [19].

### Upsampling: transposed convolution, pixel shuffle and resize-convolution

**Plain version.** Upsampling makes a feature map larger. There are three common ways: let each input pixel "paint" a learned patch into the output (transposed convolution), compute several values per input pixel and rearrange them (pixel shuffle), or enlarge first and convolve afterwards (resize-convolution). The first two can easily paint a regular checkerboard pattern; the third cannot.

**Precise version.** The three methods and the exact pixel-shuffle index rule are in [DLCV 09](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-restoration/#how-a-network-gets-bigger-three-upsampling-strategies) and [DLCV 09A](/en/learn/deep-learning/super-resolution/#decision-2-how-to-upsample-transposed-or-sub-pixel-convolution) [36]. What matters for composition is a counting argument. With stride $s$, the output pixels fall into $s^2$ **phases** (their row and column positions modulo $s$). In a transposed convolution, the output at a given phase receives only some of the kernel's taps. Its value on a flat input equals the sum of those taps, the **phase sum**. If the phase sums differ, a flat input comes out as a periodic pattern. One cause, which Odena et al. call **uneven overlap**, occurs when the kernel size is not divisible by the stride: the phases then receive different numbers of taps [37]. Even with divisible sizes, random weights give unequal phase sums, so a freshly initialized layer still makes a pattern; Aitken et al. list these two causes and strided operations in the loss as the three sources of checkerboard artifacts [38]. A sub-pixel convolution has the same freedom, since each phase has its own filter. A resize-convolution (nearest-neighbor upsampling, then an ordinary convolution) applies the same full kernel at every output pixel, so all phases see the same sum. The snippet upsamples a flat $16 \times 16$ input by 2.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import correlate

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def transposed_conv(x, w, s):
    """Each input pixel 'paints' a scaled copy of the k x k kernel w; copies are s pixels apart."""
    k, (H, W) = w.shape[0], x.shape
    out = np.zeros((s * (H - 1) + k, s * (W - 1) + k))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            out[s * i:s * i + k, s * j:s * j + k] += x[i, j] * w
    return out

def resize_conv(x, w, s):
    """Nearest-neighbour upsampling by s, then an ordinary 'same' convolution with w."""
    return correlate(np.kron(x, np.ones((s, s))), w, mode='nearest')

def subpixel_conv(x, ws, s):
    """s*s ordinary convolutions whose outputs are interleaved (pixel shuffle)."""
    out = np.zeros((s * x.shape[0], s * x.shape[1]))
    for n, w in enumerate(ws):
        out[n // s::s, n % s::s] = correlate(x, w, mode='nearest')
    return out

x = np.ones((16, 16))                          # flat input: a good upsampler returns a flat image
cases = {
    'transposed k=3, s=2, uniform w': transposed_conv(x, np.ones((3, 3)), 2)[4:-4, 4:-4],
    'transposed k=4, s=2, uniform w': transposed_conv(x, np.ones((4, 4)), 2)[4:-4, 4:-4],
    'transposed k=4, s=2, random w ': transposed_conv(x, rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, (4, 4)), 2)[4:-4, 4:-4],
    'sub-pixel, 4 random 3x3 kernels': subpixel_conv(x, rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, (4, 3, 3)), 2)[4:-4, 4:-4],
    'resize + conv, random 3x3 w   ': resize_conv(x, rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, (3, 3)), 2)[4:-4, 4:-4],
}
for name, y in cases.items():
    phases = [y[a::2, b::2].mean() for a in (0, 1) for b in (0, 1)]     # the four 2x2 sub-pixel phases
    print(f"{name}: phase means {np.round(phases, 2)}  relative spread {np.ptp(y) / y.mean():.2f}")
```

```text
transposed k=3, s=2, uniform w: phase means [4. 2. 2. 1.]  relative spread 1.30
transposed k=4, s=2, uniform w: phase means [4. 4. 4. 4.]  relative spread 0.00
transposed k=4, s=2, random w : phase means [4.04 3.22 5.01 3.85]  relative spread 0.44
sub-pixel, 4 random 3x3 kernels: phase means [ 8.71 10.13  9.49  8.91]  relative spread 0.15
resize + conv, random 3x3 w   : phase means [7.86 7.86 7.86 7.86]  relative spread 0.00
```

With $k = 3$ and $s = 2$, one phase receives 4 taps, two receive 2 and one receives 1, even with uniform weights: the 4 : 2 : 2 : 1 pattern is built into the shapes. With $k = 4$ every phase receives 4 taps, so uniform weights give a flat output, but random weights do not. Only resize-convolution is flat for any weights. Figure 4 shows the same four upsamplers on a flat input and on a photograph. Training can push the phase sums together, and Odena et al. report their best results with nearest-neighbor resize-convolution [37]; for pixel shuffle, initializing all phases from one kernel removes the pattern at the start [38], as noted in [DLCV 09A](/en/learn/deep-learning/super-resolution/#decision-2-how-to-upsample-transposed-or-sub-pixel-convolution).

### Skip connections: add or concatenate

**Plain version.** A skip connection brings an earlier feature map to a later layer. It can be **added** (the two maps must have the same shape, and the result keeps the width) or **concatenated** (the maps are stacked along channels, and the next layer decides how to combine them). Concatenation is more flexible and more expensive.

**Precise version.** Concatenation followed by a $1 \times 1$ convolution contains addition as a special case: with encoder map $e$ and decoder map $d$, each with $C$ channels, the $1 \times 1$ convolution with weight matrix $[I \;\; I]$ (two $C \times C$ identity blocks side by side) maps $[e; d]$ to $e + d$. So concatenation can learn anything addition can, and more, at the cost of a wider layer and of storing both maps. The snippet checks the identity and counts the cost of the first convolution after each merge in a 4-level U-Net decoder with widths 32 to 256, on a $256 \times 256$ input.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1) Concatenation followed by a 1x1 convolution contains addition as a special case.
C, H, W = 8, 5, 5
enc, dec = rng.standard_normal((C, H, W)), rng.standard_normal((C, H, W))
cat = np.concatenate([enc, dec])                       # (2C, H, W)
W_fuse = np.hstack([np.eye(C), np.eye(C)])             # a 1x1 conv is a (C_out x C_in) matrix
print("concat + 1x1 conv [I I] == add:", np.allclose(np.tensordot(W_fuse, cat, 1), enc + dec))

# 2) What each choice costs in the decoder of a 4-level U-Net on a 256 x 256 input.
widths, size = [32, 64, 128, 256], 256
totals = {'add': [0, 0], 'concat': [0, 0], 'concat + 1x1': [0, 0]}
for level, c in enumerate(widths):
    hw = (size // 2 ** level) ** 2                     # pixels at this level
    first = {'add': 9 * c * c,                         # first 3x3 conv after the merge
             'concat': 9 * 2 * c * c,
             'concat + 1x1': 2 * c * c + 9 * c * c}    # 1x1 halves channels, then 3x3
    for k, p in first.items():
        totals[k][0] += p
        totals[k][1] += p * hw
for k, (p, m) in totals.items():
    print(f"{k:13s} merge-layer weights {p:7d}   MACs {m / 1e9:.2f} G")
```

```text
concat + 1x1 conv [I I] == add: True
add           merge-layer weights  783360   MACs 2.42 G
concat        merge-layer weights 1566720   MACs 4.83 G
concat + 1x1  merge-layer weights  957440   MACs 2.95 G
```

Concatenating doubles the merge layer's weights and MACs; a $1 \times 1$ fusion first, as Restormer does, recovers most of the saving. The choice differs across well-known designs:

- **U-Net** concatenates the (cropped) encoder map with the decoder map after a $2 \times 2$ up-convolution that halves the channels [39].
- **FPN** adds: the coarser map is upsampled by 2 with nearest-neighbor interpolation and added to a $1 \times 1$-projected backbone map, and a $3 \times 3$ convolution on each merged map reduces the aliasing of the upsampling [5] ([DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#feature-pyramids-fpn)).
- **Restormer** concatenates and then halves the channels with a $1 \times 1$ convolution at all levels except the top [21].
- **NAFNet**'s code adds the encoder map to the upsampled decoder map [14].
- **DenseNet** concatenates the outputs of all earlier layers in a block [40] ([DLCV 02 note](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/notes/two-more-cnn-designs-densenet-and-regnet/)).

### Multi-scale modules: FPN, ASPP and the U-Net

**Plain version.** Objects and degradations come at many sizes. Multi-scale modules let one network look at several scales at once: by building a pyramid of feature maps (FPN), by probing one map with filters of several sizes (ASPP), or by going down and back up (U-Net).

These are composition patterns more than blocks, and each is taught where it is used: FPN in [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/object-detection/#feature-pyramids-fpn) [5]; atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP), parallel atrous convolutions at several rates, in [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-segmentation/#multi-scale-context-aspp-and-pyramid-pooling) [41]; the U-Net in [DLCV 01](/en/learn/deep-learning/deep-learning-for-image-processing/#encoderdecoders-for-dense-prediction) and the [IP 10 paper guide](/en/learn/image-processing/segmentation/papers/u-net-a-reading-guide-to-the-paper/) [39]. One rule connects them: **context comes from going coarse, detail comes from a path that stayed fine**. FPN's lateral connections, U-Net's skips and the low-level skip of the segmentation decoders in DLCV 03B are all that second path.

## Composing for classification

**Plain version.** A classifier must answer one question about the whole image. So it shrinks the image quickly, spends its computation on small, wide feature maps where each position already sees a large region, and finally throws away the spatial grid altogether.

The principles, with the reason for each:

1. **Downsample early with a stem.** The first layers run on the most pixels, so the stem cuts the resolution by 4 before the body starts (ResNet's $7 \times 7$ stride-2 convolution plus max-pool [1]; ConvNeXt's $4 \times 4$ stride-4 patchify layer, which cost nothing in accuracy in its roadmap [2]).
2. **Halve the resolution and double the width at each stage.** This keeps the cost per layer roughly constant (see the anatomy section) and grows the receptive field geometrically.
3. **Put most blocks in the third stage.** ResNet-50 uses (3, 4, 6, 3) blocks per stage; ConvNeXt changed this to (3, 3, 9, 3), following Swin's 1:1:3:1 ratio, and gained accuracy [2]. The third stage, at output stride 16, has a large receptive field and still enough positions.
4. **Pool globally, then classify linearly.** Global average pooling makes the output invariant to where the object is and lets the network accept any input size. The ViT paper found that a class token and global average pooling work similarly well, but need different learning rates [3].
5. **Put expensive global mixing where tokens are few.** Global attention is affordable in late stages; hybrid designs put convolutions early and attention late ([ML 05](/en/learn/ml/vision-transformers/#hybrid-cnntransformer-models)). Cheap gates like SE can go in every block.
6. **Batch normalization is fine** when batches are large; use GN or LN when they are not.

**How fast does the receptive field grow?** The recurrence of [ML 03](/en/learn/ml/convolutional-networks/#receptive-field) covers strided stacks; the snippet also handles upsampling by walking back from one output unit and tracking the interval of inputs that reach it. It compares ResNet-50 with two restorers on a $256 \times 256$ input: a plain full-resolution body of the EDSR-baseline shape (16 residual blocks of two $3 \times 3$ convolutions, 64 channels, plus head and tail) and a 4-level U-Net with widths 32 to 512.

```python
def analyze(layers, H=256):
    """Exact 1-D receptive field of one output unit, plus the multiply-accumulates (MACs)
    of a square H x H input. A layer is ('conv', k, stride, c_in, c_out) or ('up', f),
    where 'up' is nearest-neighbour upsampling by f (the convolution after it pays the cost)."""
    macs, size = 0, H
    for layer in layers:                              # forward: track the map size and cost
        if layer[0] == 'conv':
            _, k, s, cin, cout = layer
            size //= s
            macs += size * size * k * k * cin * cout
        else:
            size *= layer[1]
    rf = 0
    for p0 in range(16):                              # a few output positions (alignment matters)
        lo = hi = p0
        for layer in reversed(layers):                # backward: which inputs reach unit p0?
            if layer[0] == 'conv':
                _, k, s, _, _ = layer
                pad = (k - 1) // 2
                lo, hi = s * lo - pad, s * hi - pad + k - 1
            else:
                lo, hi = lo // layer[1], hi // layer[1]
        rf = max(rf, hi - lo + 1)
    return rf, macs, size

# Classification: ResNet-50 (stride on the 3x3 conv of each first block, max-pool as a k=3, s=2 layer).
resnet = [('conv', 7, 2, 3, 64), ('conv', 3, 2, 64, 0)]        # stem conv, then max-pool (no MACs)
cin = 64
for width, blocks, stride in [(64, 3, 1), (128, 4, 2), (256, 6, 2), (512, 3, 2)]:
    for b in range(blocks):
        s = stride if b == 0 else 1
        resnet += [('conv', 1, 1, cin, width), ('conv', 3, s, width, width),
                   ('conv', 1, 1, width, 4 * width)]
        if b == 0:
            resnet += [('conv', 1, 1, cin, 4 * width)]          # projection shortcut (cost only)
        cin = 4 * width

# Restoration A: plain full-resolution body, 64 channels, 32 convs of 3x3 (EDSR-baseline-like).
plain = [('conv', 3, 1, 3, 64)] + [('conv', 3, 1, 64, 64)] * 32 + [('conv', 3, 1, 64, 3)]

# Restoration B: 4-level U-Net, 2 convs per level, widths double as resolution halves.
w = [32, 64, 128, 256, 512]
unet = [('conv', 3, 1, 3, w[0])]
for i in range(4):
    unet += [('conv', 3, 1, w[i], w[i])] * 2 + [('conv', 2, 2, w[i], w[i + 1])]
unet += [('conv', 3, 1, w[4], w[4])] * 2
for i in reversed(range(4)):
    unet += [('up', 2), ('conv', 1, 1, w[i + 1], w[i]),
             ('conv', 3, 1, 2 * w[i], w[i]), ('conv', 3, 1, w[i], w[i])]   # concat skip doubles c_in
unet += [('conv', 3, 1, w[0], 3)]

for name, net in [('ResNet-50', resnet), ('plain 64ch x 34', plain), ('U-Net 4 levels', unet)]:
    rf, macs, size = analyze(net)
    print(f"{name:16s} RF {rf:4d} px   output {size:3d}x{size:<3d}   {macs / 1e9:6.2f} GMACs")
```

```text
ResNet-50        RF  427 px   output   8x8       5.34 GMACs
plain 64ch x 34  RF   69 px   output 256x256    77.54 GMACs
U-Net 4 levels   RF  206 px   output 256x256    14.47 GMACs
```

The U-Net's skips are left out because its deepest path sets the receptive field (a brute-force check over all paths gives the same 206). Figure 5 plots the growth. ResNet-50 reaches 427 pixels for 5.3 GMACs but returns an $8 \times 8$ map; the plain restorer grows by 2 pixels per layer, 69 pixels for 77.5 GMACs; the U-Net reaches 206 pixels at full output resolution for less than a fifth of that compute. These are theoretical receptive fields; the effective one, measured by gradients, is smaller and concentrated in the center [42] ([DLCV 01](/en/learn/deep-learning/deep-learning-for-image-processing/#receptive-field)).

## Composing for restoration

**Plain version.** A restoration network returns an image of the same size (or larger, for super-resolution) that is almost the input. So it keeps the resolution, or comes back to it with skips; it predicts only the correction; it avoids layers that change the intensity range; and it buys context without paying full resolution for every layer.

The principles:

1. **Keep the resolution, or come back to it.** The comparative study behind X-Restormer sorts restoration networks into three architectures: U-shaped encoder–decoders (such as Restormer and NAFNet), plain residual-in-residual networks that keep the original size (RCAN, SwinIR) and multi-stage progressive networks, which process the image in several sub-networks one after another [22]. It describes the plain design as favorable for reconstructing high-frequency detail but expensive, and the U-shaped one as handling large inputs with a receptive field that grows quickly through downsampling [22]. Figure 1(b) and (c) show the two main shapes.
2. **Learn the residual.** The output is the input plus a predicted correction, $\hat x = y + R(y)$ for degraded input $y$ and network $R$. VDSR predicted the residual between the high-resolution image and its interpolated input [43]; DnCNN predicts the noise [29]; SwinIR uses $I_{\text{HQ}} = H(I_{\text{LQ}}) + I_{\text{LQ}}$ for tasks without upsampling [4]; Restormer outputs a residual image $R$ and returns $\hat I = I + R$ [21]; NAFNet's code adds the input to the last convolution's output [14]. The correction is small and centered on zero, so no capacity is spent copying the image.
3. **Add a long skip for low frequencies.** SwinIR sends its shallow features directly to the reconstruction module, $H_{\text{REC}}(F_0 + F_{\text{DF}})$, because shallow features mainly contain low frequencies and the deep features can then focus on the lost high frequencies [4]. EDSR and RCAN use long skips around the body or around groups [12], [15].
4. **Preserve the intensity range.** No BN in the body (EDSR, ESRGAN), normalization only inside residual branches (NAFNet, Restormer, SwinIR), as shown in the normalization section [12], [28].
5. **Match the receptive field to the degradation, and pay for it wisely.** The X-Restormer study argues that super-resolution depends mostly on local information and strong spatial mapping, that U-shaped downsampling and upsampling help denoising, and that motion deblurring and dehazing need large-range or global information [22]. The receptive-field snippet shows the price: a full-resolution body grows its field by a few pixels per layer at full cost, downsampling grows it fast and cheaply, and channel attention or state-space scans reach the whole image at linear cost [21], [44].
6. **Design the output layer for the target.** The last layer is a plain convolution (SwinIR's reconstruction for denoising, Restormer's final convolution, NAFNet's ending layer [4], [21], [14]); a ReLU or sigmoid there would clip the correction. For super-resolution, the upsampler goes at the end so the body runs at low resolution ([DLCV 09A](/en/learn/deep-learning/super-resolution/#decision-2-how-to-upsample-transposed-or-sub-pixel-convolution)).
7. **Plan for full-size test images.** Restorers train on crops and test on whole images. A U-Net needs sizes divisible by $2^{\text{levels}}$, which NAFNet's code handles by padding [14]. Global statistics, such as channel attention's pooling, cover a crop in training but the whole image at test time; NAFNet notes that this degrades performance and adopts a remedy from earlier work [19], and Restormer trains on progressively larger patches [21].

## Side by side: what changed and why

**Plain version.** The fastest way to see composition at work is to put a classification model next to the restoration model derived from it and list the differences. Each difference has a reason from the two lists above.

### ResNet versus EDSR and NAFNet

| Component | ResNet [1] | EDSR [12] | NAFNet [19], [14] | Why it changed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stem | $7 \times 7$ stride 2 + max-pool | one $3 \times 3$ conv | one $3 \times 3$ conv | keep every pixel |
| Resolution | 4 stages, output stride 32 | constant (low-resolution input size) | U-shape, 2×2 stride-2 conv down, $1 \times 1$ conv + pixel shuffle up | detail versus context |
| Block | conv-BN-ReLU-conv-BN, add, ReLU | conv-ReLU-conv, ×0.1 (large model), add | LN, $1 \times 1$, $3 \times 3$ dw, SimpleGate, SCA, $1 \times 1$, ×β, add; then a gated MLP, ×γ, add | range preservation; cheap gating |
| Normalization | BN | none | LayerNorm inside the branches | see the normalization section |
| Skips | short only | short + long skip around the body | short + add-skips between levels + global residual | low frequencies bypass the body |
| Head | global average pool + FC | upsampler (conv + pixel shuffle) + conv | $3 \times 3$ conv + input | output is an image |
| Loss | cross-entropy | L1 | PSNR loss in the ablations | regression targets |

EDSR's own table lists the step from SRResNet: 16 blocks of 64 filters for both baseline models, BN removed, L1 instead of L2, and for the full model 32 blocks of 256 filters with residual scaling 0.1, growing from 1.5M to 43M parameters [12].

### Swin versus SwinIR

| Component | Swin [6] | SwinIR [4] | Why it changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Input | $4 \times 4$ patch partition and linear embedding | $3 \times 3$ conv (shallow features) at full resolution | one token per pixel |
| Hierarchy | patch merging between 4 stages | none: 6 residual Swin Transformer blocks (RSTB) at one resolution | restoration needs every pixel |
| Window | $M = 7$ | $M = 8$ (7 for JPEG artifact removal) | JPEG [45] codes 8 × 8 blocks; the authors observed a drop with 8 |
| Block group | Swin layers | 6 Swin layers + a $3 \times 3$ conv + a residual connection per RSTB | the conv adds translation equivariance; the residual helps aggregation |
| Head | global average pool + linear | conv (+ pixel shuffle for SR) on $F_0 + F_{\text{DF}}$ | low frequencies bypass the body |
| Width | 96 channels and up | 180 channels, 6 heads | balance accuracy and size |

SwinIR's ablation reports that the residual connection inside each RSTB improves PSNR [30] by 0.16 dB, and that a $1 \times 1$ convolution there brings little improvement, possibly because it cannot extract local neighboring information as a $3 \times 3$ convolution does [4]. The JPEG window size is a good example of a composition choice that interacts with the data: the authors suggest the drop with $M = 8$ may come from JPEG's $8 \times 8$ block partitions.

### ViT versus Restormer

| Component | ViT [3] | Restormer [21] | Why it changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tokens | $16 \times 16$ patches | every pixel, after a conv embedding | no information is thrown away |
| Token mixer | global self-attention over patches, $N \times N$ map | MDTA: channel attention, $C \times C$ map, with depthwise convs | linear cost in pixels, global reach |
| Channel mixer | MLP, 4× expansion, GELU | GDFN: gated, depthwise convs, expansion 2.66 | gating and local context |
| Shape | isotropic, one resolution | 4-level U-shape, pixel-unshuffle down, pixel-shuffle up, concat + $1 \times 1$ skips, refinement stage | multi-scale at manageable cost |
| Head | class token + MLP | conv to a residual image, added to the input | output is an image |
| Training | fixed resolution | progressive patch sizes, starting at $128 \times 128$ | learn from larger regions |

Restormer's configuration uses [4, 6, 6, 8] blocks, [1, 2, 4, 8] heads and [48, 96, 192, 384] channels from level 1 to level 4, plus 4 blocks in the refinement stage [21]. Both columns use LayerNorm in pre-norm residual branches: the Transformer block itself survived; what changed is the token mixer and the macro-architecture around it.

## Exploring a design space

**Plain version.** A design question ("does block X help?") has no answer in general; it has an answer for a given set of other choices, data and budget. So change one thing at a time, keep the budget fixed, look at more than one task or seed, and when you can, compare populations of models rather than single winners.

### Reading an ablation

NAFNet's paper is a model of a readable ablation. Starting from a plain block of convolution, ReLU and shortcut in a U-Net, under a fixed budget of 16 GMACs, the authors add one change at a time and report PSNR on SIDD [31] (real-camera denoising) and GoPro [32] (motion deblurring) [19]:

| Step | SIDD (dB) | GoPro (dB) |
|---|---|---|
| PlainNet | 39.29 | 28.51 |
| + LayerNorm (and a 10× larger learning rate) | 39.73 | 31.90 |
| ReLU → GELU | 39.71 | 32.11 |
| + channel attention | 39.85 | 32.35 |
| GELU → SimpleGate | 39.93 | 32.76 |
| CA → simplified CA | 39.96 | 32.85 |

What to check when you read such a table:

- **Is the budget fixed?** Here it is (16 GMACs), so a gain is not just "more compute".
- **Are the steps cumulative?** Yes: each row includes the previous changes, so the table measures each change **given** the earlier ones, not in isolation. A different order could give different increments.
- **Do the tasks agree?** Not always: GELU is neutral on SIDD and helps on GoPro. A change that matters for one degradation may not matter for another.
- **Is the training recipe confounded with the block?** The LayerNorm row also changes the learning rate, because LayerNorm made the larger rate stable. The authors say so explicitly; many papers do not.
- **How large is the noise?** A single run per row gives no error bar. Differences of a few hundredths of a dB should be read as "no loss", which is how the paper reads the last row.

ConvNeXt's roadmap reads the same way for classification: a ResNet-50 went from 76.1% to 78.8% ImageNet top-1 with a modern training recipe alone, and then to 82.0% through a sequence of architectural changes, the largest single one adding less than the recipe [2]. That is why architectures trained with different recipes cannot be compared ([DLCV 01A](/en/learn/deep-learning/training-practice/#baselines-ablations-and-budgets)).

### Comparing populations: design spaces

RegNet replaces "which single model is best?" with "which **design space** produces good models more often?" [16]. A design space is a set of models defined by a few parameters (number of blocks per stage, widths, bottleneck ratio, group width). The tool is the **error empirical distribution function**:

$$
F(e) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}[e_i \lt e],
$$

where $e_i$ is the error of the $i$-th of $n$ models sampled from the space and trained, $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is 1 when its argument is true and 0 otherwise, and $F(e)$ is the fraction of models with error below $e$. A space whose curve lies higher is better as a whole, a far more robust comparison than two hand-tuned models. RegNet sampled $n = 500$ models at 360 to 400 million FLOPs, trained each for 10 epochs, and kept constraints that did not hurt the curve (shared bottleneck ratio and group width) or improved it (widths and depths increasing across stages) [16]. The best models had about 20 blocks across compute regimes, used no bottleneck, and benefited from SE [16].

### A practical protocol

1. Fix the budget (MACs, parameters, memory or measured latency) and the training recipe before comparing blocks.
2. Change one thing at a time, and keep a log of every run, including the ones that did not help.
3. Evaluate on more than one task, dataset or seed; report the spread.
4. Confirm at the target model size; a block that helps a tiny model may not help a large one.
5. For architecture searches, think in terms of the three dimensions of the NAS survey: the search space, the search strategy and how performance is estimated [46]. Cheap estimates such as short training (RegNet's 10 epochs) are fine for ranking populations but should be confirmed with full training.

## Common composition mistakes

**Plain version.** Most architecture bugs are not wrong blocks; they are right blocks in the wrong place. The list gives the symptom, the cause and the fix.

- **BN in a restoration body.** Symptom: batch-dependent brightness or color shifts, or test-time artifacts. Fix: remove BN, or normalize per sample inside residual branches with a global residual.
- **Small per-device batches with BN** (detection, segmentation, high resolution). Fix: GroupNorm, or freeze the pretrained BN statistics ([DLCV 01A](/en/learn/deep-learning/training-practice/#initialization-and-normalization-in-practice)).
- **Transposed convolution with a kernel size not divisible by the stride.** Symptom: checkerboard texture. Fix: resize-convolution, or pixel shuffle with a matched initialization.
- **No global residual in a restorer.** The network must learn to copy the image before it can correct it. Fix: predict the correction.
- **A ReLU or sigmoid at the output.** Corrections cannot be negative, or highlights saturate. Fix: a linear last layer; clip only for display and evaluation.
- **A receptive field smaller than the degradation.** Symptom: large blur or haze remains. Fix: downsampling levels, dilation, or global mixing; compute the receptive field first.
- **Downsampling too early in a restorer**, or a classification backbone used unchanged: fine detail is lost. Fix: a full-resolution path (skips, a refinement stage).
- **Global attention at full resolution** runs out of memory. Use windows, channel attention or downsampling.
- **Input sizes not divisible by $2^{\text{levels}}$ in a U-Net**, or odd sizes with concatenated skips. Symptom: shape errors or misaligned skips. Fix: pad and crop, as NAFNet's code does [14].
- **Counting parameters as speed.** Depthwise and concatenation-heavy designs can be slow despite few parameters or MACs. Measure latency and memory.
- **Comparing designs trained with different recipes or budgets**: the recipe alone can be worth more than any single block [2].
- **Ignoring interactions with the data**, such as SwinIR's window size and JPEG's blocks [4].

## Modern view

### What the surveys say

**Khan et al.** [47] review CNN architectures and sort their innovations into seven categories: spatial exploitation, depth, multi-path, width, feature-map exploitation, channel boosting and attention. The taxonomy is useful here because most categories are composition choices (depth, width, multi-path connections) rather than new layer types.

**Guo et al.** [20] survey attention in computer vision and classify it by what it selects: channel attention (what to attend, e.g. SE), spatial attention (where, including self-attention), temporal attention (when), branch attention (which branch), and the two hybrids channel-and-spatial (e.g. CBAM) and spatial-and-temporal. Their framing of attention as "dynamic weight adjustment" based on the input matches the gate view of this chapter.

**Khan et al.** [48] review Transformers in vision, from the basics of self-attention and large-scale pretraining to applications in recognition, detection, segmentation, generation, video and low-level vision, compare the advantages and limitations of popular designs, and end with open research directions.

**Elsken, Metzen and Hutter** [46] organize neural architecture search along three dimensions: the search space, the search strategy and the performance-estimation strategy. Their survey is the natural next step after the manual design-space exploration of this chapter.

**Chen et al.** [22] is a comparative study rather than a survey: it benchmarks five restoration backbones, among them SwinIR, Restormer and NAFNet, on five tasks, classifies them by architecture (U-shaped, plain residual-in-residual, multi-stage) and core operator (convolution, spatial self-attention, transposed self-attention), and finds that networks strong on one task often fall short on another.

### The state of the art, 2023–2026

- **Convolutions and attention converge on one block shape.** ConvNeXt V2 adds a Global Response Normalization layer and masked-autoencoder pretraining to ConvNeXt [49]; the MetaFormer view treats the token mixer as one interchangeable part [8].
- **Pretraining matters as much as the block.** Battle of the Backbones compared many pretrained backbones across tasks in more than 1,500 training runs and found that convolutional networks pretrained with supervision on large datasets still performed best on most of the tasks it considered, while self-supervised backbones were competitive in matched comparisons [50].
- **Restoration mixes operators.** HAT combines channel attention with window self-attention and adds overlapping cross-attention between windows [51]; X-Restormer replaces half of Restormer's channel-attention blocks with spatial attention blocks to improve its spatial mapping [22]; MambaIR and MambaIRv2 replace attention with state-space scans that reach the whole image at linear cost, adding local enhancement and channel attention, and in v2 non-causal "attentive" modeling [44], [52].

### Open problems

1. **General restoration backbones.** No single composition is best on all degradations; the comparative study frames task generality as the open target [22].
2. **Fair comparisons.** Architecture, recipe and pretraining are entangled, and population-level studies like RegNet's need hundreds of training runs [16], which is costly for high-resolution restoration models.
3. **Receptive field in practice.** Theoretical fields are easy to compute; how much context a trained restorer actually uses is harder to measure, and attribution analyses suggest it can be less than the design allows [51], [42].
4. **Cost models.** MACs, memory traffic and latency disagree, especially for depthwise convolutions, gating and attention; design rules still depend on hardware.
5. **Train–test shift inside the architecture.** Global statistics (pooling, normalization, attention) behave differently on crops and full images, and the remedies in use change the training or test procedure rather than the architecture [19], [21].

## Key takeaways

- A network is a stem, stages of repeated blocks, downsampling layers and a head; most design decisions are about where the resolution changes and where the skips go, not about inventing blocks.
- Every block mixes space and channels at some cost; count weights and MACs per pixel, compare blocks at equal cost, and measure latency separately.
- Classifiers shrink the resolution early, double the width as they halve the map, put most blocks in the third stage and pool globally; their receptive field grows geometrically and cheaply.
- Restorers keep or recover full resolution, learn a residual, send low frequencies through long skips, avoid BN in the body, and end with a linear layer.
- Batch normalization makes a restored image depend on its batch; per-sample normalization removes that dependence but also hides the image's own level, so it belongs inside residual branches.
- Transposed convolutions and untrained sub-pixel layers make checkerboards when their phase sums differ; resize-convolution cannot.
- Concatenation plus a $1 \times 1$ convolution contains addition; it costs about twice as much at the merge.
- Read ablations for a fixed budget, cumulative steps, agreement across tasks and confounded recipes; when possible, compare populations of models, not single winners.

## Supplementary notes

### CBAM's channel and spatial gates

This note writes out the two gates of CBAM and compares them with the other channel gates of the chapter.

For a feature map $F \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$, CBAM computes [18]

$$
M_c(F) = \sigma\big(\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{AvgPool}(F)) + \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{MaxPool}(F))\big), \qquad
M_s(F') = \sigma\big(f^{7 \times 7}([\mathrm{AvgPool}_c(F');\, \mathrm{MaxPool}_c(F')])\big),
$$

where $M_c(F) \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times 1 \times 1}$ is the channel gate, $\mathrm{AvgPool}$ and $\mathrm{MaxPool}$ reduce each channel to one number, the MLP has one hidden layer of $C/r$ units with a ReLU (reduction ratio $r$) and is shared between the two pooled vectors, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid, $F' = M_c(F) \odot F$ is the channel-refined map, $\mathrm{AvgPool}_c$ and $\mathrm{MaxPool}_c$ pool **along the channel axis** to give two $H \times W$ maps, $[\cdot\,;\cdot]$ concatenates them, $f^{7 \times 7}$ is a $7 \times 7$ convolution with one output channel, $M_s(F') \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times H \times W}$ is the spatial gate, and the output is $M_s(F') \odot F'$. The product $\odot$ broadcasts each gate over the axes it lacks: the channel gate over all positions, the spatial gate over all channels.

**Relation to the other gates.** SE is the channel gate with average pooling only [17]. RCAN's channel attention is the SE form applied inside each residual block of a super-resolution network [15]. NAFNet's simplified channel attention keeps the pooling and a single linear map but drops the MLP's ReLU and the sigmoid, $\mathrm{SCA}(X) = X * W\,\mathrm{pool}(X)$, where $*$ is channel-wise multiplication [19]. All of them are multiplicative gates computed from global statistics, which is why NAFNet reads channel attention as a special case of a gated linear unit [19].

**Pitfalls.** The pooled statistics are computed over the whole map, so they differ between a training crop and a full test image; this is one form of the train–test shift discussed in the restoration principles. The spatial gate's $7 \times 7$ kernel sees only a local window of the pooled maps, so it is a local, not a global, spatial attention.

### Phase sums of a transposed convolution in 1-D

This note derives how many kernel taps each output position of a transposed convolution receives, which is the arithmetic behind the checkerboard snippet.

**Setup.** A 1-D transposed convolution with stride $s$ and kernel $w_0, \dots, w_{k-1}$ maps input $x_i$ to

$$
y_n = \sum_{i} x_i \, w_{n - s i}, \qquad 0 \le n - s i \lt k,
$$

where $n$ is the output position and the sum runs over the inputs $i$ whose kernel copy covers $n$. Write $n = s m + p$ with phase $p \in \{0, \dots, s - 1\}$. The kernel index $n - s i = p + s(m - i)$ takes the values $p, p + s, p + 2s, \dots$ that are less than $k$.

**Taps per phase.** Phase $p$ therefore receives the taps $w_p, w_{p+s}, w_{p+2s}, \dots$, and their number is

$$
T_p = \left\lceil \frac{k - p}{s} \right\rceil,
$$

for $0 \le p \lt s$, away from the borders. For $k = 3$, $s = 2$: $T_0 = 2$, $T_1 = 1$. In 2-D the counts multiply, giving the $4 : 2 : 2 : 1$ pattern of the snippet. All $T_p$ are equal exactly when $s$ divides $k$; this is Odena et al.'s condition for even overlap [37].

**Phase sums.** On a flat input $x_i = 1$, the output at phase $p$ is the phase sum $S_p = \sum_{t \ge 0,\; p + s t \lt k} w_{p + s t}$. A flat output requires $S_0 = S_1 = \dots = S_{s-1}$. Equal tap counts make this possible but do not enforce it: random weights break it, which is the "random initialization" source of artifacts described by Aitken et al. [38].

**Resize-convolution.** Nearest-neighbor upsampling by $s$ followed by a convolution with kernel $v$ of length $r$ is itself a transposed convolution with stride $s$ whose kernel is $v$ convolved with a box of $s$ ones. Each of its phase sums is $\sum_t v_t$, the same for every phase, so a flat input always gives a flat output, whatever $v$ is. That is the precise sense in which resize-convolution is "biased against" checkerboards.

### Receptive fields through upsampling and skips

This note explains the backward interval method used by the receptive-field snippet, and when it is exact.

**The question.** For one output unit at position $p$ of the last layer, which input positions can change its value? For a chain of layers, answer it backward: start with the interval $[p, p]$ at the output and map it through each layer, from the last to the first, to the interval of positions in that layer's input that feed it.

**Convolution.** A layer with kernel size $k$, stride $s$ and padding $q = \lfloor (k - 1)/2 \rfloor$ computes output unit $u$ from inputs $s u - q, \dots, s u - q + k - 1$. An interval $[\ell, h]$ of outputs therefore depends on the inputs

$$
[\,s\ell - q,\;\; s h - q + k - 1\,],
$$

where $\ell$ and $h$ are the first and last output positions and the new endpoints are the first input of unit $\ell$ and the last input of unit $h$. Positions outside the image stand for padding; they do not limit the interval, so the result is the theoretical receptive field away from borders.

**Nearest-neighbor upsampling by $f$.** Output unit $u$ copies input unit $\lfloor u / f \rfloor$, so $[\ell, h]$ maps to $[\lfloor \ell / f \rfloor, \lfloor h / f \rfloor]$. Because of the floor, the size of the result depends on where $\ell$ falls relative to the blocks of $f$ copies: two neighboring output units can have receptive fields that differ by up to one input unit of that layer, which can be many pixels deep in a network. That is why the snippet takes the maximum over 16 consecutive output positions, a full period of a 4-level U-Net.

**Skips.** At a merge (addition or concatenation) the input interval is the union of the intervals through the two branches. In a U-Net, the branch through the deeper level passes through the same encoder output and then through more layers; with nearest-neighbor upsampling and a stride-2 downsampling whose window contains its own position, the deeper branch's interval contains the skip branch's interval. So the union equals the deepest path, and the snippet can list that path alone. The brute-force check mentioned in the main text built the U-Net in 1-D with positive weights, pushed a unit impulse from every nearby input position through it, and recorded which ones reached a given output; it found the same 206 pixels.

**Edge cases.** Dilated convolutions make the dependence set a lattice with holes, not an interval, so the interval method reports the span but not the gaps (see the gridding note in [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-segmentation/notes/gridding-in-stacked-dilated-convolutions/)). Attention layers with global reach make the receptive field the whole input after one layer; window attention lets each token see its own $M \times M$ window, so one layer adds at most $M - 1$ tokens of reach, and the shifted windows of the next layer carry it across window borders. Araujo, Norris and Sim give closed-form expressions for receptive field size, stride and offset in general networks, including how to handle multiple paths [53].

### The ConvNeXt roadmap step by step

This note lists the steps of ConvNeXt's "modernization" of ResNet-50 with the ImageNet top-1 accuracy the paper reports after each one [2].

| Step | Change | Top-1 (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | ResNet-50 with its original training | 76.1 |
| 1 | modern training recipe (300 epochs, AdamW, heavy augmentation) | 78.8 |
| 2 | stage compute ratio (3, 3, 9, 3) | 79.4 |
| 3 | patchify stem, $4 \times 4$ stride 4 | 79.5 |
| 4 | depthwise (grouped) convolution, width raised to 96 | 80.5 |
| 5 | inverted bottleneck | 80.6 |
| 6 | depthwise convolution moved up | 79.9 |
| 7 | kernel size $7 \times 7$ | 80.6 |
| 8 | ReLU replaced by GELU | 80.6 |
| 9 | fewer activations | 81.3 |
| 10 | fewer normalization layers | 81.4 |
| 11 | LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm | 81.5 |
| 12 | separate $2 \times 2$ stride-2 downsampling layers, with normalization where resolution changes | 82.0 |

**How to read it.** The steps are cumulative, so each number is conditional on everything above it. Step 6 lowers accuracy on purpose: it prepares step 7, and the two must be judged together. Steps 8 and 11 change accuracy little by themselves; the paper adopts them anyway, so the final design contains choices that were neutral when they were made. Several steps also change the compute: step 4 raises it, and steps 5 and 6 lower it, so the paper tracks FLOPs next to accuracy. The roadmap is a single path through a large design space, which the authors acknowledge for the stage ratio: a better distribution of computation "is likely to exist" [2].

### RegNet's quantized linear widths

This note writes out how RegNet turns three numbers into a network's stage widths and depths.

RegNet first describes per-block widths with a line [16]:

$$
u_j = w_0 + w_a \cdot j, \qquad 0 \le j \lt d,
$$

where $d$ is the network depth in blocks, $j$ the block index, $w_0 \gt 0$ the initial width, $w_a \gt 0$ the slope, and $u_j$ the continuous width of block $j$. To get a few stages of constant width, it introduces a multiplier $w_m \gt 0$ and writes each $u_j$ as a power of it:

$$
u_j = w_0 \cdot w_m^{s_j}, \qquad w_j = w_0 \cdot w_m^{\lfloor s_j \rceil},
$$

where $s_j$ is a real exponent, $\lfloor s_j \rceil$ rounds it to the nearest integer, and $w_j$ is the quantized width. Blocks with the same rounded exponent form one stage: stage $i$ has width $w_0 \cdot w_m^{i}$ and $d_i = \sum_j \mathbf{1}[\lfloor s_j \rceil = i]$ blocks. A ratio $w_m = 2$ gives the classic doubling of width from stage to stage; RegNet found that good models had $w_m$ near 2.5 [16]. When it studies four-stage networks, the paper ignores parameter combinations that give a different number of stages [16]; the snippet shows only the three equations, with illustrative values that are not one of the paper's models.

```python
import numpy as np

def regnet_stages(d, w0, wa, wm):
    u = w0 + wa * np.arange(d)                     # continuous widths, one per block
    s = np.log(u / w0) / np.log(wm)                # exponents with u = w0 * wm**s
    q = np.round(s).astype(int)                    # quantized exponents
    widths = w0 * wm ** q
    return [(int(w0 * wm ** i), int((q == i).sum())) for i in np.unique(q)], widths

stages, widths = regnet_stages(d=16, w0=48, wa=36, wm=2.5)
print("stage (width, blocks):", stages)
print("block widths:", widths.astype(int).tolist())
```

```text
stage (width, blocks): [(48, 1), (120, 3), (300, 8), (750, 4)]
block widths: [48, 120, 120, 120, 300, 300, 300, 300, 300, 300, 300, 300, 750, 750, 750, 750]
```

Two things to notice. Depth per stage comes out of the line, not from a separate choice: a steeper slope moves blocks into later, wider stages. And the first stage can end up with a single block, which is one of the parameter combinations a practical generator would filter or adjust.

## Paper guides

These guides are optional: each walks through one paper the chapter builds on — the problem it poses, the idea, what it showed and what to check when you read it.

### Simple Baselines for Image Restoration (NAFNet)

#### Challenge

Chen et al. [19] ask whether increasingly elaborate restoration blocks are necessary. They keep a plain single-stage U-Net and build a competitive block through controlled additions, then remove components that can be replaced by simpler gates. The result is a study of macro-architecture versus block design, not a claim that all nonlinear computation is unnecessary.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperNafnetSource} alt="Compare the baseline and NAF blocks from left to right: multiplication replaces GELU, while simplified channel attention retains an input-dependent gate." caption="Original paper figure 3 — Compare the baseline and NAF blocks from left to right: multiplication replaces GELU, while simplified channel attention retains an input-dependent gate." />

*Figure source: [19], PDF page 6. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04676).*

**Establish a useful baseline before simplifying.** Starting from a convolutional network, the paper adds layer normalization, changes ReLU to GELU and adds channel attention. The stable baseline provides a reference against which a simplification can be evaluated. The normalization experiment also changes the learning rate, which matters when attributing its gain.

**Replace a named activation with multiplication.** After pointwise expansion and depthwise convolution, split an even-channel feature tensor into equal halves $U,V$. SimpleGate is

$$
\operatorname{SG}([U,V])=U\odot V.
$$

$[U,V]$ denotes channel concatenation and $\odot$ elementwise multiplication. The output has half the input channels. This operation is nonlinear: scaling both branches by $a$ scales their product by $a^2$. “Nonlinear Activation Free” refers to removing conventional activation functions such as GELU, not to turning the network into a linear filter.

**Simplify channel attention too.** For gated feature map $F$, simplified channel attention has the form

$$
\operatorname{SCA}(F)=F\odot W\,\operatorname{GAP}(F).
$$

$\operatorname{GAP}$ averages spatial positions, $W$ is a learned channel projection, and the resulting channel vector broadcasts over space. Removing the original attention bottleneck/activation retains a data-dependent multiplicative channel gate.

A NAF block uses normalization, expansion, depthwise filtering, SimpleGate, SCA and projection in one residual path. A second normalized feed-forward path uses another SimpleGate and residual addition. Learnable residual scales stabilize the initial mapping. The encoder–decoder carries information across resolutions; the simple block is not a substitute for those skip and scale connections.

**Read the figure and choose a baseline.** Follow a feature tensor through the block: normalization prepares the input, spatial convolution mixes nearby evidence, and SimpleGate multiplies two channel groups before channel reweighting and the residual addition. The multiplication makes the mapping nonlinear even without a named activation. To judge a replacement block, keep the restoration task, width, training recipe and inference conversion fixed. Otherwise, an improvement cannot be assigned to removing activations. Use this as a compact learned baseline when paired training data are available; it does not estimate a physical blur kernel.

#### Results

The full denoising comparison in Table 6 reports **40.30 dB / 0.962 SSIM** on SIDD with **65G MACs**. The stronger baseline has the same displayed quality but 84G MACs. These full-model values should be kept separate from smaller ablation configurations.

For deblurring, Table 4's final crop-training/test-time configuration reaches **33.69 dB / 0.967 SSIM** on GoPro. Test-time local conversion is part of that setting; the block alone does not account for every improvement.

#### Ablations

Table 1 builds the baseline cumulatively. PlainNet at learning rate $10^{-4}$ gives GoPro PSNR **28.51**; the unstable $10^{-3}$ run fails, while normalization permits the larger learning rate and reaches **31.90**. That comparison changes both stability and optimization.

Table 2 gives baseline GoPro **32.35 dB**, replacing GELU with SimpleGate **32.76**, replacing attention with SCA **32.54**, and both replacements **32.85**. On SIDD the corresponding values are **39.85, 39.93, 39.95 and 39.96**. Tasks differ in how much each change helps.

Table 5 reintroduces activations inside the gate. Some slightly improve SIDD while lowering GoPro performance, supporting a useful simple design without proving that one activation choice dominates all tasks. Table 3's depth study also shows that more blocks increase latency and do not monotonically improve every metric.

#### Conclusion

NAFNet demonstrates that a carefully controlled plain architecture with multiplicative gates can match more elaborate restoration designs. Its argument depends on a strong baseline, explicit ablations and the distinction between named activation functions and nonlinear feature interactions.

#### Problems

The tested simplicity does not remove the need for training data, memory or global pooling. Crop-trained pooling statistics can differ at full-image inference, motivating the evaluated local conversion. A result on paired denoising/deblurring does not establish robustness to arbitrary real degradations, and equal operation counts need not imply equal device latency.

### Designing Network Design Spaces (RegNet)

#### Challenge

Radosavovic et al. [16] ask how to make an entire family of networks easy to describe and likely to perform well. Searching for one winning model does not reveal whether nearby designs are reliable. RegNet therefore studies distributions of sampled architectures, rather than treating one selected benchmark winner as the whole argument.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperRegnetSource} alt="The left diagram defines a network family through stages and blocks; the right diagrams expose the grouped residual block whose widths and groups parameterize that family." caption="Original paper figure 3–4 — The left diagram defines a network family through stages and blocks; the right diagrams expose the grouped residual block whose widths and groups parameterize that family." />

*Figure source: [16], Published paper PDF, PDF page 4. [Original PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2020/papers/Radosavovic_Designing_Network_Design_Spaces_CVPR_2020_paper.pdf).*

**Describe the space before searching it.** AnyNet uses a stem, repeated residual stages and a classification head. Stage depth, width, bottleneck ratio and group width define architectural choices. The paper samples models under comparable complexity/training settings and inspects their error distribution. An empirical cumulative distribution function answers what fraction of sampled models achieve error below a chosen threshold; it does not report only the best sample.

**Remove freedoms that evidence does not justify.** Successive spaces share bottleneck/group choices and favor stage widths that increase with depth. The useful pattern is a simple increasing per-block width sequence, quantized into repeated stage widths. In a simplified account of the parameterization,

$$
u_j=w_0+w_a j,\quad q_j=\operatorname{round}\!\left(\log(u_j/w_0)/\log w_m\right),\quad
w_j=8\operatorname{round}\!\left(w_0w_m^{q_j}/8\right).
$$

$j=0,\ldots,d-1$ indexes blocks, $d$ is depth, $w_0$ the initial width, $w_a$ its linear increment, $w_m>1$ a quantization multiplier, and $w_j$ the resulting channel count. Equal consecutive widths form stages. Valid group-convolution configurations require additional width/group compatibility checks. The formula alone does not guarantee exactly four stages.

RegNetX uses the residual grouped-convolution block; RegNetY adds squeeze-and-excitation. These are concrete model families generated from the space, not a claim that quantization itself learns the best architecture from images.

#### Results

The paper compares ImageNet error, complexity and actual GPU inference time across budgets. Its main result is that a compact, structured space can yield competitive models across those budgets. An attractive FLOP count does not by itself establish low latency: the timing comparisons include the effects of width, depth, activation sizes and device execution.

#### Ablations

The AnyNet-to-RegNet sequence compares sampled distributions as constraints are introduced. A distribution shifting toward lower error is stronger evidence for a useful space than one lucky winning model, but does not mean every sample improves. The per-stage width analyses motivate the quantized linear form; the bottleneck and group studies test which freedoms can be shared or restricted.

Read the sampling and training budget alongside these plots. Restricting the space changes which models can be sampled, so this is a design-space study rather than an isolated layer replacement in one otherwise fixed network.

#### Conclusion

RegNet's contribution is a small, interpretable generator of useful network families. It connects architecture design to distributional evidence and separates model complexity from measured hardware speed.

#### Problems

The design rules were established under particular classification data, blocks and training schedules. They are not universal laws for restoration or other dense tasks. Searching and validating the space still costs compute, and stronger training recipes can alter rankings. A deployment should measure its own resolution, batch size and device rather than inherit a latency claim from a different setup.

### A Comparative Study of Image Restoration Networks for General Backbone Network Design (X-Restormer)

#### Challenge

Chen et al. [22] ask whether a backbone that excels on one restoration task is also a good general design. Super-resolution, denoising, deblurring, deraining and dehazing need different combinations of fine spatial mapping and broad context. Comparing isolated best scores from unrelated papers would mix architecture with training choices.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperXrestormerSource} alt="Compare which dimensions convolution, spatial attention and transposed attention mix; the operators exchange different kinds of spatial and channel evidence." caption="Original paper figure 3 — Compare which dimensions convolution, spatial attention and transposed attention mix; the operators exchange different kinds of spatial and channel evidence." />

*Figure source: [22], Published paper PDF, PDF page 6. [Original PDF](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2024/papers_ECCV/papers/08961.pdf).*

**Build a controlled benchmark.** The study compares five representative networks under common per-task training/evaluation settings. It separates macro-architecture—plain residual, U-shaped or multi-stage—from core operators such as convolution, spatial attention and transposed channel attention. Networks are trained for each task; this is not one jointly trained all-in-one checkpoint.

**Track where evidence can move.** A convolution combines nearby positions with learned shared kernels. Spatial attention makes position-to-position interactions content-dependent. Transposed attention summarizes spatial evidence into channel relationships, then mixes feature channels. These mechanisms have different costs and do not become equivalent merely because all can have large receptive fields.

**Combine complementary attention.** X-Restormer retains the four-level U-shaped design and replaces half the transposed-attention blocks with spatial-attention blocks. The two kinds alternate, each with normalization, a residual attention update and a residual feed-forward update. Transposed attention uses Restormer's MDTA; spatial processing uses overlapping cross-attention, where a window's queries access a larger overlapping key/value region. It is not unrestricted full-image pixel attention.

The replacement aims to improve spatial mapping while retaining multi-scale context. Because this replaces blocks rather than simply appending a second backbone, parameter growth is controlled. Parameter counts still do not fully describe activation memory or spatial-attention cost.

#### Results

Table 2 compares the common-setting benchmark. SwinIR is strong in super-resolution but less competitive in deblurring/dehazing; Restormer is comparatively balanced and ranks in the top two across the five evaluated tasks, while leaving room to improve super-resolution. The X-Restormer evaluations test whether adding the missing spatial interaction improves that balance.

For super-resolution, U-shaped models operate on an already enlarged input, unlike models that do most feature processing at low resolution. This affects cost and behavior even under a common output scale. “Same task” therefore does not imply identical internal image sizes.

#### Ablations

The operator comparisons and local-attribution visualizations support the authors' explanation that different tasks benefit from different spatial/contextual processing. They are evidence for a hypothesis about architecture behavior, not direct measurements of a uniquely defined “spatial mapping ability.”

Read the proposed-backbone comparisons together with the controlled benchmark. Separately check the training schedule, upsampling convention, parameter budget and inference complexity. A replacement that improves several task scores is useful evidence, but does not isolate every property of overlapping windows from every property of the surrounding network.

#### Conclusion

The study offers both a comparison protocol and a concrete response to its findings: alternate channel and spatial attention inside a multi-scale restoration network. The lesson is to choose complementary operations for the degradation, rather than assume a classification backbone transfers unchanged.

#### Problems

Five tasks and their selected datasets do not span all real degradations. Mechanistic explanations based on attribution and rankings remain partial, and different optimization recipes can change conclusions. Overlapping windows have finite support and nonzero cost. General-backbone performance should also be kept distinct from robustness to unknown mixtures or from a single shared-model result.

### RepVGG: training with branches, deploying a plain network

RepVGG [54] separates the network that is convenient to train from the network that is convenient to run. Its contribution is an exact conversion of trained blocks, rather than a claim that one convolution can reproduce arbitrary nonlinear branches.

#### Challenge

A deployment engine can execute a sequence of ordinary convolutions efficiently, yet that simple inference graph is not necessarily the easiest graph to optimize during training. A residual branch supplies an additional route for information and gradients; it also requires intermediate activations and an addition when the model runs. RepVGG asks whether those training benefits can be retained without retaining the branches at inference.

The paper targets throughput on GPUs and possible specialized hardware. It emphasizes that operation counts alone do not describe memory access, kernel support or parallel execution. Its argument is therefore about a measured deployment trade-off, not about making every model as small as possible. In particular, it does not establish that a dense convolutional network is preferable on every mobile processor.

#### Method

<Figure src={paperRepvggSource} alt="Read structural branch merging on the left and kernel/bias construction on the right; the equivalent deployed convolution depends on folding fixed normalization statistics." caption="Original paper figure 4 — Read structural branch merging on the left and kernel/bias construction on the right; the equivalent deployed convolution depends on folding fixed normalization statistics." />

*Figure source: [54], Published paper PDF, PDF page 5. [Original PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2021/papers/Ding_RepVGG_Making_VGG-Style_ConvNets_Great_Again_CVPR_2021_paper.pdf).*

**The training block.** A block adds three branches: a $3\times3$ convolution followed by batch normalization (BN), a $1\times1$ convolution followed by BN, and an identity followed by BN. One ReLU follows their **sum**. The identity branch exists only when the spatial stride is one and the input and output channel counts agree. Downsampling blocks use the two convolutional branches. The convolutions use compatible stride, padding and grouping so that their outputs can be added.

That placement of the nonlinearity is essential. BN with frozen inference statistics is an affine transform, so all three branches can become affine convolutions. A ReLU inside each branch would prevent their replacement by one linear convolution followed by one ReLU. During training, BN uses batch statistics; the conversion is not an equivalence between the training dynamics of a branched block and those of a plain convolution.

**Fold each convolution–BN pair.** Write a branch's convolution as $z_o=W_o*x+b_o$, where $x$ is the input, $o$ indexes output channels, $W_o$ is that output channel's kernel, $*$ denotes convolution and $b_o$ is its optional bias. Let $\mu_o$ and $v_o$ be BN's stored mean and variance, $\gamma_o$ and $\beta_o$ its learned scale and shift, and $\epsilon$ its stability constant. Evaluation-mode BN is absorbed into

$$
s_o=\frac{\gamma_o}{\sqrt{v_o+\epsilon}},\qquad
\widehat W_o=s_oW_o,\qquad
\widehat b_o=\beta_o+s_o(b_o-\mu_o).
$$

Here $s_o$ scales every weight of output channel $o$; $\widehat W_o$ and $\widehat b_o$ are the converted kernel and bias. A convolution without a bias has $b_o=0$. These are stored-statistic calculations, with no calibration batch needed for the algebra itself.

**Put the branches in the same kernel space.** Place the folded $1\times1$ kernel at the centre of a zero-filled $3\times3$ kernel. For a dense identity branch, construct a kernel with a one at the centre for matching input/output channels and zeros elsewhere, then fold its BN with the same formula. Grouped blocks require the corresponding identity within each group; the identity branch cannot mix groups. The paper uses the same group count in the two convolutional branches.

If $P(\cdot)$ centre-pads a $1\times1$ kernel, $\widehat W_3$ and $\widehat W_1$ are the folded convolution kernels, and $\widehat W_I$ is the folded identity kernel, the deployed parameters are

$$
W_{\rm deploy}=\widehat W_3+P(\widehat W_1)+\widehat W_I,\qquad
b_{\rm deploy}=\widehat b_3+\widehat b_1+\widehat b_I.
$$

A missing identity contributes zero kernel and bias. $W_{\rm deploy}$ and $b_{\rm deploy}$ define one $3\times3$ convolution; the original post-sum ReLU remains after it. This conversion removes the branches and their BN operators from the inference graph. It is not an approximation or a further learning stage. Floating-point evaluation can differ slightly because the order of arithmetic changes.

The [step-by-step folding note](/en/learn/deep-learning/low-light-enhancement/notes/structural-re-parameterization-step-by-step/) already checks a complete three-branch block. This smaller check isolates a detail that is easy to get wrong: an identity **with BN** becomes a channel-scaled centre kernel **and a bias**, not just an identity kernel.

```python
import numpy as np
from numpy.lib.stride_tricks import sliding_window_view

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
x = rng.normal(size=(1, 2, 5, 5))  # batch, channels, height, width
gamma = np.array([1.2, -0.7])
beta = np.array([0.1, 0.3])
mean = np.array([0.2, -0.4])
variance = np.array([0.5, 1.3])
scale = gamma / np.sqrt(variance + 1e-5)
kernel = np.zeros((2, 2, 3, 3))  # output, input, kernel height/width
kernel[np.arange(2), np.arange(2), 1, 1] = scale
bias = beta - scale * mean
windows = sliding_window_view(
    np.pad(x, ((0, 0), (0, 0), (1, 1), (1, 1))), (3, 3), axis=(2, 3)
)
fused = np.einsum("nchwkl,ockl->nohw", windows, kernel) + bias[None, :, None, None]
branch = (
    gamma[None, :, None, None] * (x - mean[None, :, None, None])
    / np.sqrt(variance[None, :, None, None] + 1e-5)
    + beta[None, :, None, None]
)
print(f"max error: {np.max(np.abs(fused - branch)):.3e}")
assert np.allclose(fused, branch, rtol=1e-12, atol=1e-12)
```

The tested output is `max error: 4.441e-16`. This is an arithmetic check with synthetic inputs, not a trained-model accuracy experiment.

**The whole architecture.** The body has five stages, each beginning with a stride-two convolution; global average pooling and a classifier follow it. Table 2 gives stage block counts of $(1,2,4,14,1)$ for the A family and $(1,4,6,16,1)$ for B. For width multipliers $a$ and $b$, the stage widths are $\min(64,64a)$, $64a$, $128a$, $256a$ and $512b$. The first stage is capped rather than widened to avoid expensive processing of large feature maps. A separate multiplier for the final stage permits a wider low-resolution representation.

Table 3 sets $(a,b)$ to $(0.75,2.5)$, $(1,2.5)$ and $(1.5,2.75)$ for A0/A1/A2, and $(1,2.5)$, $(2,4)$, $(2.5,5)$ and $(3,5)$ for B0/B1/B2/B3. The g2/g4 variants use two/four groups in selected alternating convolution layers, with dense convolutions between them to allow inter-channel exchange. They are deployment variants with a different accuracy/cost balance, not a different folding principle.

**Training and timing.** The simple ImageNet recipe uses random crops and horizontal flips, SGD, a global batch of 256, initial learning rate 0.1, cosine decay over 120 epochs, momentum 0.9 and weight decay $10^{-4}$ on convolution/classifier kernels. The stronger 200-epoch recipe adds five warmup epochs, AutoAugment [59], label smoothing and mixup [60]. Throughput uses one GTX 1080 Ti, FP32 and batch size 128, after 50 warmup batches followed by 50 timed batches. Baseline convolution–BN sequences are fused too. These settings matter when interpreting the results.

#### Results

Tables 4 and 5 report the following ImageNet top-1 accuracy and throughput. The rows retain their training recipe; a higher score after a stronger recipe is not an architecture-only improvement.

| Model | Training epochs | Top-1 (%) | Images/s |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RepVGG-A0 | 120 | 72.41 | 3256 |
| ResNet-18 | 120 | 71.16 | 2442 |
| RepVGG-A2 | 120 | 76.48 | 1322 |
| ResNet-50 | 120 | 76.31 | 719 |
| RepVGG-B1g4 | 120 | 77.58 | 868 |
| ResNet-101 | 120 | 77.21 | 430 |
| RepVGG-B3 | 200 | 80.52 | 363 |
| RegNetX-12GF | 200 | 80.55 | 277 |

All rows shown use $224\times224$ ImageNet inputs. Those are throughput measurements under the paper's batch-128 protocol, not batch-one latency or a guarantee for another device. In the Cityscapes [58] validation experiment, Table 8 reports a PSPNet [57] with RepVGG-B1g2-fast at 78.88% mIoU and 10.9 images/s, versus the ResNet-50 backbone at 77.17% and 10.4 images/s. This timing instead uses FP32, batch 16 and $713\times713$ inputs on the same GPU. The “fast” variant limits dilation to the final five layers; the comparison therefore includes a concrete dilation choice, rather than showing that all deployment operators have equal efficiency.

#### Ablations

**Branch removal, Table 6.** With the same final B0 inference structure, training without either auxiliary branch reaches 72.39% top-1. Adding only identity–BN gives 74.79%; adding only $1\times1$–BN gives 73.15%; using both gives 75.14%. The full block runs at 1061 images/s before conversion, versus the deployed B0's 1817 in Table 4. Thus the extra training graph helps optimization, and removing it after training recovers throughput; the final architecture alone does not explain the accuracy difference.

**Placement and nonlinearities, Table 7.** Removing BN from the identity branch gives 74.18%; moving BN after the branch sum gives 73.52%, against 75.14% for the proposed block. Adding a ReLU inside each branch reaches 75.69%, but destroys the one-convolution conversion. This is an instructive trade-off: an accuracy gain is not a valid replacement when it breaks the deployment constraint.

**Alternative parameterizations, Table 7 and Section 4.2.** The paper's B0 comparisons report 73.97% for DiracNet-style parameterization [55], 73.51% for directly adding an identity kernel, 73.58% for the asymmetric convolution block [56] and 74.56% for reorganizing the layers into residual blocks. These comparisons concern the variants trained in this experiment; they do not rank every possible training recipe for those methods. Replacing ResNet-50 convolutions with RepVGG blocks gives 76.34%, versus the 76.31% baseline. The nearly unchanged result is evidence against treating the block as a universal accuracy upgrade for an already residual network.

#### Conclusion

The useful design pattern is to choose a trainable graph whose linear inference branches can be converted exactly into a simpler graph. RepVGG demonstrates that pattern for plain convolutional classifiers and segmentation backbones. The proof of equivalence, the accuracy comparison and the hardware timing answer three different questions; a sound deployment decision needs all three.

#### Problems

The paper explicitly prioritizes GPU throughput over the smallest parameter count. Its results do not settle low-power or batch-one deployment, and the training branches still require memory and computation. Folding relies on frozen, meaningful BN statistics; it does not repair a poorly calibrated model. Subsequent quantization can introduce rounding error, so floating-point folding equivalence is not a proof of integer-inference equivalence. See [DLCV 01C](/en/learn/deep-learning/model-compression/) for the separate quantization and deployment questions, and [DLCV 03A](/en/learn/deep-learning/real-time-detection/) for detector designs where training and inference structures can differ. Neither the paper's accuracy nor its images/s should be carried into those tasks without a new evaluation.
