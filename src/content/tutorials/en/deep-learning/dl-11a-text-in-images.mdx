## What you'll learn

- How a text-reading system is organized — **detection** (where is the text?), **recognition** (what does it say?) and **layout and understanding** (how do the pieces fit together?) — and which errors start at each stage.
- Why text needs its own detectors: quadrilateral regression (EAST), segmentation with shrunk text kernels (PSENet) and **differentiable binarization** (DBNet), with the formulas checked in NumPy.
- How a cropped word becomes a character string: CRNN with **CTC** decoding, attention decoders with rectification, transformer recognizers such as TrOCR, and the help and harm of a language model.
- How document models use layout: LayoutLM's 2-D position embeddings, key information extraction, and **OCR-free** models (Donut, Pix2Struct, vision-language models) that write the answer directly, sometimes including text that is not there.
- How OCR is measured — **CER and WER** from edit distance, detection **precision, recall and H-mean at IoU 0.5**, end-to-end and document metrics — on which benchmarks, and which failure modes the numbers hide.

## The big picture

Text is everywhere in images: street signs, product labels, license plates, receipts, forms, slides and screenshots. Reading it turns pixels into a string that a program can search, translate, check or store. This is **optical character recognition (OCR)**. The hard version, text in ordinary photos, is called **scene text** reading; the structured version, scans and PDFs of pages, is **document** analysis.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Think of how you read a poster on a wall. First your eyes find the patches that have writing on them (that is **detection**). Then you look closely at one patch and sound out the letters, using what words usually look like to guess a smudged one (that is **recognition**). Finally you work out what the poster means: the big line at the top is the title, the small line at the bottom is the date, and the two columns are read one after the other (that is **layout and understanding**). A computer does the same three jobs, and it can make a mistake at each one.
</Callout>

Why a separate chapter? A word is long, thin, often rotated or curved, and packed close to its neighbors; its "class" is not a label from a fixed list but any string over an alphabet that may hold tens of thousands of symbols; and one wrong character can change the meaning ("1046" versus "1O46" on an invoice). The field therefore has its own detectors, sequence recognizers, metrics and benchmarks. It is now being reshaped by models that read a whole page and write structured text directly, which makes the pipeline optional but adds a new risk: fluent output that the image does not support. ## The reading pipeline and where it breaks

**Plain version.** A classical OCR system is a chain: find text regions, read each one, then order the readings and attach meaning. Every link passes its mistakes to the next.

### Scene text versus document text

The two settings differ in what is hard.

| | Scene text | Document text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | photos, video, street views | scans, PDFs, screenshots, phone photos of pages |
| Amount | a few words per image | hundreds to thousands of words per page |
| Geometry | any orientation, perspective, curves | mostly horizontal lines; columns, tables |
| Appearance | decorative fonts, clutter, glare, blur | small print, noise, stamps, handwriting |
| Main difficulty | finding and reading each word | reading order, structure and meaning |

Benchmarks follow the split. The Robust Reading competitions held with ICDAR (the International Conference on Document Analysis and Recognition) defined many of the standard scene-text benchmarks: ICDAR 2013 with focused, mostly horizontal English text [5], and ICDAR 2015's *Incidental Scene Text* challenge [6], recorded with wearable cameras (Google Glass), where words are often small, blurred and rotated [7]. Document benchmarks ask for layout regions, form fields or answers about a page ([Document understanding](#document-understanding)).

### Four places where errors start

Errors are easier to fix when you know which stage produced them.

1. **Image.** Blur, glare, low resolution and compression remove the evidence. No later stage can recover a character that is not in the pixels; a generative model that "recovers" it is guessing (see [Failure modes](#failure-modes)).
2. **Detection.** A missed word is lost for good; two words merged into one box are read as one string; a box that cuts off the last letter produces a deletion.
3. **Recognition.** Look-alike glyphs (0/O, 1/l/I, rn/m), unseen fonts and scripts, and vertical text cause substitutions.
4. **Layout and understanding.** Correct words in the wrong order, a table read across instead of down, or a value attached to the wrong key give a wrong answer from perfect characters.

A useful habit: evaluate each stage on its own (recognition on ground-truth crops, for example) before the whole chain, so you know which stage to improve.

## Before deep learning: thresholds and connected components

**Plain version.** On a clean page, text is just dark ink on light paper. Choose a gray level that separates the two, glue nearby ink pixels into blobs, and draw a box around each blob. This works surprisingly often, and seeing where it fails explains what learned detectors had to fix.

The classical recipe has three steps. **Binarization** labels each pixel as ink or paper by comparing it with a threshold. A single global threshold, for example the one chosen by Otsu's method [8] (derived in [IP 10](/en/learn/image-processing/dip-10-segmentation/#otsus-optimum-global-thresholding)), fails under uneven lighting, so document systems use a local threshold computed in a window around each pixel. Sauvola's method [9], designed for document images, is a common choice:

$$
T(x, y) = m(x, y)\left[1 + k\left(\frac{s(x, y)}{R} - 1\right)\right],
$$

where $T(x, y)$ is the threshold at pixel $(x, y)$, $m(x, y)$ and $s(x, y)$ are the mean and standard deviation of the gray levels in a $w \times w$ window centered on the pixel, $R$ is the dynamic range of the standard deviation, and $k \gt 0$ (scikit-image's default is 0.2) controls how much local contrast lowers the threshold. A pixel darker than $T$ is ink. **Grouping** then joins ink pixels into **connected components** ([IP 09](/en/learn/image-processing/dip-09-morphology/#extraction-of-connected-components)), usually after a horizontal dilation that bridges the gaps between the letters of a word. **Box extraction** takes the bounding box of each component.

The snippet renders a small invoice with matplotlib, darkens its left side, adds noise, and runs this recipe with a global and a local threshold. It scores the boxes with the precision, recall and H-mean used by text benchmarks (defined precisely in [Measuring OCR](#measuring-ocr)): a predicted box counts as correct if its IoU with a still-unmatched ground-truth word is at least 0.5.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import ndimage as ndi
from skimage.filters import threshold_otsu, threshold_sauvola

H, W = 200, 560
WORDS = [("INVOICE", 20, 18, 22), ("No.", 200, 24, 14), ("1046", 245, 24, 14),
         ("Date:", 330, 24, 14), ("2026-11-01", 385, 24, 14),
         ("Item", 20, 80, 13), ("Qty", 260, 80, 13), ("Price", 400, 80, 13),
         ("Paper", 20, 110, 13), ("12", 265, 110, 13), ("3.50", 405, 110, 13),
         ("Total", 20, 160, 15), ("42.00", 395, 160, 15)]     # (word, x, y, font size)

def render(words):
    """Draw words with matplotlib; return a gray image in [0, 1] and word boxes."""
    fig = plt.figure(figsize=(W / 100, H / 100), dpi=100)
    texts = [fig.text(x / W, 1 - y / H, s, fontsize=fs, va="top") for s, x, y, fs in words]
    fig.canvas.draw()
    img = np.asarray(fig.canvas.buffer_rgba())[..., :3].mean(axis=2) / 255.0
    r = fig.canvas.get_renderer()
    boxes = [[b.x0, H - b.y1, b.x1, H - b.y0]
             for b in (t.get_window_extent(r) for t in texts)]
    plt.close(fig)
    return img, np.array(boxes)

def iou(a, b):
    w = min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0]); h = min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1])
    inter = max(w, 0) * max(h, 0)
    area = lambda r: (r[2] - r[0]) * (r[3] - r[1])
    return inter / (area(a) + area(b) - inter)

def detect(img, thresh, gap=9):
    ink = img < thresh                                   # dark pixels = ink
    ink = ndi.binary_opening(ink, np.ones((2, 2)))       # drop isolated noise pixels
    words = ndi.binary_dilation(ink, np.ones((3, gap)))  # bridge gaps between letters
    lab, _ = ndi.label(words)
    out = []
    for y, x in ndi.find_objects(lab):
        if (y.stop - y.start) * (x.stop - x.start) > 40:   # ignore tiny blobs
            out.append([x.start, y.start, x.stop, y.stop])
    return np.array(out, float)

def h_mean(pred, gt, thr=0.5):
    used, tp = set(), 0
    for p in pred:                                       # greedy one-to-one matching
        cand = [(iou(p, g), j) for j, g in enumerate(gt) if j not in used]
        best = max(cand, default=(0, -1))
        if best[0] >= thr:
            used.add(best[1]); tp += 1
    P, R = tp / max(len(pred), 1), tp / len(gt)
    return P, R, 2 * P * R / max(P + R, 1e-9)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
clean, gt = render(WORDS)
shade = 0.45 + 0.55 * np.linspace(0, 1, W)[None, :]      # light falls off to the left
img = np.clip(clean * shade + rng.normal(0, 0.04, clean.shape), 0, 1)

t_sau = threshold_sauvola(img, window_size=31)
for name, t, gap in [("Otsu, gap 9", threshold_otsu(img), 9), ("Sauvola, gap 3", t_sau, 3),
                     ("Sauvola, gap 9", t_sau, 9), ("Sauvola, gap 25", t_sau, 25)]:
    pred = detect(img, t, gap)
    P, R, F = h_mean(pred, gt)
    print(f"{name:16s} boxes={len(pred):2d}  P={P:.2f}  R={R:.2f}  H-mean={F:.2f}")
```

```text
Otsu, gap 9      boxes= 8  P=0.62  R=0.38  H-mean=0.48
Sauvola, gap 3   boxes=33  P=0.15  R=0.38  H-mean=0.22
Sauvola, gap 9   boxes=13  P=1.00  R=1.00  H-mean=1.00
Sauvola, gap 25  boxes=11  P=0.64  R=0.54  H-mean=0.58
```

The local threshold recovers every word, but only for one hand-chosen gap: a narrow dilation splits words into letters, a wide one merges "No." with "1046" and "Date:" with the date. All numbers here come from this synthetic toy.

This experiment is the whole motivation for learned text detectors. Real images have every font size at once, text on textured backgrounds, rotation, and words closer together than letters inside other words. A network can learn *where text is* from context and appearance, and it can learn to keep neighbors apart.

## Text detection

**Plain version.** A text detector is an object detector with one class, "text", and special care for three things: long thin shapes, any orientation (even curves), and words that nearly touch. Modern detectors either regress the corners of each word from every pixel, or segment a text mask and then split it into words.

### Why text is not just another object

The detectors of [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/) predict upright (axis-aligned) boxes. For text this breaks in three ways.

- **Extreme aspect ratios.** A text line can be many times wider than it is tall, far outside anchor shapes tuned for ordinary objects.
- **Orientation and curvature.** Words on signs and packages are rotated, seen in perspective, or follow a curve. Benchmarks therefore annotate quadrilaterals (ICDAR 2015 [6]) or polygons (Total-Text [10], CTW1500 [11]), and compute IoU between polygons instead of rectangles.
- **Dense neighbors.** Words on a line are a few pixels apart; merging them, or letting [NMS](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#non-maximum-suppression-nms-and-soft-nms) delete one, loses recall.

The first two problems combine badly. The snippet computes the IoU between a long thin word and the tightest upright box around it, as the word rotates.

```python
import numpy as np
from skimage.draw import polygon

def rotated_word(cx, cy, w, h, deg):
    """Corners of a w x h word box rotated by deg around its center (x, y)."""
    t = np.deg2rad(deg)
    R = np.array([[np.cos(t), -np.sin(t)], [np.sin(t), np.cos(t)]])
    corners = np.array([[-w, -h], [w, -h], [w, h], [-w, h]]) / 2
    return corners @ R.T + [cx, cy]

def mask(quad, shape=(300, 300)):
    m = np.zeros(shape, bool)
    rr, cc = polygon(quad[:, 1], quad[:, 0], shape)
    m[rr, cc] = True
    return m

for deg in [0, 10, 20, 30, 45]:
    quad = rotated_word(150, 150, 160, 24, deg)          # a long, thin word
    x0, y0 = quad.min(axis=0); x1, y1 = quad.max(axis=0)
    aabb = np.array([[x0, y0], [x1, y0], [x1, y1], [x0, y1]])  # tightest upright box
    a, b = mask(quad), mask(aabb)
    print(f"angle {deg:2d} deg: IoU(word, its upright box) = {(a & b).sum() / (a | b).sum():.2f}")
```

```text
angle  0 deg: IoU(word, its upright box) = 1.00
angle 10 deg: IoU(word, its upright box) = 0.47
angle 20 deg: IoU(word, its upright box) = 0.31
angle 30 deg: IoU(word, its upright box) = 0.25
angle 45 deg: IoU(word, its upright box) = 0.22
```

For a $w \times h$ rectangle rotated by $\theta$ the exact value is $wh / \big[(w\cos\theta + h\sin\theta)(w\sin\theta + h\cos\theta)\big]$, where $w$ and $h$ are the word's width and height and the denominator is the area of its upright bounding box. Already at 10 degrees the *best possible* upright box fails the usual IoU 0.5 test (Figure 3). Text detectors therefore output rotated rectangles, quadrilaterals or polygons; how IoU is computed for rotated shapes is the same polygon-clipping problem solved for bird's-eye-view boxes in [DLCV 09](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-09-3d-object-detection/#bev-iou-and-3d-iou-of-rotated-boxes).

Right: IoU between the word and its upright box against rotation angle; the curve drops from 1 at 0 degrees below the dashed 0.5 line before 10 degrees, reaches about 0.23 at 45 degrees, and rises back to 1 at 90 degrees; orange dots mark the rasterized values from the snippet." caption="Figure 3 — Why text detectors predict quadrilaterals. ### Regression-based detectors: EAST

**Plain version.** Let every pixel inside a word vote for the word's shape: "I am text, and the word I belong to has its edges this far from me, at this angle." Keep the confident votes and merge the ones that agree.

EAST (Efficient and Accurate Scene Text detector) [12] is a fully convolutional network with a U-shaped feature-merging branch that outputs, at each location of a downsampled map, a **score** in $[0, 1]$ (is this pixel inside a shrunk version of a word?) and a **geometry**. Two geometries were proposed. **RBOX** has five channels: the distances from the pixel to the four sides of a rotated rectangle and the rectangle's rotation angle. **QUAD** has eight channels: the offsets $(\Delta x_i, \Delta y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, 4$, from the pixel to the four corners of a quadrilateral. Because tens of thousands of pixels vote, EAST first merges neighboring geometries row by row, averaging them with their scores as weights (**locality-aware NMS**), and only then runs standard NMS [12]. One network pass replaces the candidate-grouping steps of earlier systems. Its weakness is reach: a pixel must predict edges that may be hundreds of pixels away, and a quadrilateral cannot follow a curved word.

### Segmentation-based detectors: PSENet and DBNet

**Plain version.** Instead of asking each pixel for the whole word's shape, ask only "is this pixel text?" That gives a mask that can follow any curve. The difficulty is splitting a mask into words when the words touch, so these detectors predict a *thinner* core for each word, keep the cores apart, and grow them back afterwards.

The network is an encoder–decoder like those of [DLCV 03B](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03b-image-segmentation/#encoderdecoders-and-skip-connections), predicting a per-pixel text **probability map** $P$; the trick is in the labels. Each ground-truth polygon $G$ with area $A$ and perimeter $L$ is shrunk inward by an offset

$$
D = \frac{A\,(1 - r^2)}{L},
$$

where $r \in (0, 1)$ is a shrink ratio; the shrunk polygon (the word's **kernel**) is the positive region. Kernels of adjacent words are separated by a margin even when the words touch, so connected components of the predicted kernel map are word instances; each is then dilated back to full size.

**PSENet** (Progressive Scale Expansion Network) [13] predicts several kernels per word at different shrink ratios and grows the smallest, well-separated ones into the larger ones with a breadth-first search, so a pixel claimed by one word cannot be taken by its neighbor. The price is a slow, sequential post-process.

**DBNet** [14] keeps a single kernel and makes the binarization step itself trainable. A standard binarization $B = \mathbf 1[P \ge t]$ with a fixed threshold $t$ has zero gradient almost everywhere, so it cannot be trained. DBNet predicts a second map, a **threshold map** $T$, and uses the approximate step

$$
\hat B_{i,j} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-k\,(P_{i,j} - T_{i,j})}},
$$

where $\hat B_{i,j}$ is the approximate binary value at pixel $(i, j)$, $P_{i,j}$ the predicted text probability, $T_{i,j}$ the learned threshold and $k$ an amplifying factor set to 50 [14]. Writing $x = P_{i,j} - T_{i,j}$ and $f(x) = \hat B_{i,j}$, the binary cross-entropy for a text pixel, $\ell_+ = -\log f(x)$, has derivative $\partial \ell_+ / \partial x = -k\,(1 - f(x))$, and for a background pixel, $\ell_- = -\log(1 - f(x))$, $\partial \ell_- / \partial x = k\,f(x)$. The gradient is scaled by $k$ and is largest where a pixel is on the wrong side of its threshold. The threshold map is supervised to be high in a band around each word's border, so the binarization is strict where words meet and relaxed inside them.

The snippet checks the derivative numerically and shows, on a 1-D profile, a threshold that is high between two words separating them where a fixed threshold merges them.

```python
import numpy as np

def db(P, T, k=50.0):
    """Differentiable binarization: an approximate step at the learned threshold T."""
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-k * (P - T)))

x = np.array([-0.10, -0.02, 0.0, 0.02, 0.10])            # x = P - T at five pixels
for k in [1.0, 50.0]:
    f = db(x, 0.0, k)
    g_pos = -k * (1 - f)       # d/dx of -log f       (pixel labeled text)
    g_neg = k * f              # d/dx of -log(1 - f)  (pixel labeled background)
    # check the text-pixel gradient by finite differences
    eps = 1e-6
    num = (-np.log(db(x + eps, 0, k)) + np.log(db(x - eps, 0, k))) / (2 * eps)
    assert np.allclose(num, g_pos, rtol=1e-4)
    print(f"k={k:4.0f}  B_hat={np.round(f, 3)}  dl+/dx={np.round(g_pos, 2)}  dl-/dx={np.round(g_neg, 2)}")

# Two words whose probability map P does not drop to 0 between them.
P = np.array([0.1, 0.9, 0.95, 0.9, 0.55, 0.5, 0.55, 0.9, 0.95, 0.9, 0.1])
T = np.array([0.3, 0.3, 0.3, 0.3, 0.7, 0.7, 0.7, 0.3, 0.3, 0.3, 0.3])   # high at the gap
print("fixed t=0.3:", (P >= 0.3).astype(int))
print("DB, k=50   :", (db(P, T) > 0.5).astype(int))
```

k=  50  B_hat=[0.007 0.269 0.5   0.731 0.993]  dl+/dx=[-49.67 -36.55 -25.   -13.45  -0.33]  dl-/dx=[ 0.33 13.45 25.   36.55 49.67]

With $k = 1$ the "binary" map is a gray blur near 0.5 and every pixel gets the same small gradient; with $k = 50$ it is nearly binary, and a text pixel 0.1 below its threshold gets a gradient of about $-50$ while a correct one gets almost none. Figure 4 repeats the separation in 2-D.

At inference DBNet drops the threshold branch: it binarizes $P$ at a constant 0.2, takes connected components, and expands each region by $D' = A' r' / L'$, with $A'$ and $L'$ the region's area and perimeter and $r' = 1.5$ [14]. The threshold map is thus mainly a training device that makes $P$ itself sharp, and the simple post-processing keeps the detector fast.

### Character-level and curved-text detectors

Some detectors find letters first and glue neighbors into words. CRAFT [15] predicts a **region score** (center of a character) and an **affinity score** (between two characters of one word) and links characters into words of any shape; because real datasets label only words, it estimates character labels on real images with an interim model. Polygon detectors such as the one introduced with CTW1500 [11] instead regress many boundary points along a curved line. Richer shape models follow irregular text better but need more complex outputs or post-processing.

## Text recognition

**Plain version.** Recognition takes one cropped word or line and outputs its characters. The modern approach reads the crop like a sequence: slide along it from left to right, describe each thin slice, and turn the sequence of slices into a sequence of characters, without ever cutting the word into separate letters.

### Recognition as sequence prediction

Early systems cut a word into characters and classified each, which fails when letters touch or break. **CRNN** (Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network) [16] made recognition segmentation-free and end-to-end trainable. A crop is resized to a fixed height; a CNN turns it into a feature map whose columns form a **feature sequence**, each column describing a thin vertical strip of the image; a deep bidirectional LSTM (see [ML 03a](/en/learn/ml/ml-03a-recurrent-recursive-networks/#deep-bidirectional-and-streaming-rnns)) adds left and right context; and a transcription layer turns per-column predictions into a label sequence. With rectangular pooling windows, a 100 × 32 input yields a sequence of 25 frames, longer than most English words [16]. Nothing tells the network which frame belongs to which character; CTC handles that missing alignment.

### CTC: training without alignments

**Plain version.** At every frame the network may say a character or "nothing new" (a **blank**). To read the output, merge repeated characters and delete the blanks. To train, add up the probability of every frame-by-frame story that reads out as the correct word.

**Connectionist temporal classification** (CTC) [17] defines, for per-frame probabilities $q_t(s)$ of each symbol $s$ (a character of the alphabet or the blank $\varnothing$) at frame $t$, a collapsing map $\mathcal B$ (merge repeats, then remove blanks) and the probability of a label sequence $y$ given the input $x$,

$$
p(y \mid x) = \sum_{z:\ \mathcal B(z) = y}\ \prod_{t=1}^{T} q_t(z_t),
$$

where $z = (z_1, \dots, z_T)$ is an alignment (one symbol per frame), $T$ the number of frames, and the sum runs over every alignment that collapses to $y$. Training minimizes $-\log p(y \mid x)$, computed by a forward recursion in $O(TL)$ for a label of length $L$. The full derivation, with a brute-force check, is in [PR 06's CTC note](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/pr-06-templates-structured-models/notes/#connectionist-temporal-classification-ctc); here we only need what it implies for decoding.

The cheapest decoder is **greedy** (best-path) decoding: take the most likely symbol at each frame and collapse. CRNN uses exactly this approximation in its lexicon-free mode [16]. But the most likely *path* need not collapse to the most likely *text*, because a text's probability is spread over many paths. The snippet builds a five-frame toy where this matters.

```python
import itertools
import numpy as np

alphabet = ["-", "c", "a", "t"]                 # index 0 is the CTC blank
# Per-frame probabilities from a recognizer (rows: 5 frames, columns: symbols).
probs = np.array([[0.10, 0.80, 0.05, 0.05],
                  [0.20, 0.05, 0.70, 0.05],
                  [0.60, 0.05, 0.30, 0.05],
                  [0.55, 0.02, 0.03, 0.40],
                  [0.55, 0.02, 0.03, 0.40]])

def collapse(path):
    """CTC map B: merge repeated symbols, then remove blanks."""
    merged = [s for i, s in enumerate(path) if i == 0 or s != path[i - 1]]
    return "".join(alphabet[s] for s in merged if s != 0)

greedy = probs.argmax(axis=1)                   # best symbol at every frame
print("greedy path:", "".join(alphabet[s] for s in greedy), "->", repr(collapse(greedy)))

# Exact: add up every alignment that collapses to the same text (4**5 = 1024 paths).
total = {}
for path in itertools.product(range(4), repeat=len(probs)):
    p = np.prod(probs[np.arange(len(probs)), path])
    total[collapse(path)] = total.get(collapse(path), 0.0) + p
for text, p in sorted(total.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:3]:
    print(f"p({text!r}) = {p:.3f}")
print("collapse('caa-at') =", repr(collapse([1, 2, 2, 0, 2, 3])),
      " collapse('caaat') =", repr(collapse([1, 2, 2, 2, 3])))
```

```text
greedy path: ca--- -> 'ca'
p('cat') = 0.363
p('ca') = 0.179
p('ct') = 0.091
collapse('caa-at') = 'caat'  collapse('caaat') = 'cat'
```

The "t" is never the single most likely symbol in any frame, so greedy decoding drops it, yet the alignments that contain a "t" in frame 4 or 5 add up to twice the probability of "ca" (Figure 5). The last line shows the blank's second job: a doubled letter survives only with a blank between its copies. Real decoders use **prefix beam search**, which keeps the best few partial transcriptions and merges alignments that collapse to the same prefix.

CTC has two built-in limits (see PR 06's note): frame outputs are independent given the image, so the model cannot condition on characters it has already emitted, and the alignment is monotonic, so reading order must match frame order. Vertical or curved text breaks the second unless it is rectified first.

### Attention decoders and rectification

**Plain version.** An attention decoder writes the word one character at a time. Before each character it looks back at the image features and chooses where to look; it also remembers what it has already written, so it behaves like a small language model of spelling.

An **attention decoder** is the encoder–decoder of [NLP 07](/en/learn/nlp/nlp-07-attention-transformers/#7-self-attention-and-cross-attention) applied to image features: at step $i$ it attends to the feature sequence and predicts character $y_i$ from the result and from $y_1, \dots, y_{i-1}$, until an end token. It drops CTC's independence assumption at the price of sequential decoding. **ASTER** [18] pairs such a decoder with a **rectification network**: a thin-plate spline (TPS) transformation, predicted from the image and trained only through the recognition loss, warps a curved or perspective word into a straight horizontal one before reading it.

Baek et al. [7] showed that most recognizers of the time fit one four-stage template — **transformation** (none or TPS), **feature extraction**, **sequence modeling** (none or BiLSTM) and **prediction** (CTC or attention) — and, retraining all combinations on the same data, that attention mainly costs speed and that earlier comparisons had been distorted by different training sets and test subsets.

### Transformer recognizers

**TrOCR** [19] replaces both the CNN and the RNN with pre-trained transformers. The image is resized to 384 × 384 and split into 16 × 16 patches, as in a Vision Transformer ([ML 04](/en/learn/ml/ml-04-vision-transformers/#the-vision-transformer-vit)); an image encoder initialized from DeiT or BEiT encodes the patches, and a text decoder initialized from RoBERTa or MiniLM generates **wordpieces** (subword tokens) autoregressively, decoded with beam search [19]. It is pre-trained on synthetic text images and fine-tuned on printed, handwritten and scene text: recognition now borrows its backbones and decoder from general vision and language pre-training.

**PARSeq** [20] trains one transformer decoder on many random orders of the output positions (**permutation language modeling**), so the same weights can decode left to right, decode all characters in parallel without context, or refine a first guess using context on both sides.

### Language models: help and harm

A recognizer that "knows English" can fix a smudged letter: "PLEA_E" is almost certainly "PLEASE". The knowledge can be **implicit** (an autoregressive decoder conditions on its previous characters), a **lexicon** (CRNN can restrict the output to a word list [16]) or an **explicit language model**: in ABINet [21] a separate bidirectional language model iteratively corrects the vision model's output.

The same prior can hurt. Text that is not language — serial numbers, codes, prices, names, mixed scripts — is exactly where OCR matters most and where a language model "corrects" correct readings. PARSeq's authors note this risk for external language models that do not see the image [20], and OCRBench reports that large multimodal models are weak on **non-semantic text** (random character strings) as well as on handwriting, multilingual text and mathematical expressions [22]. When the content is not natural language, measure with and without the language prior.

### Training data: synthetic text

Labeled real crops are scarce, and recognizers trained only on **synthetic text** generalize well across scene-text datasets [1]. MJSynth [23] renders single words with varied fonts, shadows, distortions and noise (8.9 million word images); SynthText [24] pastes text into natural photos following the scene geometry (5.5 million cropped words) [7]. Training on both beats either alone, and a combined 20% sample of each beats either full set, so diversity can matter more than size [7].

## End-to-end text spotting

**Plain version.** Instead of running a detector and then a separate recognizer on each crop, one network does both and shares its features. Reading helps finding (a region that reads as a word is probably text), and finding helps reading.

A **text spotter** outputs (shape, transcription) pairs directly. FOTS [25] shares convolutional features between a dense per-pixel detection branch and a recognition branch decoded with CTC, through **RoIRotate**, an operator that crops and rotates the feature region of each detected quadrilateral into an upright, fixed-height strip. Mask TextSpotter [26] extends Mask R-CNN (see [instance segmentation in DLCV 03B](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03b-image-segmentation/#instance-segmentation)) with masks for whole words and for individual characters, so it can read curved text without a sequence model. ABCNet [27] fits each curved text instance with parametric Bézier curves and samples features along them with **BezierAlign**, a curved analogue of RoIAlign. End-to-end evaluation then asks whether each ground-truth word was both located and read correctly, which we define in [Measuring OCR](#measuring-ocr).

## Document understanding

**Plain version.** On a page, reading the words is often the easy part. The questions are which words belong together, in which order to read them, and what they mean: which number is the total, which line is the title, which cell is in which column.

### Layout analysis and reading order

**Layout analysis** finds the regions of a page — paragraphs, titles, lists, tables, figures — usually by object detection or instance segmentation on page images. PubLayNet [28] matched the XML and PDF versions of over a million PubMed Central articles to label over 360 thousand page images automatically with five classes (text, title, list, table, figure). DocLayNet [29] has 80,863 manually annotated pages from diverse sources with 11 classes, in COCO format, because models trained only on scientific articles transfer poorly to reports, manuals and forms.

Given regions and words, the system must also produce a **reading order**. The obvious rule, "top to bottom, then left to right", fails on the simplest multi-column page, as the snippet shows on a two-column notice.

```python
import numpy as np

# Word boxes (x0, y0, x1, y1) on a two-column page: each column is one sentence.
left = "Pay the invoice within thirty days of receipt".split()
right = "Late payments carry a two percent monthly fee".split()
words, boxes = [], []
for col, text in [(0, left), (1, right)]:
    for k, w in enumerate(text):
        row, pos = divmod(k, 3)                       # three words per line
        x0 = 40 + 300 * col + 80 * pos
        y0 = 100 + 30 * row + 3 * col                 # columns not perfectly aligned
        words.append(w); boxes.append([x0, y0, x0 + 70, y0 + 20])
boxes = np.array(boxes, float)

def naive_order(boxes, line_tol=10):
    """Top-to-bottom, then left-to-right, treating boxes within line_tol as one line."""
    line = np.round(boxes[:, 1] / line_tol)
    return np.lexsort((boxes[:, 0], line))

def column_order(boxes, line_tol=10):
    """Split the page at the widest empty vertical strip, then read column by column."""
    x_min, x_max = int(boxes[:, 0].min()), int(boxes[:, 2].max())
    covered = np.zeros(x_max - x_min, bool)
    for x0, _, x1, _ in boxes.astype(int):
        covered[x0 - x_min:x1 - x_min] = True
    edges = np.flatnonzero(np.diff(np.r_[1, covered.astype(int), 1]))  # empty-run bounds
    starts, ends = edges[::2], edges[1::2]
    widest = np.argmax(ends - starts)
    split = x_min + (starts[widest] + ends[widest]) / 2
    col = (boxes[:, 0] > split).astype(int)
    line = np.round(boxes[:, 1] / line_tol)
    return np.lexsort((boxes[:, 0], line, col))

print("naive :", " ".join(words[i] for i in naive_order(boxes)))
print("column:", " ".join(words[i] for i in column_order(boxes)))
```

```text
naive : Pay the invoice Late payments carry within thirty days a two percent of receipt monthly fee
column: Pay the invoice within thirty days of receipt Late payments carry a two percent monthly fee
```

Every word in the naive output is correct, and the text is still useless. The column rule fixes this page but is another hand-made rule that fails on tables or figures (Figure 6); learned layout and document models exist largely to replace such rules.

### Text plus layout: the LayoutLM family

**Plain version.** Give a language model not only the words of a page but also where each word sits. Then "Total" followed by a number at the bottom right of a receipt means something different from the same word in the middle of a paragraph.

LayoutLM [30] starts from BERT ([NLP 10](/en/learn/nlp/nlp-10-pretrained-bert/#2-masked-and-conditional-objectives)) and adds a **2-D position embedding** to each word's token embedding. Each word comes with its OCR box $(x_0, y_0, x_1, y_1)$ — upper-left and lower-right corners — rescaled to integer "virtual" coordinates from 0 to 1,000 so that pages of different sizes share one scale. Four embedding lookups are added, with one table shared by $x_0$ and $x_1$ and another by $y_0$ and $y_1$:

$$
\mathbf h_i = \mathbf e_{\text{tok}}(w_i) + \mathbf e_{\text{pos}}(i) + \mathbf X(x_{0,i}) + \mathbf Y(y_{0,i}) + \mathbf X(x_{1,i}) + \mathbf Y(y_{1,i}),
$$

where $\mathbf h_i$ is the input vector of the $i$-th word $w_i$, $\mathbf e_{\text{tok}}$ and $\mathbf e_{\text{pos}}$ are BERT's token and 1-D position embeddings, and $\mathbf X$, $\mathbf Y$ are the learned tables for horizontal and vertical coordinates. Pre-training masks some words but keeps their 2-D positions, and the model predicts them from text and layout context (a **masked visual-language model**), on about 11 million scanned pages OCRed with the open-source Tesseract engine [30], [31]. LayoutLM also adds visual features of each word's image region, computed with a Faster R-CNN model, for downstream tasks [30]. LayoutLMv3 [32] instead feeds the page image as patches, masks both text tokens and image patches and adds a word–patch alignment objective, so one model serves text-centric tasks (forms, receipts, question answering) and image-centric ones (document classification, layout analysis).

**Key information extraction** — pulling "invoice number", "date" and "total" out of a page — is then token classification over the OCR words, as in [NLP 06's sequence labeling](/en/learn/nlp/nlp-06-sequence-labeling/#sequence-labels-and-span-prediction), plus linking keys to values. FUNSD [33] is a small benchmark of this kind: 199 real, noisy scanned forms whose semantic entities are labeled *question*, *answer*, *header* or *other*, with links between them.

The family's weakness is its input: it cannot fix a word the OCR engine missed or misread, and it inherits the engine's reading order.

### OCR-free models: Donut and Pix2Struct

**Plain version.** Skip the OCR engine. Train one model to look at the page image and *write* the desired output — the full text, a JSON record, or an answer — token by token, the way an image captioner writes a caption.

**Donut** (Document understanding transformer) [34] is a Swin Transformer encoder ([ML 04](/en/learn/ml/ml-04-vision-transformers/#swin-and-hierarchical-transformers)) and a BART decoder. It is pre-trained to read all the text of a page in reading order — a "pseudo-OCR" task on 11 million scanned English documents plus synthetic pages in Chinese, Japanese, Korean and English from its SynthDoG generator — and fine-tuned to emit a token sequence that converts one-to-one into JSON, for classification, information extraction or question answering [34]. It avoids the cost of running OCR, the inflexibility of OCR engines across languages and document types, and the propagation of OCR errors. **Pix2Struct** [35] is pre-trained to parse masked screenshots of web pages into simplified HTML, which mixes OCR, language modeling and captioning in one objective; it accepts variable-resolution inputs and renders the question directly onto the image.

The price is resolution and verifiability. Donut uses 2560 × 1920 inputs and reports that accuracy rises quickly with input size on documents with tiny text, at a growing compute cost [34]; and because the model *generates* rather than *transcribes*, nothing ties each output character to a region of the image.

### Vision-language models for documents

General multimodal large language models ([ML 09](/en/learn/ml/ml-09-foundation-models/#multimodal-large-language-models)) now answer questions about documents and transcribe pages; reading small text depends on tiling or dynamic resolution ([What the diagram hides](/en/learn/ml/ml-09-foundation-models/#what-the-diagram-hides)). Three cautions apply. First, a fluent answer is not a transcription: check it against detections or ask for coordinates and verify them. Second, generative readers have their own failure modes: the academic-document model Nougat [36] reports that it sometimes degenerates into repeating a sentence over and over (on 1.5% of its test pages, more often out of domain), and that numbers in reference lists and section titles are sometimes skipped or hallucinated. Third, measure the specific skill: OCRBench [22] gathers 29 datasets to test text recognition, scene-text and document question answering, key information extraction and handwritten mathematical expressions separately, instead of hiding OCR inside one aggregate score.

## Handwriting, many scripts and CJK text

**Plain version.** Handwriting varies from writer to writer and joins letters together; many scripts have far more symbols than the Latin alphabet; and some, such as Chinese, can also be written vertically. Each of these breaks an assumption that English scene-text models make quietly.

### Handwriting

Offline handwriting recognition reads lines of handwritten text from scans. The IAM database [37] of handwritten English sentences is, in the TrOCR authors' words, the most popular dataset for handwritten text recognition; TrOCR uses a split of 6,161 training, 966 validation and 2,915 test lines and reports case-sensitive CER [19]. Line-level recognizers with CTC or attention apply unchanged; the difficulty is variation between writers, connected letters and limited labeled data, which is why synthetic pre-training and large pre-trained decoders help. For Chinese handwriting, the CASIA online and offline databases [38] provide data for both pen-trajectory (online) and scanned (offline) recognition.

### Large character sets and vertical text

Chinese and Japanese use alphabets of tens of thousands of symbols, against a few dozen for Latin script [1]. RCTW-17 lists what makes Chinese text different: a much larger character set, no spaces between words, and many characters made of several unconnected parts [39]. Each has a consequence. The recognizer's output layer grows with the alphabet and most characters are rare: the Chinese Text in the Wild dataset labels 1,018,402 character instances in 32,285 street-view images, covering 3,850 distinct characters, most of them rarely used [40]. Unconnected parts break connected-component grouping. And without spaces, systems work on lines and characters rather than words (see [NLP 01](/en/learn/nlp/nlp-01-introduction/#7-chinese-text-segmentation-and-two-character-sets)).

**Vertical text** is the other difficulty: Chinese can be set vertically as well as horizontally, and street-view datasets such as CTW contain vertical text [40]. The W3C's *Requirements for Chinese Text Layout* describes vertical composition: characters run from top to bottom and lines from right to left, while embedded Latin words are either set upright or rotated 90° clockwise [41]. A recognizer that assumes left-to-right frames on a horizontal crop reads such a column as garbage; Baek et al. list vertical text among the failure cases common to all 24 recognizers they tested [7]. The remedy is to decide each detected line's orientation first and rotate or rearrange it before reading.

### Many scripts at once

Multilingual systems must also decide *which* script they are looking at. The ICDAR 2019 multi-lingual challenge (MLT-2019) [42] has 20,000 real images with text in 10 languages, and includes a script-identification task; its languages cover seven scripts [2].

## Measuring OCR

**Plain version.** Count mistakes the way a proofreader would: how many characters must be inserted, deleted or changed to turn the output into the truth. For detection, count how many found boxes are right and how many real words were found, as in object detection.

### CER and WER from edit distance

The **edit distance** (Levenshtein distance) between a reference string $r$ and a recognized string $h$ is the minimum number of insertions, deletions and substitutions that turn one into the other; the Wagner–Fischer dynamic program computes it [43] (derived in [PR 06](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/pr-06-templates-structured-models/#edit-distance)). Following an optimal alignment back through the table splits the total into $S$ substitutions, $D$ deletions and $I$ insertions. Then

$$
\text{CER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N}, \qquad \text{WER} = \frac{S_w + D_w + I_w}{N_w},
$$

where $N$ is the number of characters in the reference, and $S_w$, $D_w$, $I_w$ and $N_w$ are the same quantities counted over words (sequences split at spaces). Both are error rates: 0 is perfect, and they can exceed 1 when the output contains many insertions. Other conventions exist: scene-text recognition on cropped words usually reports **word accuracy** (the fraction of crops read exactly), some papers call one minus that the word error rate, and multilingual competitions report one minus a **normalized edit distance** (NED) [2]. Always state which one you report.

```python
import numpy as np

def edit_ops(ref, hyp):
    """Levenshtein alignment of two sequences; returns (S, D, I) counts."""
    n, m = len(ref), len(hyp)
    E = np.zeros((n + 1, m + 1), int)
    E[:, 0], E[0, :] = np.arange(n + 1), np.arange(m + 1)
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        for j in range(1, m + 1):
            E[i, j] = min(E[i - 1, j] + 1, E[i, j - 1] + 1,
                          E[i - 1, j - 1] + (ref[i - 1] != hyp[j - 1]))
    S = D = I = 0
    i, j = n, m
    while i > 0 or j > 0:                       # walk back along one optimal path
        if i > 0 and j > 0 and E[i, j] == E[i - 1, j - 1] + (ref[i - 1] != hyp[j - 1]):
            S += ref[i - 1] != hyp[j - 1]; i, j = i - 1, j - 1
        elif i > 0 and E[i, j] == E[i - 1, j] + 1:
            D += 1; i -= 1
        else:
            I += 1; j -= 1
    return S, D, I

def rates(ref, hyp):
    c = edit_ops(ref, hyp)
    w = edit_ops(ref.split(), hyp.split())
    return c, sum(c) / len(ref), w, sum(w) / len(ref.split())

ref = "Total due 25.80 EUR by 1 May"
for hyp in ["Total due 25.80 EUR by 1 May",
            "Tota1 due 25.8O EUR by l May",      # three look-alike glyphs
            "Total due25.80 EUR by 1 May",       # one missing space
            "Total due 25.80 EUR"]:              # last words not read
    c, cer, w, wer = rates(ref, hyp)
    print(f"{hyp:30s} chars S,D,I={c} CER={cer:.3f} | words S,D,I={w} WER={wer:.3f}")
```

```text
Total due 25.80 EUR by 1 May   chars S,D,I=(0, 0, 0) CER=0.000 | words S,D,I=(0, 0, 0) WER=0.000
Tota1 due 25.8O EUR by l May   chars S,D,I=(3, 0, 0) CER=0.107 | words S,D,I=(3, 0, 0) WER=0.429
Total due25.80 EUR by 1 May    chars S,D,I=(0, 1, 0) CER=0.036 | words S,D,I=(1, 1, 0) WER=0.286
Total due 25.80 EUR            chars S,D,I=(0, 9, 0) CER=0.321 | words S,D,I=(0, 3, 0) WER=0.429
```

Three look-alike substitutions cost 3 of 28 characters (CER 0.107) but ruin 3 of 7 words (WER 0.429), and one turns an amount into a non-number. One missing space costs one character but two word errors; losing the last three words costs nine characters, spaces included. CER says how much must be fixed; WER, or better a task check such as "is the total correct?", says whether the output is usable (Figure 7).

### Detection: precision, recall and H-mean at IoU 0.5

Text detection uses the precision and recall of [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#precision-recall-and-ap) with one matching rule and one summary number. In the protocol used by the Robust Reading competitions, as described for MLT-2019, a detection is a true positive if its IoU with a ground-truth quadrilateral exceeds 0.5, each ground truth and each detection may be matched at most once, and detections that overlap a region marked "do not care" (illegible or out-of-scope text) by more than half of that region's area are removed before matching [42]. Counts are accumulated over the whole test set, and

$$
P = \frac{\text{TP}}{|\mathcal D|}, \qquad R = \frac{\text{TP}}{|\mathcal G|}, \qquad H = \frac{2PR}{P + R},
$$

where TP is the number of matched pairs, $|\mathcal D|$ the number of detections after filtering, $|\mathcal G|$ the number of ground-truth words that are not "do not care", and $H$ the **H-mean** (harmonic mean of precision and recall, the F-score). Unlike COCO AP, H-mean is a single point of the precision–recall curve, at a confidence threshold chosen by the submitter; Long et al. recommend also reporting AP [1]. ICDAR 2013 used a different, overlap-based protocol, so its numbers are not comparable with IoU-based ones [1].

### End-to-end and document metrics

End-to-end spotting first matches detections to ground truth as above and then compares the transcriptions, either for every word (**end-to-end recognition**) or only for words in a given vocabulary (**word spotting**); results depend strongly on the size of that vocabulary [2]. Document benchmarks use task metrics: **ANLS** (average normalized Levenshtein similarity), an edit-distance-based score for DocVQA answers, chosen so that small OCR slips are not scored as total failures [44]; field-level F1 and a tree-edit-distance accuracy for extracted JSON records [34]; and COCO-style mAP for layout regions [29].

## Failure modes

**Plain version.** OCR fails where the pixels stop carrying the answer, where the shape or script differs from training, and where a model fills a gap with something plausible. The last kind is the most dangerous, because the output looks perfect.

**Look-alike glyphs and low resolution.** Many characters differ by a few pixels: 0 and O, 1, l and I, 5 and S, 8 and B, "rn" and "m". The snippet makes a tiny template reader for 15 characters and shows how it degrades as the number of pixels per character drops.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from skimage.transform import resize

CHARS = "0O8B5S1lIZ2aekx"

def glyph(c):
    """Render one character (ink = 1, paper = 0) on a 48 x 48 canvas."""
    fig = plt.figure(figsize=(0.48, 0.48), dpi=100)
    fig.text(0.5, 0.5, c, fontsize=26, ha="center", va="center")
    fig.canvas.draw()
    img = 1 - np.asarray(fig.canvas.buffer_rgba())[..., :3].mean(axis=2) / 255.0
    plt.close(fig)
    return img

def ncc(a, b):
    a, b = a - a.mean(), b - b.mean()
    return (a * b).sum() / np.sqrt((a * a).sum() * (b * b).sum())

templates = {c: glyph(c) for c in CHARS}
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for h in [48, 12, 8, 6]:                          # pixels per character cell
    errors, total = {}, 0
    for c, img in templates.items():
        for _ in range(20):
            low = resize(img, (h, h), anti_aliasing=True)       # camera sees few pixels
            low = low + rng.normal(0, 0.05, low.shape)          # sensor noise
            back = resize(low, (48, 48), order=1)               # what the reader gets
            guess = max(CHARS, key=lambda t: ncc(back, templates[t]))
            total += 1
            if guess != c:
                errors[(c, guess)] = errors.get((c, guess), 0) + 1
    top = sorted(errors.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:3]
    print(f"{h:2d}px  accuracy={1 - sum(errors.values()) / total:.2f}  most confused: "
          + ", ".join(f"{a}->{b} ({n})" for (a, b), n in top))
```

12px  accuracy=0.93  most confused: l->I (20)
 8px  accuracy=0.84  most confused: I->1 (13), l->1 (12), 0->O (6)
 6px  accuracy=0.53  most confused: B->8 (20), 0->8 (15), S->8 (12)

**Other common failures**, each with the stage where it starts (Figure 1):

- **Curved, rotated and perspective text** (detection and recognition): upright boxes miss it (Figure 3); horizontal recognizers misread it unless it is rectified.
- **Unusual fonts and styles** (recognition): calligraphic brand names and shop signs were among the cases that all 24 models in Baek et al.'s study failed on, along with vertical text, special characters, heavy occlusion and low resolution [7].
- **Merged or split instances** (detection): Figure 2's gap trade-off in learned form; segmentation detectors fight it with shrunk kernels and learned thresholds.
- **Wrong layout** (understanding): correct words in the wrong order (Figure 6), tables read across rows, a value linked to the wrong key.
- **Distribution shift** (all stages): layout models trained only on article pages degrade on more diverse documents [29]; [DLCV 11](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-11-robustness/) treats shift in general.
- **Hallucinated text** (any generative stage): a generative reader can output text that is not in the image — repeated sentences, invented section numbers [36], a "corrected" serial number. The same happens upstream: a generative restoration or super-resolution model can turn unreadable letters into clean, legible, wrong ones, as discussed in [DLCV 04C](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04c-generative-restoration/#the-hallucination-problem); and text in generated images is often misspelled ([DLCV 10](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-10-image-generation/#failure-modes)). Never run OCR on a generatively enhanced image for anything that must be true, such as an amount, a name or a license plate, without checking against the original.

## Modern view

### What the surveys say

Four reviews cover this chapter's ground. **Long et al.** [1] organize scene-text detection by how much of a word each prediction covers (whole words versus characters, segments or kernels) and recognition into CTC-based and encoder–decoder methods; they see generalization across datasets and large alphabets as the open problems. **Chen et al.** [2] focus on recognition, organized by pipeline stage, and collect the datasets and evaluation protocols. **Cui et al.** [3] review document tasks from rule-based analysis to pre-trained LayoutLM-style models, and **Zhang et al.** [4] split document parsing into modular pipelines and unified VLM-based models, naming complex layouts, the reliability of VLM parsing and inference cost as the main challenges. OCRBench [22] complements them with an evaluation of large multimodal models on text-related tasks.

*Optional: [Guide to the Modern view surveys](#guide-to-the-modern-view-surveys) (in the Paper guides). What each review covers, how it organizes the field and what it concludes.*

### The state of the art, 2023–2026

- **Pipelines remain the natural choice** when coordinates, speed or auditability matter: a DBNet-style detector, an attention or transformer recognizer and a layout model, each evaluated and corrected separately.
- **End-to-end page readers are maturing fast.** Models that turn a page into Markdown or structured text in one pass now cover formulas, tables and charts: Nougat for academic papers [36]; GOT, which treats plain text, formulas, tables, charts and sheet music as "characters" for one encoder–decoder model [48]; olmOCR, a fine-tuned vision-language model for linearizing PDFs at scale, released with its own benchmark of hard pages [49]; and DeepSeek-OCR, which studies how few vision tokens a page can be compressed into while remaining readable [50]. All of these reports come from their authors.
- **Evaluation is catching up.** OmniDocBench [47] (CVPR 2025) evaluates pipelines and end-to-end models on pages from nine document sources with 19 layout categories and 15 attribute labels, so that results can be broken down by document type and difficulty instead of one score.

### Open problems

1. **Faithfulness.** Generative readers can produce text that is not on the page, and their errors look like correct text. Character-level grounding (which pixels support each output token) and calibrated confidence are not yet standard.
2. **Non-semantic and rare text.** Codes, numbers, names, rare characters in large alphabets and mixed scripts are where language priors help least and errors cost most [22].
3. **Layout and reading order** on complex pages — multi-column articles with floating figures, nested tables, forms with handwritten entries — remain hard for pipelines and end-to-end models alike; robustness to complex layouts is among the main challenges named by Zhang et al. [4].
4. **Generalization across domains and scripts**, including vertical and curved text, without collecting new labels for each domain [1].
5. **Comparable evaluation.** Different subsets, metrics (CER, word accuracy, NED, ANLS) and output normalizations make published numbers hard to compare [7].

## Key takeaways

- Reading text is a pipeline — detection, recognition, layout and understanding — and each stage has its own failures; evaluate stages separately before the whole chain.
- Text breaks generic detection: long, thin, rotated, curved and densely packed words need quadrilateral or polygon outputs and polygon IoU; even a perfect upright box fails IoU 0.5 beyond about 8.5° for a 160 × 24 word.
- Segmentation detectors (PSENet, DBNet) predict shrunk word kernels so that touching words stay separate; differentiable binarization makes the threshold learnable with a $k$-amplified gradient, and at inference DBNet needs only a fixed threshold and connected components.
- Recognition is sequence prediction: CTC trains without alignments but greedy decoding can miss the most probable text; attention and transformer decoders model context and spelling, which helps on natural words and can hurt on codes and numbers.
- Document models add layout: LayoutLM's 2-D position embeddings on OCR boxes; OCR-free models (Donut, Pix2Struct, VLMs) write the output directly, trading the OCR error chain for resolution limits and possible hallucinated text.
- Report the right metric: CER and WER from edit distance (and say which variant), detection P/R/H-mean at IoU 0.5 with "do not care" filtering, task metrics such as ANLS; check benchmark subsets and label noise before comparing numbers.

### CTC prefix beam search, step by step

This note explains how a CTC decoder finds a likely *text* rather than a likely *path*, by searching over prefixes instead of alignments.

**What is searched.** A **prefix** $\ell$ is a partial transcription, such as "ca". After reading frames $1, \dots, t$, many alignments collapse to the same prefix. Prefix beam search keeps, for each prefix, two numbers:

- $p_b(\ell, t)$, the total probability of alignments of frames $1..t$ that collapse to $\ell$ and **end in a blank**;
- $p_{nb}(\ell, t)$, the total probability of those that **end in the last label of $\ell$**.

Their sum is the probability that the first $t$ frames read as $\ell$. The split is needed because the next symbol behaves differently in the two cases.

**Update rules.** For each kept prefix $\ell$ and each symbol $s$ at frame $t+1$, with $q = q_{t+1}(s)$ the network's probability of $s$:

1. **Blank** ($s = \varnothing$): the prefix does not change, and the alignment now ends in a blank: add $(p_b + p_{nb})\,q$ to $p_b(\ell, t+1)$.
2. **Repeat of the last label** ($s$ equals the last label of $\ell$): if the previous symbol was that label, the repeat merges and the prefix stays $\ell$: add $p_{nb}\,q$ to $p_{nb}(\ell, t+1)$. If the previous symbol was a blank, the repeat starts a new copy: add $p_b\,q$ to $p_{nb}(\ell s, t+1)$. This is how "aa" can be written as "a-a".
3. **Any other label**: extend the prefix: add $(p_b + p_{nb})\,q$ to $p_{nb}(\ell s, t+1)$.

After each frame, keep only the $B$ prefixes with the largest $p_b + p_{nb}$ (the **beam width**). At the end, return the best prefix. With an unlimited beam the result is exact for the CTC model; with $B = 1$ it behaves almost like greedy decoding.

```python
import numpy as np

alphabet = ["-", "c", "a", "t"]
probs = np.array([[0.10, 0.80, 0.05, 0.05],
                  [0.20, 0.05, 0.70, 0.05],
                  [0.60, 0.05, 0.30, 0.05],
                  [0.55, 0.02, 0.03, 0.40],
                  [0.55, 0.02, 0.03, 0.40]])

def prefix_beam_search(probs, beam=3):
    # Each prefix keeps two numbers: p_b (all its alignments end in blank)
    # and p_nb (they end in the prefix's last label).
    beams = {(): (1.0, 0.0)}
    for frame in probs:
        nxt = {}
        def add(prefix, pb, pnb):
            ob, onb = nxt.get(prefix, (0.0, 0.0))
            nxt[prefix] = (ob + pb, onb + pnb)
        for prefix, (pb, pnb) in beams.items():
            add(prefix, (pb + pnb) * frame[0], 0.0)              # emit blank
            for s in range(1, len(frame)):
                p = frame[s]
                if prefix and prefix[-1] == s:
                    add(prefix, 0.0, pnb * p)                    # repeat merges
                    add(prefix + (s,), 0.0, pb * p)              # blank separated
                else:
                    add(prefix + (s,), 0.0, (pb + pnb) * p)      # new label
        beams = dict(sorted(nxt.items(), key=lambda kv: -sum(kv[1]))[:beam])
    return [("".join(alphabet[s] for s in k), sum(v)) for k, v in beams.items()]

for B in [1, 3, 20]:
    top = prefix_beam_search(probs, beam=B)[:2]
    print(f"beam={B:2d}: " + ", ".join(f"{t!r} p={p:.3f}" for t, p in top))
```

```text
beam= 1: 'ca' p=0.155
beam= 3: 'cat' p=0.344, 'ca' p=0.179
beam=20: 'cat' p=0.363, 'ca' p=0.179
```

On the chapter's toy, a beam of 1 returns "ca", like greedy decoding. A beam of 3 already finds "cat", with a slightly low probability (0.344) because some of its alignments passed through prefixes that were pruned. A beam of 20 keeps every prefix here and reproduces the exact value 0.363 from the brute-force sum.

**Pitfalls.** Work in log space for long sequences, since the products underflow. Do not deduplicate by path, only by prefix. When a lexicon or language model is added, its score is usually applied when a prefix is extended by a label, weighted by a tuned factor, and often combined with a bonus per emitted character so that the search does not favor short outputs; the weights are tuned on held-out data, and the same over-correction risk described in the main text applies.

### Shrinking and expanding text polygons

This note works through the label geometry of segmentation-based text detectors: how much a word is shrunk for training, and how a detection is grown back.

**Shrinking.** For a ground-truth polygon with area $A$ and perimeter $L$, PSENet and DBNet move every edge inward by $D = A(1 - r^2)/L$ with a shrink ratio $r$ (DBNet uses $r = 0.4$) [14], [13]. For a polygon this offset is applied with a polygon-clipping algorithm (DBNet uses Vatti clipping). For an axis-aligned $w \times h$ rectangle the formula becomes

$$
D = \frac{wh\,(1 - r^2)}{2(w + h)}, \qquad w_s = w - 2D, \qquad h_s = h - 2D,
$$

where $w_s \times h_s$ is the shrunk kernel. Since $w/(w + h) \lt 1$, we have $2D \lt h(1 - r^2) \lt h$, so the kernel never vanishes, however thin the word: the offset adapts to the shape through $A/L$. For a long word, $A/L \approx h/2$ and the kernel height is about $h r^2$; for $r = 0.4$, about 16% of the word's height.

**Expanding.** At inference DBNet expands each detected region by $D' = A' r'/L'$ with $r' = 1.5$, where $A'$ and $L'$ are the region's area and perimeter [14]. If the detected region were exactly the shrunk kernel of a rectangle, the result would be:

```python
def shrink_offset(w, h, r=0.4):
    """DB/PSENet-style inward offset D = A (1 - r^2) / L for a w x h rectangle."""
    return w * h * (1 - r**2) / (2 * (w + h))

def unclip_offset(w, h, r_prime=1.5):
    """Outward offset D' = A' r' / L' applied to a detected (shrunk) rectangle."""
    return w * h * r_prime / (2 * (w + h))

for w, h in [(100, 20), (300, 20), (40, 40)]:
    D = shrink_offset(w, h)
    ws, hs = w - 2 * D, h - 2 * D                      # the training target ("kernel")
    Dp = unclip_offset(ws, hs)
    print(f"word {w:3d}x{h:2d}: D={D:4.1f} -> kernel {ws:5.1f}x{hs:4.1f}; "
          f"D'={Dp:4.1f} -> box {ws + 2 * Dp:5.1f}x{hs + 2 * Dp:4.1f}")
```

```text
word 100x20: D= 7.0 -> kernel  86.0x 6.0; D'= 4.2 -> box  94.4x14.4
word 300x20: D= 7.9 -> kernel 284.2x 4.2; D'= 3.1 -> box 290.5x10.5
word  40x40: D= 8.4 -> kernel  23.2x23.2; D'= 8.7 -> box  40.6x40.6
```

In this idealized calculation, $r' = 1.5$ restores a square word almost exactly but leaves thin words too short in height: the expansion is not the inverse of the shrink, because both depend on the shape through $A/L$ and the kernel's $A'/L'$ shrinks faster for thin shapes. In a real network the binarized region at threshold 0.2 is not exactly the kernel (the probability map has soft edges), and $r'$ is a constant tuned on data [14]. Two practical consequences: check the expansion ratio on your own data's aspect ratios, and expect height errors on long text lines to lower IoU even when the word is found.

**Edge cases.** Very small words have kernels of a pixel or two that a downsampled output map may not resolve, so detectors often ignore or down-weight ground-truth words below a minimum size. Self-intersecting or badly ordered annotation polygons make $A$ meaningless and must be cleaned before label generation. The threshold-map target uses the band between the shrunk and the dilated polygon (both at offset $D$), with values derived from the distance to the original border [14].

### Where the language model sits in a recognizer

This note compares the ways linguistic knowledge enters a text recognizer and how to test whether it is helping.

**Four placements.**

1. **None (pure CTC on visual features).** Each frame's output depends only on the image features, through the encoder's receptive field. Errors are visual (look-alike glyphs) and the output can be any string, which is right for codes and serial numbers.
2. **Lexicon constraint.** The output must be a word from a list $\mathcal V$. CRNN computes $p(y \mid x)$ only for words within edit distance $\delta$ of the greedy output, found quickly with a BK-tree (a metric tree for discrete distances), and returns the most probable one [16]. Strong when the vocabulary is known (a product catalog, a street-name list); wrong for anything outside it.
3. **Implicit language model.** An autoregressive attention or transformer decoder predicts $y_i$ from the image and $y_{\lt i}$, so spelling statistics of the training text are learned inside the decoder. Baek et al. attribute attention's ability to recover occluded letters to this implicit character-level modeling [7]. The statistics come from the training labels; models trained on synthetic data rendered from a word list learn that list's spelling.
4. **Explicit language model.** A separate model scores or corrects the visual output. ABINet blocks the gradient from the language model to the vision model so that the language model is trained as a real language model, uses a bidirectional cloze network, and iterates the correction to reduce the effect of noisy input [21]. PARSeq instead learns an ensemble of internal autoregressive models with shared weights by training on permutations of the output order, and argues that an external model that never sees the image may "rectify" correct predictions [20].

**How to test for over-correction.** Build a held-out set of non-semantic strings — random letters, digits, mixed case — rendered like your real data, and compare CER with and without the language component; OCRBench's non-semantic subset does the same for multimodal models [22]. If CER on random strings is much worse than on dictionary words with the same glyph statistics, the model is reading partly from its prior. For fields such as amounts and identifiers, use a format grammar (digits, separators, check digits) instead of a natural-language prior.

### Reading vertical and mixed-direction text

This note collects the layout facts that matter when an OCR system meets Chinese, Japanese or Korean text set vertically.

**The layout rules.** In vertical composition, characters are arranged from top to bottom and lines from right to left; columns of text blocks are arranged from top to bottom [41]. Embedded Latin text has three conventions: single letters and acronyms are set upright, one per character cell, in the same direction as the Han characters; general words, sentences and long numbers are set in proportional fonts rotated 90° clockwise; and two- or three-digit numbers are often set horizontally inside the vertical line ("horizontal-in-vertical") [41]. Quotation marks are usually replaced by corner brackets in vertical text [41].

**Consequences for OCR.**

- **Orientation is a per-line decision.** A detector must output the reading direction of each line, not only its shape. A tall, narrow box could be a vertical line of Chinese or a rotated horizontal line; only the content tells. One practical design adds an orientation classifier (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, or horizontal/vertical) between detection and recognition.
- **The crop must match what the recognizer was trained on.** A recognizer trained on horizontal lines can read a vertical line only after the line is transformed so that reading order runs left to right in the frames. For upright CJK characters this means stacking the character cells side by side (each character keeps its orientation); simply rotating the whole crop by 90° turns every character on its side. Rotated Latin words inside the line need the opposite treatment.
- **Mixed lines need segment-level direction.** A vertical line containing a rotated English word has two directions inside one line, which a single CTC or attention pass over one frame order cannot read correctly.
- **Evaluation must follow reading order.** CER compares strings, so a correct set of characters in the wrong order is scored as many errors; ground truth must be stored in the intended reading order, which for vertical layouts is not the order of increasing $x$.

Baek et al. found vertical text among the failure cases shared by all the recognizers they compared, because the models assume horizontal input by construction [7]; street-view data such as the CTW dataset contain it [40].

### Evaluation protocols in detail

This note fills in the details of text-detection and recognition metrics that change reported numbers.

**"Do not care" regions.** Ground truth marks illegible text and other text outside the task as "do not care". In the protocol described for MLT-2019, a detection $t$ is discarded if its intersection with a "do not care" region $d$ covers more than half of $d$, that is if $|t \cap d| / |d| \gt 0.5$, where $|\cdot|$ is area; remaining detections are matched one-to-one to the remaining ground truth with IoU $\gt 0.5$ [42]. Total-Text likewise asks that detections of "do not care" areas be filtered out before scoring [10]. A method that detects illegible text is therefore neither rewarded nor punished, which is the intent; but a system that merges a legible word with a neighboring "do not care" region can lose the legible word.

**One-to-many matching (ICDAR 2013).** The ICDAR 2011/2013 protocol follows the overlap-based method of Wolf and Jolion [51]: a match requires both the fraction of the ground truth covered and the fraction of the detection covered to exceed thresholds, and one-to-many or many-to-one matches (a line detected as several words, or several words as one line) are accepted with a reduced score, usually 0.8, instead of failing [1]. Numbers from this protocol and from the IoU protocol are not comparable.

**Single-threshold H-mean.** H-mean is computed at the confidence threshold chosen by the method's authors, usually the one that maximizes it on the test set itself. This is optimistic and hides the precision–recall trade-off; AP, as used by RCTW-17 [39], integrates over all thresholds [1].

**End-to-end vocabularies.** End-to-end and word-spotting results are reported with a strongly contextualised list (100 words per image, including all words in the image plus distractors), a weakly contextualised list (all words in the test set), or a generic list of about 90,000 words [2]. With the 100-word list, recognition reduces to choosing among a few candidates; numbers with different lists cannot be compared.

**Normalized and average edit distance.** NED divides each line's edit distance by the longer of the two strings, so every line contributes at most 1 regardless of length; $1 - \text{NED}$ is reported as a score [2]. RCTW-17's end-to-end task also used the **average edit distance (AED)**: each detection is matched to the ground-truth polygon of maximum IoU (or to nothing if no IoU exceeds 0.5), the edit distances of matched pairs are summed — an unmatched detection or ground truth counts as an edit distance against the empty string — and the sum is divided by the number of test images [39]. AED punishes missed and spurious text by its length, which NED alone does not.

**Document metrics.** DocVQA's primary metric, ANLS, is an edit-distance-based similarity between predicted and ground-truth answers, averaged over questions; it was first proposed for scene-text question answering, and the DocVQA authors adopted it because exact-match accuracy gives zero credit to an answer with one OCR slip [44]. Read the metric's definition on the benchmark's evaluation page before comparing with other similarity scores. Donut's TED-based accuracy for structured outputs is $\max\big(0,\ 1 - \text{TED}(\text{pr}, \text{gt}) / \text{TED}(\phi, \text{gt})\big)$, where TED is the tree edit distance between predicted and ground-truth trees and $\phi$ is the empty tree; unlike field-level F1, it gives partial credit and measures the nesting of groups [34].

**Normalization.** Many recognition benchmarks evaluate only case-insensitive letters and digits; TrOCR, for example, filters its scene-text outputs to a 36-character set of lowercase letters and digits, while it scores IAM handwriting with case-sensitive CER [19]. Always state case sensitivity, punctuation handling and whitespace rules alongside the number.

## Paper guides

These guides are optional: each walks through one paper the chapter builds on — the problem it poses, the idea, what it showed and what to check when you read it.

### Guide to the Modern view surveys

This guide expands the short survey summary in the Modern view: for each review, what it covers, how it organizes the field and what it concludes.

**Long, He and Yao, "Scene Text Detection and Recognition: The Deep Learning Era"** (IJCV 2021) [1] organizes detection into early deep methods, methods inspired by generic object detection, and methods based on sub-text components (characters, segments, kernels); recognition into CTC-based and encoder–decoder methods; then end-to-end systems and auxiliary techniques such as synthetic data and weak or semi-supervision. It also reviews the benchmarks and the evaluation protocols (IoU-based and DetEval-style matching, end-to-end vocabularies). Its conclusions: deep learning made oriented and curved text tractable, but most methods and datasets focus on English, languages with alphabets of tens of thousands of symbols remain hard, robustness to imperfect detection and generalization across datasets are open, and single-threshold F-scores should be complemented with AP.

**Chen, Jin, Zhu, Luo and Wang, "Text Recognition in the Wild: A Survey"** (ACM Computing Surveys 2021) [2] concentrates on recognition. It separates segmentation-based methods (preprocessing, character segmentation, character recognition) from segmentation-free ones, and describes the latter by stage: image preprocessing, feature representation, sequence modeling and prediction (CTC or attention). Its most practical parts are the catalogue of synthetic and real datasets and the evaluation protocols for Latin text (word accuracy, end-to-end recognition and word spotting with three vocabularies) and for multilingual text (normalized edit distance).

**Cui, Xu, Lv and Wei, "Document AI: Benchmarks, Models and Applications"** (arXiv 2021) [3] is a short review of document tasks — layout analysis, visual information extraction, document question answering and document image classification — with their benchmarks, tracing the methods from rule-based heuristics through statistical learning to deep, pre-trained multimodal models.

**Zhang et al., "Document Parsing Unveiled"** (arXiv 2024) [4] reviews document parsing, the conversion of pages into structured, machine-readable form. Its taxonomy has two branches: modular pipelines (layout analysis followed by recognizers for text, tables, mathematical expressions and visual elements) and unified models driven by vision-language models. It summarizes metrics and benchmarks and names robustness to complex layouts, the reliability of VLM-based parsing and inference efficiency as the open challenges.

**Liu et al., "OCRBench"** (Science China Information Sciences 2024) [22] is an evaluation rather than a survey. It tests large multimodal models on text recognition, scene-text and document question answering, key information extraction and handwritten mathematical expressions across 29 datasets, and finds them weakest on multilingual, handwritten, non-semantic and mathematical text.

### Baek et al., "What Is Wrong With Scene Text Recognition Model Comparisons?" (ICCV 2019)

**The problem.** By 2019 many scene-text recognizers each claimed state-of-the-art accuracy, but they had been trained on different synthetic datasets and tested on different versions of the same benchmarks, so it was impossible to tell which ideas helped [7].

**The idea.** Fix the data, then decompose the models. The paper fixes training to the union of MJSynth (8.9 M images) and SynthText (5.5 M) and evaluates on the union of seven benchmarks, 8,539 images in total. It then describes recognizers as four stages — transformation (none or TPS), feature extraction (VGG, RCNN or ResNet), sequence modeling (none or BiLSTM) and prediction (CTC or attention) — and trains all $2 \times 3 \times 2 \times 2 = 24$ combinations, each with five random seeds, measuring accuracy, time per image and number of parameters.

**What it showed.**

- **Dataset inconsistency was real.** Two versions of IC03 (860 and 867 images) and of IC15 (1,811 and 2,077) were in use, and on IC03 the seven missing examples alone can move accuracy by 0.8 points. Training on MJSynth only, SynthText only or both gave clearly different accuracies for the same model.
- **Modules have different costs.** Attention mainly costs time; the feature extractor mainly costs memory. Accuracy gains from each upgrade were about twice as large on irregular benchmarks (curved, rotated, perspective) as on regular ones.
- **Some failures are shared by all 24 models**: 644 of 8,539 images (7.5%). The paper groups them into calligraphic fonts, vertical text, special characters, heavy occlusion, low resolution and label noise.

**How to read the tables and plots.** The main figure plots accuracy against time and against parameters for all combinations, with the frontier of best trade-offs; read along a frontier to see which module upgrade buys the most accuracy per millisecond or per parameter. The module table reports *marginal* accuracy (averaged over all combinations containing a module), which shows a module's typical effect but not its best case. Check which benchmark subsets every reported number uses.

**Limits.** All models are trained on synthetic data only and evaluated on English letters and digits; the framework predates transformer recognizers and large pre-training.

**Why it matters here.** It is the source of the four-stage view of recognition in the main text, of the "diversity over size" lesson for synthetic data, and of the warning to check subsets and label noise before comparing numbers.

### DBNet: a reading guide

**The problem.** Segmentation-based detectors handle curved text, but turning a probability map into separate word instances needed heuristic, slow post-processing (PSENet's progressive expansion, for example), and the fixed binarization threshold was outside training [14].

**The idea.** Put the binarization inside the network. Predict a probability map $P$ and a threshold map $T$, combine them with the approximate step $\hat B = 1/(1 + e^{-k(P - T)})$, $k = 50$, and supervise $P$ and $\hat B$ with binary cross-entropy on shrunk text kernels and $T$ with an L1 loss in a band around each border, weighting the three losses 1 : 1 : 10. Hard negative mining keeps positives and negatives at a 1:3 ratio in the cross-entropy terms. Deformable convolutions in the backbone give a flexible receptive field for very long or very short words. At inference, binarize $P$ at 0.2, take connected components and expand them.

**What it showed.** Ablations on MSRA-TD500 and CTW1500 compare a plain segmentation baseline with and without DB and deformable convolution, for ResNet-18 and ResNet-50 backbones, and DB improves F-measure for both backbones on both datasets; a second ablation, on MLT-2017, tests whether supervising the threshold map helps. The paper then reports precision, recall, F-measure and frames per second on five benchmarks (Total-Text, CTW1500, ICDAR 2015, MSRA-TD500 and MLT-2017), at several input sizes.

**How to read the tables.** Each benchmark table lists P, R, F and FPS; the number in brackets is the height of the input image, which changes both accuracy and speed, so compare rows at matching sizes. Speeds are measured with batch size 1 on one GPU and include post-processing, about 30% of the inference time; check whether competing methods' speeds were measured on the same hardware or copied from their papers. Because the threshold branch is dropped at inference, a model trained with DB runs exactly as fast as the same model trained without it. The threshold-map ablation is the cleanest evidence for the "adaptive threshold" claim; the DB-versus-baseline ablation mixes the effect of the learned threshold with that of the extra supervision.

**Limits.** The learned threshold is not used at inference, so the separation of touching words at test time rests on how sharp $P$ has become. A word whose kernel is lost (very small or very thin text) cannot be recovered by expansion. The expansion ratio $r'$ is a fixed constant (see the Supplementary note on polygons).

**Why it matters here.** DBNet is the reference design for the segmentation branch of text detection in the main text, and it shows how moving a hand-tuned step into training can simplify inference.

### Donut: a reading guide

**The problem.** Document-understanding systems relied on an external OCR engine whose cost, language coverage and errors limited everything downstream [34].

**The idea.** A single image-to-sequence transformer (a Swin encoder and a BART decoder) trained in two stages. **Reading**: predict all the text of a page in reading order, using 11 million scanned English documents with pseudo-labels from a commercial OCR service, plus synthetic pages from SynthDoG in Chinese, Japanese, Korean and English. **Understanding**: fine-tune the decoder to emit token sequences that map one-to-one to JSON — a class token for classification, nested fields for information extraction, an answer for question answering.

**What it showed.** Experiments cover document classification (RVL-CDIP), information extraction (the public CORD receipt and Chinese train-ticket datasets, plus in-service Japanese business-card and Korean receipt datasets) and DocVQA, comparing against OCR-based models in accuracy and in speed including OCR time. Analyses vary the backbone, the pre-training data and the input resolution, visualize the decoder's cross-attention as a rough text locator, and show that OCR-based baselines depend strongly on which OCR engine they use.

**How to read the tables.** Two metrics are used for information extraction: field-level F1 (exact field match) and TED-based accuracy (tree edit distance, with partial credit). They can rank methods differently, so read both. Speed columns for OCR-based methods include the OCR engine; check which engine, because the paper shows that this choice changes both speed and accuracy. For DocVQA, the resolution study is the key result: accuracy grows quickly with input size because documents contain tiny text, and the larger input makes the model slower.

**Limits.** The paper's own example shows Donut missing tiny text in a large image, and generation offers no character-level grounding, so errors cannot be traced to pixels as with an OCR box. The pre-training labels on real documents come from an OCR engine, so OCR is not absent from the pipeline, only from inference.

**Why it matters here.** Donut is the clearest early statement of the OCR-free design that today's VLM-based document readers follow, and of its two costs: resolution and verifiability.
