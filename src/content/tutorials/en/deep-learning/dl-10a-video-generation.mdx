- Why video generation is much harder than image generation: the size of the data, the need for believable motion, and **temporal consistency** (no flicker, no identity drift, objects that persist).

- How the condition enters: **text-to-video**, **image-to-video**, camera and motion control, and how long videos are produced by extension or hierarchy.
- How **autoregressive** and **diffusion** video generators differ, and how hybrids make diffusion causal and streamable.

## The big picture

A video generator makes a short moving picture from a request: a sentence ("a dog runs along a beach at sunset"), a still image to bring to life, a camera path, or the first seconds of a clip to continue. The result must look good in every frame, the motion must be believable, and things must stay the same thing from one frame to the next.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Think of a flipbook. If you draw every page separately from memory, the dog's spots move around, its ears change size and the whole book seems to shimmer when you flip it. A good flipbook artist first sketches the rough motion through all the pages at once, then adds the details so that each spot stays on the same part of the dog as it runs. A video generator does the same thing: it plans many frames together, in a compressed sketch space, so that the details move with the objects instead of jumping around.
</Callout>

**Why it matters.** Video generation is used in creative tools, for animating photos, for generating training data and simulation scenes, and as a candidate "world simulator" for robots and vehicles. It is also the most expensive kind of visual generation: a five-second clip contains dozens of images that must all agree. Most of the engineering is about one trade-off: modeling all frames jointly, for consistency, without paying for every pixel of every frame to interact with every other.

## What makes video harder than images

**Plain version.** A video is a stack of images in time, so everything about image generation still applies, plus three new problems: there is far more data, the motion itself must be right, and the frames must agree with each other. A model that draws each frame well but independently produces a shimmering, unstable clip.

### The tasks

Every task below samples a video $x \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times H \times W \times 3}$ (with $F$ frames of height $H$ and width $W$, three color channels) from a conditional distribution $p(x \mid c)$; the tasks differ only in the condition $c$.

| Task | Condition $c$ | Examples in this chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Text-to-video (T2V) | a text prompt | Make-A-Video [1], CogVideoX [2], Wan [3] |
| Image-to-video (I2V) | a first frame, often plus a prompt | Stable Video Diffusion [4], DynamiCrafter [5] |
| Video extension or prediction | earlier frames of the same clip | VDM [6], Video LDM [7], StreamingT2V [8] |
| Frame interpolation | keyframes to fill in between | Make-A-Video [1], Stable Video Diffusion [4] |
| Video editing | an input video plus an edit request | Tune-A-Video [9], Text2Video-Zero [10] |
| Controlled generation | a camera path or object trajectories | MotionCtrl [11], CameraCtrl [12], DragNUWA [13] |

As with images ([DLCV 12](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/#six-kinds-of-generation)), one trained model can often serve several rows: a first frame, earlier frames or keyframes are extra conditions, and masking decides which frames are generated.

### Three demands on every clip

- **Per-frame quality.** Each frame on its own should look like a real image: sharp, free of artifacts, well composed.
- **Motion quality.** The clip should move, and the motion should be plausible: speed, direction and deformation should look like the real thing. A clip that barely moves is a failure even if every frame is perfect.
- **Temporal consistency.** What is the same object should stay the same object. Three failures have names. **Flicker** is fast, frame-to-frame change of brightness or fine texture that no real motion explains. **Identity drift** is a slow change of a subject's appearance (a face, a logo, the pattern on a shirt) over the clip. A failure of **object permanence** is an object that disappears, changes or reappears wrongly after being hidden.

The demands pull against each other. The easiest way to remove flicker is to generate almost no motion; the easiest way to get dramatic motion is to let details change freely. Evaluation, later in this chapter, has to check all three together.

### The cost

A video is large. A clip of $F = 81$ frames at $480 \times 832$ pixels (about five seconds at 16 frames per second) holds $81 \cdot 480 \cdot 832 \cdot 3 \approx 9.7 \times 10^7$ numbers, the size of about 81 images. A generator also cannot simply treat the frames one after another: consistency requires information to flow between distant frames. With attention, the cost of letting every position see every other position grows with the **square** of the number of positions. Two design levers control it: **compress** the video before generating (the next section), and **restrict** which positions attend to which (the section after it).

## Compressing video: 3D VAEs and video tokenizers

**Plain version.** Like latent image diffusion, almost every modern video generator works on a compressed version of the video, produced by an autoencoder. The new step is compressing **time** as well as space: neighboring frames are very similar, so a group of four frames can share one latent frame. Compression makes generation affordable, but the decoder then limits how much detail and fast motion the final video can contain.

### Why not compress each frame with an image autoencoder?

The first latent video models reused the autoencoder of an image latent diffusion model [14] and encoded every frame separately. The Video LDM authors report that this causes flickering, because the image autoencoder was trained on images only and does not know that consecutive frames should decode consistently; they fine-tuned the **decoder** with added temporal layers and a 3D-convolutional discriminator on video, and found this step critical for good results [7]. Stable Video Diffusion also builds on a pretrained image latent model and compresses only in space [4], [3]. A frame-wise autoencoder also keeps one latent frame per video frame, so it does nothing for the cost in time.

### 3D causal VAEs

A **3D VAE** uses convolutions over time as well as space, so it can downsample both [2]. Most recent systems make it **causal in time**: every temporal convolution is padded only at the beginning of the clip, so a latent frame depends only on the current and earlier video frames, never on later ones [15], [2]. A useful consequence is that the very first frame is encoded on its own. The usual frame layout is therefore "$1 + T$": the first frame becomes the first latent frame, and every following group of $f_t$ frames becomes one more latent frame. A still image is then just a one-frame video, which lets one autoencoder, and one generator, train on images and videos together [15], [16], [3].

Let a clip have $F = 1 + T$ frames of size $H \times W$, compressed by a factor $f_t$ in time and $f_s$ along each spatial axis into $C$ latent channels. The latent grid and the number of transformer tokens are

$$
T' = 1 + \frac{T}{f_t},\qquad H' = \frac{H}{f_s},\qquad W' = \frac{W}{f_s},\qquad
L = T' \cdot \frac{H'}{p} \cdot \frac{W'}{p},
$$

where $T'$, $H'$ and $W'$ are the numbers of latent frames, rows and columns, $p$ is the side of the square patch that the transformer groups into one token (often $p = 2$), and $L$ is the sequence length. Wan's report gives exactly this count, $L = (1 + T/4) \cdot H/16 \cdot W/16$, for its $4 \times 8 \times 8$ autoencoder and $2 \times 2$ patches [3]. Two numbers summarize a design: the **compression of numbers**, $\frac{3 F H W}{C\, T' H' W'}$, and the **pixels per token**, about $f_t f_s^2 p^2$. Because the latent has more channels than the 3 of RGB, a video VAE compresses tokens much more than it compresses numbers.

### Continuous latents or discrete tokens

Like image models ([DLCV 12](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/#the-model-families-on-one-map)), video generators either work on a **continuous** latent grid (a VAE, used by diffusion and flow models) or on **discrete tokens** from a learned codebook (used by masked and autoregressive token models). MAGVIT introduced a 3D tokenizer that quantizes a video into spatiotemporal tokens for masked generation [20]. MAGVIT-v2 replaced it with a causal 3D CNN tokenizer and a **lookup-free quantizer** whose vocabulary can grow large, and with it a language model outperformed diffusion models on image and video benchmarks in the authors' tests [15]; codebooks and their problems are covered in [ML 09](/en/learn/ml/autoregressive-vision/#codebook-problems-and-alternatives).

### Code: latent size and attention cost

The snippet computes the latent grid, the number of tokens and the number of query–key pairs in one attention layer for an 81-frame, $480 \times 832$ clip, for three autoencoders. The pair counts anticipate the next section: "full" lets every token attend to every token, "factorized" splits attention into a spatial part within each latent frame and a temporal part at each position.

```python
def latent_grid(frames, height, width, ft, fs, channels=16):
    """Latent shape of a causal video VAE: the first frame alone, then groups of ft frames."""
    t = 1 + (frames - 1) // ft
    return t, height // fs, width // fs, channels

def attention_pairs(t, s):
    """Query-key pairs in one attention layer over t latent frames of s tokens each."""
    full = (t * s) ** 2                 # every token attends to every token
    factorized = t * s**2 + s * t**2    # spatial attention per frame + temporal attention per position
    return full, factorized

frames, H, W, patch = 81, 480, 832, 2   # about 5 s at 16 fps; DiT patches of 2 x 2 latent positions
pixels = frames * H * W * 3
print(f"pixel values: {pixels:,}")
for name, ft, fs, c in [("image VAE, every frame", 1, 8, 4),
                        ("video VAE 4x8x8", 4, 8, 16),
                        ("video VAE 8x16x16", 8, 16, 64)]:
    t, h, w, c = latent_grid(frames, H, W, ft, fs, c)
    s = (h // patch) * (w // patch)     # tokens per latent frame after patchify
    full, fact = attention_pairs(t, s)
    print(f"{name:23s} latent {t}x{h}x{w}x{c} ({pixels / (t*h*w*c):5.1f}x fewer numbers)  "
          f"tokens {t*s:7,}  full {full:.1e}  factorized {fact:.1e}  ratio {full/fact:4.0f}")
```

It prints:

```text
pixel values: 97,044,480
image VAE, every frame  latent 81x60x104x4 ( 48.0x fewer numbers)  tokens 126,360  full 1.6e+10  factorized 2.1e+08  ratio   77
video VAE 4x8x8         latent 21x60x104x16 ( 46.3x fewer numbers)  tokens  32,760  full 1.1e+09  factorized 5.2e+07  ratio   21
video VAE 8x16x16       latent 11x30x52x64 ( 88.4x fewer numbers)  tokens   4,290  full 1.8e+07  factorized 1.7e+06  ratio   11
```

(All numbers are our own arithmetic; the $8 \times 16 \times 16$ row with 64 channels is a hypothetical configuration, not a specific model.) Three lessons. First, the $4 \times 8 \times 8$ video VAE keeps about as many numbers as the frame-wise image VAE, but it cuts the **tokens** by a factor of four, and the attention pairs by a factor of sixteen, because each latent position carries more channels. Second, the 32,760 tokens match Wan's formula for this clip size. Third, full attention over 32,760 tokens means about $10^9$ query–key pairs per layer, which is why the choice between full and factorized attention, and the compression factor, decide what a model can afford.

## Architectures: from inflated image models to video transformers

**Plain version.** Early video generators started from a good image generator and **inflated** it: they kept its layers, which treat each frame as a separate image, and inserted new temporal layers that let frames exchange information. Later systems were built as video transformers from the start, and the main design question became how attention should connect positions across space and time.

### Inflating a pretrained image model

The idea is to reuse what an image model already knows about appearance and to learn only motion. Video Diffusion Models (VDM) turned the image diffusion U-Net into a **3D U-Net factorized over space and time**: every $3 \times 3$ convolution became a $1 \times 3 \times 3$ convolution that acts on each frame separately, each spatial attention block kept attending within a frame, and a new temporal attention block after it attended over frames at each spatial position [6]. Because temporal attention can be switched off, the same network can be trained on single images and on videos at once, and the authors found this joint training important for sample quality [6].

Later systems keep the pretrained image layers and insert new temporal layers that are **initialized to do nothing**, so the inflated model starts exactly as the image model. Let $h$ be the features of one block, reshaped so that frames are treated as a batch of images. The inflated block computes

$$
h_{\text{out}} = h + Z\big(\operatorname{TempAttn}(h)\big),
$$

where $\operatorname{TempAttn}$ attends along the frame axis at each spatial position and $Z$ is a linear projection whose weights start at zero, so that $h_{\text{out}} = h$ at the start of training (the same idea as ControlNet's zero convolutions in [DLCV 12](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/notes/controlnets-zero-convolutions-why-training-starts-from-the-original-model/)). Training then moves the model gradually from "a batch of independent images" toward "a video". Make-A-Video [1], Video LDM [7], AnimateDiff [21] and Open-Sora [22] all use a version of this idea: identity-initialized layers, a learned mixing weight or zero-initialized projections. Make-A-Video also shows the payoff: it learns appearance and its link to text from image–text pairs and only motion from unlabeled video, so it needs no paired text–video data [1].

### Factorized or full spatiotemporal attention

**Plain version.** A token can look at every other token in the clip (full attention), or first at the tokens of its own frame and then at the tokens in the same place in other frames (factorized attention). Factorized attention is much cheaper; full attention can follow an object that moves far between frames.

Let a clip have $T$ latent frames of $S$ tokens each, so $N = T S$ tokens in total. One full attention layer compares $N^2 = T^2 S^2$ query–key pairs. Factorized attention runs spatial attention inside each frame ($T$ problems of size $S$, so $T S^2$ pairs) and temporal attention at each position ($S$ problems of size $T$, so $S T^2$ pairs). The ratio is

$$
\frac{T^2 S^2}{T S^2 + S T^2} = \frac{T S}{S + T},
$$

which is close to $T$ when $S \gg T$, as in the snippet above (ratio 21 for 21 latent frames). The factorized pattern comes from video understanding: TimeSformer compared self-attention schemes and found "divided" space–time attention best among those it tried for video classification [23], and ViViT proposed several variants that factorize the spatial and temporal dimensions [24]. Latte compared four such decompositions inside a latent video diffusion transformer (interleaved spatial and temporal blocks, late fusion, and two ways of splitting attention inside a block) and found interleaved spatial and temporal transformer blocks performed best in its experiments [25].

The case for full attention is about motion. CogVideoX argues that with separated spatial and temporal attention, an object that moves far between adjacent frames cannot attend directly to itself in the next frame; information must travel indirectly through other patches, which makes consistency of large motions hard to learn; it therefore uses full 3D attention over text and video tokens together [2]. HunyuanVideo chose full attention, citing better results than divided attention, a single design for images and videos, and the reuse of acceleration methods developed for language models [16]. Wan also uses full spatiotemporal attention [3]; Open-Sora Plan moved from a factorized "2+1D" denoiser to 3D full attention and added a cheaper sparse attention operation [26]; and LTX-Video uses its very high compression precisely so that full attention stays affordable [18]. The practical rule: full attention is preferred when the token count allows it, and the autoencoder is designed to make the token count allow it.

### Cascades or one model for the whole clip

**Cascades** generate a short, small, low-frame-rate video and then improve it with separate models. Imagen Video uses seven sub-models (one base model, three spatial super-resolution and three temporal super-resolution models) to reach 128 frames of $1280 \times 768$ at 24 frames per second, about 5.3 seconds [27]. Make-A-Video combines a base model, a frame-interpolation network and two super-resolution models [1]; Video LDM generates sparse keyframes and fills in frames with an interpolation model [7]. A cascade keeps each stage small, but the base stage fixes the global motion from few frames. Lumiere argued that generating distant keyframes and then interpolating makes global temporal consistency difficult, and introduced a **Space-Time U-Net** that downsamples and upsamples in time as well as space and generates the full-frame-rate clip in one pass [28]. Large latent transformers with temporal compression take the same single-stage route; Movie Gen notes that its $8\times$ temporal compression lets it forgo the frame-interpolation models of earlier work [17].

## Conditioning: text-to-video and image-to-video

**Plain version.** The prompt enters a video model the same way it enters an image model, through attention to text-encoder outputs. A first frame for image-to-video enters as an extra input that every generated frame can see. The difference from images is that the condition must hold for the whole clip, and that "how much it moves" is itself something to control.

### Text-to-video

Text encoders, cross-attention and joint text–image attention are explained in [DLCV 12](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/#the-text-to-image-system). Video systems use the same mechanisms. Wan embeds text with **cross-attention** from video tokens to text-encoder outputs [3]. CogVideoX concatenates text and video tokens in one sequence and uses an **expert adaptive LayerNorm** with separate modulation for the two modalities, because their feature spaces and numerical scales differ [2]. HunyuanVideo processes video and text tokens in separate streams first and then concatenates them for joint processing ("dual-stream to single-stream"), with a multimodal language model as its main text encoder [16]. Captions must describe motion and events, not only appearance (see Data).

Make-A-Video also feeds the **frame rate** to the model, as data augmentation and as a control of motion speed [1]. Guidance works as for images ([DLCV 12](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/#guidance-as-used-in-practice)).

### Image-to-video

Image-to-video ("animate this photo") must keep the input frame's content while inventing motion. Three documented designs:

- **Stable Video Diffusion** replaces the text embedding with the image embedding of the conditioning frame from a pretrained image–text model, and concatenates a noise-augmented copy of the frame to the denoiser input along the channel axis, repeated for every frame. The authors found that a constant guidance scale caused either drift away from the conditioning frame or oversaturation, and instead increased the guidance scale linearly across frames [4].
- **DynamiCrafter** projects the image into a text-aligned context space with a query transformer, so that the video model can attend to it like a prompt, and also concatenates the full image with the initial noise to preserve visual detail [5].
- **HunyuanVideo** uses the latent of the reference image directly as the first latent frame [16], a natural choice when the causal VAE encodes the first frame alone.

A common failure of image-to-video is **too little motion**: the model can score well on fidelity to the input by barely changing it. StreamingT2V reports that competing image-to-video models tend toward "video stagnation" when used repeatedly to extend a clip [8].

## Camera and motion control

**Plain version.** A prompt such as "the camera slowly orbits the statue" is a weak way to describe motion. Control methods give the generator the motion directly: a camera path (where the camera is in each frame) or trajectories (where an object or a dragged point should go). The challenge is to obey the motion without changing the content.

- **Camera motion as a small adapter.** Stable Video Diffusion trained separate camera-motion LoRA modules for horizontal movement, zooming and a static camera on top of its base model [4]; AnimateDiff's **MotionLoRA** adapts its motion module to new motion patterns such as shot types at a low cost [21].
- **Camera poses as a dense input.** **CameraCtrl** trains a plug-and-play camera module on top of a video diffusion model, leaving the base model untouched. Instead of feeding raw camera matrices, it represents each frame's camera as a per-pixel **Plücker embedding**, a 6-number description of the viewing ray through each pixel, which ties camera geometry to image positions; the authors found that training videos with diverse camera paths and an appearance similar to the base model's data gave the best balance of control and generalization [12].
- **Camera and objects together.** **MotionCtrl** controls camera motion and object motion independently, with camera poses and object trajectories as conditions that carry no appearance information [11]. **DragNUWA** combines text, an image and trajectories for fine-grained control of open-domain video [13].

A camera path is the same pose sequence that 3D reconstruction estimates ([DLCV 11](/en/learn/deep-learning/3d-reconstruction/)). Following it does not make the generated scene geometrically consistent, and a control signal is only as reliable as the poses or tracks it was trained on.

## Long videos

**Plain version.** Models are trained on short clips, a few seconds long, because memory grows with length. To make a longer video you must either keep extending a clip piece by piece, which tends to drift, or plan the whole video coarsely first and fill in the details, which needs long training videos. Either way, keeping the story and the identities straight over a minute is still hard.

Four strategies are documented:

1. **Extend autoregressively, chunk by chunk.** Generate a clip, then generate the next clip conditioned on the last frames of the previous one. VDM showed that simply replacing the known frames during sampling gives continuations that look good alone but do not match the earlier frames, and proposed **reconstruction guidance**, which adds a guidance term from the model's reconstruction of the conditioning frames [6]. Video LDM trains prediction models that receive a few context frames through a mask [7]. **StreamingT2V** adds a short-term memory (attention to features of the previous chunk) for smooth transitions and a long-term memory (features of the first chunk) so the model does not forget the initial scene [8].
2. **Plan coarse, then fill in.** **NUWA-XL** applies a global diffusion model to generate keyframes over the entire time range and local diffusion models that recursively fill in frames between them, which lets it train directly on long videos and generate segments in parallel [31].
3. **Change the noise, not the model.** **FreeNoise** extends a pretrained short-clip model without tuning: it reschedules a sequence of initial noises with long-range correlation and applies temporal attention over windows, and adds a motion-injection method for prompts that change over time [32].
4. **Variable-length generation from a story.** **Phenaki** compresses video into discrete tokens with a tokenizer that is causal in time, so it handles variable lengths, and generates long videos from a sequence of prompts that change over time [33].

The central difficulty is **error accumulation**: each chunk is conditioned on generated, slightly wrong frames, and errors compound, exactly as in the rollouts of a learned world model ([DLCV 13](/en/learn/deep-learning/world-models/#why-errors-compound)). The causal-diffusion methods in the section on autoregressive generation address the same problem from the training side.

## Temporal consistency

**Plain version.** A consistent video keeps each detail attached to the thing it belongs to: when the dog moves, its spots move with it; when the camera pans, the texture of the wall slides along. The simplest test is to undo the motion and check that nothing else changed. The trap is that a video that does not move at all passes this test perfectly.

### Where inconsistency comes from

A generator invents detail that the condition does not specify. If that invented detail is drawn afresh for every frame, it flickers. If it is fixed to the screen instead of to the objects, it "sticks" while the scene moves under it. Consistency means the invented detail must be **propagated along the motion**. Architectures do this through temporal layers and joint generation of all frames; several training-free methods enforce it in pretrained image models. Text2Video-Zero makes the self-attention of every frame attend to the **first frame** (cross-frame attention), to preserve the appearance and identity of the foreground object, and adds motion dynamics to the latent codes to keep the background consistent [10]. Make-A-Video uses the **same noise initialization** for every frame in its final per-frame super-resolution stage, to encourage consistent invented detail across frames [1]. Tune-A-Video adds a spatiotemporal attention mechanism to an image model and tunes it on a single video [9].

### Measuring consistency

Three measures are common, and each is defined in terms of quantities you can compute from the generated video alone:

- **Frame difference** $D = \frac{1}{(F-1)\,P} \sum_{t=2}^{F} \lVert x_t - x_{t-1} \rVert_1$, where $x_t$ is frame $t$ and $P$ the number of pixel values per frame. It is cheap but mixes real motion with flicker; VBench measures temporal flickering this way only on static scenes, where all change is flicker [34].
- **Warping error** $E = \frac{1}{(F-1)\,P} \sum_{t=2}^{F} \lVert x_t - \mathcal W(x_{t-1}, u_t) \rVert_1$, where $u_t$ is the motion (optical flow) from frame $t-1$ to frame $t$ and $\mathcal W$ warps the previous frame along it. It removes the real motion before comparing; flow, warping and its masked form are explained in [DLCV 09A.3](/en/learn/deep-learning/video-super-resolution/#temporal-consistency). For a generated video the flow must be **estimated from the video itself**.
- **Feature similarity over time.** VBench's subject consistency compares the features of each frame with those of the first and the previous frame, $S = \frac{1}{F-1}\sum_{t=2}^{F} \tfrac12\big(\langle d_1, d_t\rangle + \langle d_{t-1}, d_t\rangle\big)$, where $d_t$ is a unit-length feature vector of frame $t$ from a self-supervised image model and $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is the dot product (cosine similarity) [34].

All three reward a still video. For that reason VBench pairs them with a **dynamic degree** dimension that measures the amount of motion with an optical-flow network [34].

### Code: flicker, sticking and freezing

The toy builds a "coarse video" that pans an image 2 pixels per frame, as a prompt might request, and adds invented fine detail in four ways: new noise every frame, the same noise fixed on the screen, the same noise moved along with the scene, and a frozen video that repeats the first frame. It estimates each video's own motion with phase correlation and reports the three quantities.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi
from skimage import data, transform
from skimage.registration import phase_cross_correlation

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
T, v = 16, 2                                   # 16 frames; the prompt asks for a pan of 2 px per frame
scene = transform.resize(data.camera() / 255.0, (128, 128), anti_aliasing=True)
content = np.stack([np.roll(scene, v * t, axis=1) for t in range(T)])   # the "coarse" video

def detail():                                  # invented fine texture, as a generator would add
    n = ndi.gaussian_filter(rng.standard_normal(scene.shape), 0.7)
    return 0.04 * n / n.std()

d0 = detail()
videos = {
    "independent noise": content + np.stack([detail() for _ in range(T)]),  # new detail every frame
    "shared noise":      content + d0,                                       # same detail, fixed on screen
    "propagated noise":  content + np.stack([np.roll(d0, v * t, axis=1) for t in range(T)]),  # detail moves with the scene
}
videos["frozen"] = np.repeat(videos["propagated noise"][:1], T, axis=0)      # a still image repeated

c = (slice(None), slice(8, -8), slice(8, -8))  # ignore borders (np.roll wraps around)
def measure(vid):
    shifts = [phase_cross_correlation(a, b, upsample_factor=10)[0][1] for a, b in zip(vid[1:], vid[:-1])]
    flicker = np.abs(np.diff(vid, axis=0))[c].mean()                         # frame-difference energy
    warped = np.stack([np.roll(f, int(round(s)), axis=1) for f, s in zip(vid[:-1], shifts)])
    warp_err = np.abs(vid[1:] - warped)[c].mean()                            # error after the video's own motion
    return flicker, warp_err, np.mean(np.abs(shifts))

for name, vid in videos.items():
    f, w, m = measure(vid)
    print(f"{name:18s} frame difference {f:.4f}   warping error {w:.4f}   estimated motion {m:.2f} px/frame")
```

It prints:

```text
independent noise  frame difference 0.0808   warping error 0.0446   estimated motion 2.00 px/frame
shared noise       frame difference 0.0528   warping error 0.0419   estimated motion 2.01 px/frame
propagated noise   frame difference 0.0796   warping error 0.0000   estimated motion 2.00 px/frame
frozen             frame difference 0.0000   warping error 0.0000   estimated motion 0.00 px/frame
```

(All numbers are from our toy run.) Read the four lines together:

- **Frame difference alone is misleading.** It rates the screen-fixed noise (0.0528) as *more* stable than the correct, propagated detail (0.0796), because the propagated detail moves, and it gives the frozen video a perfect zero.
- **Warping error separates the cases that move.** After undoing the estimated motion, only the propagated detail leaves nothing behind; both new-every-frame noise and screen-fixed noise remain visible.
- **A motion measure is needed as well.** The frozen video also has zero warping error. Only the estimated motion (0.00 instead of the requested 2 pixels per frame) exposes it, which is the role of VBench's dynamic degree.

## Autoregressive and diffusion video generation

**Plain version.** There are two ways to produce a clip. A **diffusion** (or flow) model refines all frames of a fixed-length clip together, from noise to video, so every frame can see the future as well as the past. An **autoregressive** model writes the video piece by piece, each piece conditioned on what came before, like a language model writing text; it can go on as long as you like and can stream, but early mistakes stay. Recent hybrids try to get both.

### Token models: masked and autoregressive

With a discrete video tokenizer, video generation becomes sequence modeling ([ML 09](/en/learn/ml/autoregressive-vision/)). **MAGVIT** uses masked token modeling, predicting many tokens in parallel, and supports many generation tasks (such as frame prediction, interpolation and inpainting) with a single model [20]. **Phenaki** generates its causal video tokens with a bidirectional masked transformer conditioned on text [33]. **VideoPoet** is a decoder-only transformer that processes text, image, video and audio tokens; MAGVIT-v2 turns 17 frames of $128 \times 128$ video at 8 frames per second into a $5 \times 16 \times 16$ grid, that is 1,280 tokens from a vocabulary of $2^{18}$, and the model is trained like a language model, with pretraining on a mixture of generative tasks followed by task-specific adaptation [19]. Token models reuse language-model infrastructure (KV caching, [ML 09](/en/learn/ml/autoregressive-vision/#the-kv-cache)) and can extend a clip naturally, but every generated token is a commitment, and errors accumulate ([ML 09](/en/learn/ml/autoregressive-vision/#teacher-forcing-causal-masks-and-exposure-bias)).

### Diffusion: the whole clip at once

A standard video diffusion transformer denoises all latent frames jointly with **bidirectional** attention. CausVid states the consequence plainly: generating a single frame requires processing the entire sequence, including the future, which makes such models hard to use interactively [35]. Their strengths are the ones that made diffusion dominant for images (stable training, high quality, guidance), plus global consistency within the clip. Their limits are a fixed maximum length and high latency.

### Hybrids: causal, streamable diffusion

- **Diffusion Forcing** trains a diffusion model to denoise a sequence of tokens with **independent noise levels per token**, and uses it as a causal next-token model that generates future frames without fully diffusing past ones; this allows rolling out video beyond the training horizon, where its baselines diverge [36].
- **CausVid** adapts a pretrained bidirectional video diffusion transformer into a causal, autoregressive one that generates frames on the fly, distills its 50-step sampler into a 4-step one with a bidirectional teacher supervising the causal student, and uses KV caching for streaming generation; the authors report that this mitigates error accumulation and allows long videos despite training on short clips [35].
- **Self Forcing** attacks **exposure bias** directly: during training, each frame is generated conditioned on the model's own previous outputs (an autoregressive rollout with KV caching), not on ground-truth frames, and a loss is applied to the whole generated video; it reports real-time streaming generation with sub-second latency on a single GPU [37].

| Aspect | Autoregressive or causal | Bidirectional diffusion |
|---|---|---|
| Length | open-ended, chunk by chunk | fixed by the training clip length, unless extended |
| Latency | first frames available early, can stream | whole clip must be denoised first |
| Future information | none (causal) | every frame sees past and future |
| Main risk | error accumulation, exposure bias | memory and compute for long clips |
| Typical use | interactive generation, world models, live video | offline text-to-video and image-to-video |

The same causal-versus-bidirectional distinction matters for world models, where actions arrive one step at a time ([DLCV 13](/en/learn/deep-learning/world-models/#large-generative-world-models)). Remember also, from [ML 08](/en/learn/ml/diffusion-flow-matching/#video-adds-temporal-compression-and-a-consistency-problem), that a causal **autoencoder** does not make a generator autoregressive: the generator's attention and sampling decide that.

## Evaluating video generators

**Plain version.** You need evidence of four things: that the videos as a set look like real videos (distribution metrics such as FVD), that they match their prompts, that their motion and consistency are good (dimension-by-dimension suites such as VBench), and that people prefer them (human studies). The standard distribution metric turns out to look mostly at single frames, so it can miss exactly the temporal problems this chapter is about.

### Fréchet Video Distance

**FVD** extends FID ([ML 08](/en/learn/ml/diffusion-flow-matching/#fréchet-inception-distance)) to video [38]. Each video, real or generated, is passed through a pretrained video network, an I3D action-recognition model [39] trained on Kinetics-400 [40], and its output (the logits) is taken as a feature vector. A Gaussian is fitted to each set of features, and

$$
\mathrm{FVD} = \lVert \mu_r - \mu_g \rVert_2^2 + \operatorname{Tr}\!\Big(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2\big(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g\big)^{1/2}\Big),
$$

where $\mu_r, \Sigma_r$ are the mean and covariance of the real-video features, $\mu_g, \Sigma_g$ those of the generated videos, $\operatorname{Tr}$ is the trace and $(\cdot)^{1/2}$ the matrix square root. Lower is better. The original paper introduced FVD with a human study in which it correlated well with judgments of generated videos [38]. Class-conditional and unconditional benchmarks often report FVD on UCF-101, a dataset of 101 human-action classes [41], [1], [25].

**Its main limit: content bias.** Ge et al. found that FVD increases only slightly under large temporal corruption at matched frame quality, and that a well-chosen subset of generated videos **without motion** can lower FVD drastically [42]. They trace the bias to the features: an action classifier trained on Kinetics learns object and scene appearance, because many actions can be recognized from one frame; features from a self-supervised video model, VideoMAE v2 [43], were much less biased [42]. The pitfalls of FID also apply ([DLCV 12](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/#distribution-metrics-and-their-pitfalls)), plus video-specific ones: clip length, frame rate and resizing to a low, square resolution [42].

*Optional: [Ge et al., "On the Content Bias in Fréchet Video Distance"](#ge-et-al-on-the-content-bias-in-fréchet-video-distance-cvpr-2024) (in the Paper guides). How the paper separates frame quality from motion quality, and how to read its sensitivity plots.*

### Code: a Fréchet distance that cannot see time

The toy makes "real" clips of a blob moving in a straight line and two corrupted sets: one with **blurred frames** (worse frames, correct motion) and one with **shuffled frames** (perfect frames, broken motion). It computes a Fréchet distance with two hand-made feature extractors: one that averages per-frame appearance statistics over time, like an extractor that mostly sees content, and one that adds two temporal statistics.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import linalg, ndimage as ndi

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
T, S = 16, 32
yy, xx = np.mgrid[:S, :S]

def real_videos(n):
    """Toy 'real' clips: a bright blob moving in a straight line, plus mild sensor noise."""
    vids = np.empty((n, T, S, S))
    for i in range(n):
        p, vel, r = rng.uniform(10, 22, 2), rng.uniform(-0.7, 0.7, 2), rng.uniform(2.5, 4)
        for t in range(T):
            cy, cx = p + vel * t
            vids[i, t] = np.exp(-((yy - cy) ** 2 + (xx - cx) ** 2) / (2 * r**2))
    return vids + 0.02 * rng.standard_normal(vids.shape)

def frame_stats(f):                      # appearance of one frame
    gy, gx = np.gradient(f)
    return [f.mean(), f.std(), f.max(), np.abs(gx).mean() + np.abs(gy).mean()]

def appearance_features(v):              # frame statistics averaged over time: blind to frame order
    return np.mean([frame_stats(f) for f in v], axis=0)

def motion_features(v):                  # add how the content changes from frame to frame
    d = np.abs(np.diff(v, axis=0)).mean(axis=(1, 2))
    return np.concatenate([appearance_features(v), [d.mean(), d.std()]])

def frechet(a, b):                       # Fréchet distance between Gaussian fits of two feature sets
    mu1, mu2 = a.mean(0), b.mean(0)
    s1, s2 = np.cov(a, rowvar=False), np.cov(b, rowvar=False)
    covmean = linalg.sqrtm(s1 @ s2).real
    return float(((mu1 - mu2) ** 2).sum() + np.trace(s1 + s2 - 2 * covmean))

real, other_real = real_videos(400), real_videos(400)
blurred = ndi.gaussian_filter(other_real, sigma=(0, 0, 0.7, 0.7))          # worse frames, same motion
shuffled = np.stack([v[rng.permutation(T)] for v in other_real])          # perfect frames, broken motion

for name, feats in [("appearance features", appearance_features), ("+ motion features", motion_features)]:
    f = lambda vids: np.stack([feats(v) for v in vids])
    ref = f(real)
    scale = ref.std(0)                   # put all features on a comparable scale
    fd = {k: frechet(ref / scale, f(v) / scale) for k, v in
          [("second real set", other_real), ("blurred frames", blurred), ("shuffled frames", shuffled)]}
    print(f"{name:20s} " + "   ".join(f"{k} {d:6.2f}" for k, d in fd.items()))
```

It prints:

```text
appearance features  second real set   0.01   blurred frames 115.10   shuffled frames   0.01
+ motion features    second real set   0.07   blurred frames 141.87   shuffled frames 12397.17
```

(All numbers are from our toy run, with toy features, not I3D.) With appearance-only features, the shuffled set, whose motion is destroyed, is exactly as close to the real set as a second real set (0.01), while mild blur is heavily penalized. Adding two temporal statistics makes the shuffled set by far the worst. The real FVD is not this extreme, since I3D does see some motion, but this is the shape of the content bias that Ge et al. measured [42]: **the metric is only as temporal as its features**. ### Benchmark suites and human evaluation

**Plain version.** Instead of one number, modern benchmarks ask many narrow questions, each with its own prompts and its own automatic check, and confirm that the checks agree with people.

- **VBench** splits "video generation quality" into 16 dimensions, each with tailored prompts and an evaluation method: temporal quality (subject consistency, background consistency, temporal flickering, motion smoothness, dynamic degree), frame-wise quality (aesthetic and imaging quality), semantics (object class, multiple objects, human action, color, spatial relationship, scene), style (appearance and temporal style) and overall consistency; it provides human preference annotations for each dimension to validate the automatic scores [34]. **VBench++** extends it to image-to-video, with an image suite of adaptive aspect ratio, and to trustworthiness [44].
- **EvalCrafter** builds 700 prompts from an analysis of real user data, evaluates visual quality, content quality, motion quality and text–video alignment with 17 objective metrics, and fits coefficients so that the combined score agrees with users' opinions [45].
- **Human evaluation** remains the reference. Stable Video Diffusion compares its training choices with human preference studies [4]; HunyuanVideo reports evaluations by professionals [16]; Movie Gen evaluates with pairwise A/B tests by expert annotators along text alignment, visual quality, and realness and aesthetics, over a public set of 1,003 prompts, and reports that FVD and Inception Score did not correlate with its human evaluation of video quality [17]. Open-Sora 2.0 reports both human evaluation and VBench scores [46].

Physical plausibility needs its own tests; [DLCV 13](/en/learn/deep-learning/world-models/#does-realistic-video-mean-physical-understanding) describes benchmarks that found physical understanding unrelated to visual realism.

**A reporting checklist.** State the prompts and how they were chosen, the clip length, frame rate and resolution, the number of samples and seeds, the sampler, steps and guidance (including any schedule across frames), the feature extractor and preprocessing of every distribution metric, the dimension suite and its version, and the human-study protocol (pairwise or absolute, number of annotators, majority rule). Report a temporal measure **together with** a motion measure and a per-frame quality measure, and show uncurated clips.

## Data

**Plain version.** Video models learn appearance, motion and the link to language from their training clips. Video with good captions is much scarcer than captioned images, so systems combine image and video data and spend great effort on filtering and recaptioning clips.

- **Images plus videos.** VDM [6], Phenaki [33] and many later systems train jointly on images and videos; Make-A-Video learns text–visual links only from image–text pairs [1]; the causal "$1 + T$" autoencoders make images one-frame videos [16], [3].
- **Web video collections.** WebVid, web videos with weak captions, was introduced with the Frozen in Time retrieval model (WebVid-2M in the paper; a 10M version was released later, according to its arXiv record) [47], and Make-A-Video was trained on WebVid-10M [1]. **Panda-70M** curates 3.8M high-resolution videos, splits them into semantically consistent clips and captions them with several cross-modality teacher models plus a retrieval model that selects the best caption, giving 70M clips with captions [48].
- **Curation matters.** Stable Video Diffusion's Large Video Dataset starts with 580M annotated clips, forming 212 years of content, then removes nearly static clips with optical-flow scores, clips with much written text by OCR, and clips with low aesthetic scores; models trained on curated subsets were preferred by people over models trained on uncurated data [4]. CogVideoX describes its own video-captioning model as a key part of its data pipeline [2].
- **Evaluation datasets.** UCF-101 [41] and Kinetics [40] are human-action datasets that supply FVD benchmarks and FVD's feature network.

## Risks and responsible use

**Plain version.** Realistic generated video can put words in someone's mouth or show events that never happened. The defenses are the same three as for images (detect fakes, label generated content, and limit misuse at the source), but video is harder: it is re-encoded on every upload, and a few convincing seconds are enough to mislead.

- **Deepfakes and detection.** FaceForensics++ is a benchmark for detecting facial manipulations (four manipulation methods at random compression levels and sizes, over 1.8 million manipulated images, with a hidden test set); its authors found that domain-specific knowledge improves detection even under strong compression and that the detectors outperform human observers on their benchmark [49]. Detectors trained on one generation method can fail on newer ones, so detection is an arms race.
- **Watermarks for video.** **Video Seal** jointly trains a watermark embedder and extractor with transformations such as video codecs applied in between, and propagates the watermark over time so that not every high-resolution frame has to be watermarked [50]. Signed provenance manifests, invisible watermarks and their failure modes are explained in [DLCV 12, Deepfakes, provenance and watermarks](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/#deepfakes-provenance-and-watermarks) and its note [Hard bindings, soft bindings and watermarks](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/notes/hard-bindings-soft-bindings-and-watermarks/); the same lessons apply to video, where re-encoding and cropping are routine.
- **Bias, memorization and consent** follow from the data as for images ([DLCV 12, Risks](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/#risks-and-responsible-use)); people appear in web video far more often than in many image collections, which raises the stakes of consent.
- **False authority.** A generated video that looks physically right is easily taken as evidence. Treat generated clips as illustrations, not records, and see [DLCV 13](/en/learn/deep-learning/world-models/#does-realistic-video-mean-physical-understanding) on why realism is not physical correctness.

## Failure modes

**Plain version.** Today's video generators are good at short, single-shot clips of common scenes. They still fail at keeping things the same over time, at physics, at fine-grained motion and at anything long.

- **Flicker and texture sticking.** Fine detail changes from frame to frame or stays fixed while the scene moves (Figure 4).
- **Identity drift and morphing.** Faces, text and patterns change slowly; two objects merge or one object splits.
- **Object permanence.** Objects vanish when occluded or come back different.
- **Too little motion.** Near-static clips that score well on consistency; dynamic-degree measures exist because of this [34], and image-to-video extension can stagnate [8].
- **Implausible physics and dynamics.** Motion looks fluid but is physically wrong (see [DLCV 13](/en/learn/deep-learning/world-models/#does-realistic-video-mean-physical-understanding)).
- **Drift in long videos.** Error accumulation when extending clip by clip [35], [37]; forgetting of the initial scene [8].
- **Weak control.** Camera paths that are only roughly followed, trajectories that change object shape; MotionCtrl's design goal of appearance-free motion conditions targets exactly this [11].
- **Inherited image failures.** Counting, attribute binding, text rendering and hands fail as in images ([DLCV 12, Failure modes](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-generation/#failure-modes)), now with the extra chance of changing over time.

**How to test.** Build small prompt sets per failure type (fast motion, occlusion, a person turning around, written text, a requested camera move), generate several seeds per prompt, measure consistency **and** motion, inspect motion-compensated x–t slices for flicker and sticking, and watch the clips; a frame grid hides most temporal failures.

[53]** (2024) review OpenAI's Sora, released in February 2024, from public technical reports and reverse engineering: background, related technologies, applications and limitations such as safe and unbiased generation. Because it relies on reverse engineering, read its technical claims as informed hypotheses.

**State of the art, 2023–2026.** Five trends stand out. (1) **Latent transformers with full attention and causal 3D VAEs** became the dominant recipe for large open models (CogVideoX [2], HunyuanVideo [16], Wan [3]), with flow-matching objectives in several of them [16], [17], [3]. (2) **Open models report closing much of the gap** with closed systems in their own human evaluations and VBench scores, and training cost became a research topic: Open-Sora 2.0 reports training a commercial-level model for about \$200k [46]. (3) **Higher compression** reduces tokens so that full attention stays affordable, with the decoder taking over more of the detail [18]. (4) **Causal, few-step, streaming generation** brought interactive and real-time use [35], [37], [36]. (5) **Evaluation moved beyond FVD** toward dimension suites [34], [44], [45] and large human studies [17], with a recognized need for temporally sensitive features [42]. Synchronized audio generation also appeared [17], [19].

**Open problems.** Consistency and identity over minutes rather than seconds; physically correct dynamics and interaction ([DLCV 13](/en/learn/deep-learning/world-models/#does-realistic-video-mean-physical-understanding)); precise, disentangled control of camera, objects and timing; metrics that measure motion and consistency without rewarding stillness and that agree with people; the cost of training and sampling long, high-resolution clips; caption quality for events and actions; and provenance that survives re-encoding.

## Key takeaways

- Video generation adds three demands to image generation: per-frame quality, believable motion and temporal consistency; easy fixes for one (a frozen video) fail another.
- Generators work in a compressed latent. Causal 3D VAEs compress time (often 4×) and space (often 8×) with a "$1 + T$" layout so that images are one-frame videos; compression factors and patch size set the token count, and the decoder caps detail.
- Inflating an image model (temporal layers initialized to do nothing at first) reuses appearance knowledge; factorized attention is cheap, full spatiotemporal attention follows large motion, and stronger compression makes full attention affordable.
- Text enters through cross-attention or joint attention as for images; image-to-video adds the first frame as a condition; camera and motion control add pose or trajectory inputs such as Plücker embeddings.
- Long videos come from chunk-wise extension, coarse-to-fine hierarchies or noise rescheduling; all fight error accumulation.
- Bidirectional diffusion generates a fixed clip jointly; autoregressive and causal-diffusion models stream and extend but must handle exposure bias.
- FVD is biased toward per-frame content; evaluate with a temporal measure, a motion measure, dimension suites such as VBench and human studies together.
- Data curation (motion, text and aesthetic filters, recaptioning) is decisive; deepfakes and provenance are real risks, and watermarks for video must survive re-encoding.

### Causal 3D convolutions and the 1 + T frame layout

This note explains how a causal temporal convolution is padded, why the first frame ends up encoded on its own, and how strided layers produce the "$1 + T/f_t$" latent length.

**Ordinary versus causal padding.** Consider one temporal convolution with kernel size $k_t$ along the frame axis (the spatial part is unchanged). An ordinary "same" convolution pads $\lfloor (k_t-1)/2 \rfloor$ frames before the clip and $\lfloor k_t/2 \rfloor$ after it, so output frame $t$ depends on input frames $t - \lfloor (k_t-1)/2 \rfloor$ to $t + \lfloor k_t/2 \rfloor$, including future frames. A **causal** convolution pads $k_t - 1$ frames before the clip and none after, so output $t$ depends only on frames $t - k_t + 1$ to $t$ [15], [2].

**Why the first frame is independent.** With causal padding, output frame 0 sees only padding and input frame 0. Stacking causal layers keeps this property, so the latent of the first frame is a function of the first frame alone. That is what lets the same encoder tokenize a single image [15].

**Strided downsampling.** A causal convolution with stride $s$ maps $1 + s\,t$ input frames to $1 + t$ outputs; the decoder upsamples by $s$ and drops the first $s - 1$ resulting frames to map $1 + t$ back to $1 + s\,t$ [15]. Two such stages with $s = 2$ give $f_t = 4$: 17 frames become 5 latent frames, 81 become 21.

**Chunked encoding of long videos.** Because a causal layer needs only the last $k_t - 1$ frames of context, a long video can be encoded or decoded chunk by chunk while caching those frames. Wan's VAE processes one chunk per latent frame (at most 4 frames) and keeps, for a kernel of size 3, the features of the last two frames of the previous chunk, which supports encoding and decoding of arbitrarily long videos with bounded memory [3]. CogVideoX uses the same property to split long videos across devices, each device sending only $k_t - 1$ frames to the next [2].

**A tested check.** The snippet implements one causal temporal convolution with kernel 4 and stride 4 on 17 "frames" (scalars, for clarity) and confirms the output length and that no output depends on a later frame.

```python
import numpy as np

def causal_conv1d(x, w, stride):
    """Causal temporal convolution: pad len(w) - 1 zeros in front, none behind."""
    k = len(w)
    xp = np.concatenate([np.zeros(k - 1), x])
    return np.array([xp[i * stride:i * stride + k] @ w for i in range((len(x) - 1) // stride + 1)])

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x, w = rng.normal(size=17), rng.normal(size=4)
y = causal_conv1d(x, w, stride=4)
print("17 frames ->", len(y), "latent frames")
for j in [0, 1, 4, 5, 16]:                     # perturb one frame, see which outputs change
    x2 = x.copy(); x2[j] += 1.0
    changed = np.flatnonzero(~np.isclose(causal_conv1d(x2, w, 4), y))
    print(f"frame {j:2d} changes latent frames {changed.tolist()}")
```

It prints:

```text
17 frames -> 5 latent frames
frame  0 changes latent frames [0]
frame  1 changes latent frames [1]
frame  4 changes latent frames [1]
frame  5 changes latent frames [2]
frame 16 changes latent frames [4]
```

Frame 0 influences only latent frame 0, and each later group of four frames influences only its own latent frame, as in Figure 1. Real encoders stack several layers with overlapping kernels, so a latent frame also sees some earlier groups, but never later ones.

**Pitfalls.** Frame counts that do not fit $1 + s\,t$ must be padded or trimmed; Open-Sora reports that training its autoencoder only on 17-frame clips caused blurriness at other lengths, which mixed-length training fixed [22]. Normalization layers that pool statistics over time break causality; Wan replaced group normalization with RMS normalization for this reason [3].

### Four ways to start a video model from an image model

This note compares, system by system, how four video generators reuse a pretrained image model: what they freeze, what they add and how the new parts are initialized.

All four follow the main text's rule: the new temporal parts must leave the image model's behavior unchanged at the start of training. They differ in what they keep frozen and in how the "do nothing" start is implemented.

- **Make-A-Video** [1] stacks a 1D temporal convolution after each pretrained 2D convolution ("pseudo-3D convolution") and a temporal attention layer after each pretrained spatial attention layer. The new 1D layers are initialized as the **identity** function, so at initialization the network generates separate images that each match the text but are not coherent in time. Spatial and temporal layers are then trained together on video.
- **Video LDM** [7] **freezes** the spatial layers of a pretrained latent image model and trains only the inserted temporal layers (temporal attention and 3D-convolutional residual blocks). After each temporal layer, its output $z'$ is mixed with the spatial output $z$ as $\alpha z + (1 - \alpha) z'$, where $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ is learned; with $\alpha = 1$ the temporal layers are skipped and the image model is recovered exactly. Because the spatial layers are untouched, the trained temporal layers also transfer to fine-tuned versions of the same image model.
- **AnimateDiff** [21] trains in stages. First a **domain adapter** (LoRA layers in the attention of the image model, trained on single frames of the video data) absorbs the visual quality gap between the image training data and the video data, such as motion blur, compression artifacts and watermarks, so that this gap is not learned as part of the motion; it is dropped at inference. Then the model is inflated and a **motion module** (temporal transformer blocks with sinusoidal position encoding along time) is trained with the image weights frozen; its output projection is **zero-initialized** and added through a residual connection. Optionally, **MotionLoRA** adapts the motion module to a new motion pattern. Because the image layers never change, the motion module can be plugged into personalized models derived from the same base model.
- **Open-Sora** [22] starts from a pretrained image diffusion transformer and adds a temporal attention layer to each block; the projections of the new layers are initialized to zero to preserve the image generation ability at the start; the added temporal attention doubles the parameter count.

| System | Image layers during video training | New temporal parts | "Do nothing" start |
|---|---|---|---|
| Make-A-Video | trained | 1D temporal convolution and temporal attention per layer | identity initialization |
| Video LDM | frozen | temporal attention and 3D-convolution blocks | learned mix $\alpha$, skip with $\alpha = 1$ |
| AnimateDiff | frozen | motion module (temporal transformer) | zero-initialized output projection |
| Open-Sora | trained | temporal attention per block | zero-initialized projections |

**Pitfalls.** Freezing the image layers keeps the model compatible with the image ecosystem (personalized checkpoints, LoRAs) but limits how much appearance can adapt to video, such as motion blur or video-specific framing. Training everything adapts better but loses that compatibility. In both cases a frame-wise image autoencoder may still cause flicker at decoding time, which Video LDM addressed by fine-tuning the decoder on video (main text).

### Reconstruction guidance for extending a video

This note derives the correction that VDM adds when a clip is extended by sampling new frames conditioned on known ones.

**Setting.** Split a clip into known frames $x^a$ (for example the last frames of the previous chunk) and frames to generate $x^b$. A diffusion model trained on whole clips $x = [x^a, x^b]$ gives a denoising estimate $\hat x_\theta(z_t) = [\hat x^a_\theta(z_t), \hat x^b_\theta(z_t)]$ of the clean clip from the noisy clip $z_t$ at noise level $t$, where $z_t = \alpha_t x + \sigma_t \epsilon$ with schedule coefficients $\alpha_t, \sigma_t$ and Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ (notation of [ML 08](/en/learn/ml/diffusion-flow-matching/#forward-diffusion-has-a-closed-form)).

**The replacement method and its flaw.** The simple approach replaces, at every sampling step, the noisy known part $z^a_t$ by a freshly noised copy of $x^a$, and updates $z^b_t$ as usual. The update of $z^b$ then follows $\hat x^b_\theta(z_t) \approx \mathbb E[x^b \mid z_t]$, while the correct target is $\mathbb E[x^b \mid z_t, x^a]$. In the VDM experiments, the generated frames looked good alone but were often not coherent with the known frames [6].

**The correction.** VDM writes the missing part with the score of $q(x^a \mid z_t)$ and approximates that distribution by a Gaussian centered at the model's own reconstruction of the known frames, $q(x^a \mid z_t) \approx \mathcal N\big(\hat x^a_\theta(z_t), (\sigma_t^2/\alpha_t^2) I\big)$. With a weighting factor $w_r$ the adjusted estimate is [6]

$$
\tilde x^b_\theta(z_t) = \hat x^b_\theta(z_t) - \frac{w_r \alpha_t}{2}\, \nabla_{z^b_t} \big\lVert x^a - \hat x^a_\theta(z_t) \big\rVert_2^2 .
$$

The gradient asks: how should the noisy new frames change so that the model's reconstruction of the **known** frames gets closer to the real known frames? It flows through the network, because the model reconstructs all frames jointly. The authors found that a weight $w_r \gt 1$ tends to improve sample quality, as with other forms of guidance [6].

**Uses.** The same formula extends a clip in time (known frames before), interpolates in time (known frames at a lower frame rate) and, with the error measured on a downsampled prediction, upsamples in space [6]. It needs one extra backward pass through the network per step.

**Edge cases.** The Gaussian approximation is exact only as $t \to 0$ for a perfect model; at high noise, the reconstruction is blurry and the guidance is weak or misleading, so the weight matters. Conditioning on many known frames costs memory, because they are processed together with the new frames.

### Camera poses as Plücker embeddings

This note explains the per-pixel camera representation used by CameraCtrl [12] and what its six numbers encode.

**Camera parameters.** A pinhole camera has intrinsics $K \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ (focal lengths and principal point) and extrinsics: a rotation $R \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ and a translation; let $o \in \mathbb{R}^3$ be the camera center in world coordinates. CameraCtrl argues that feeding these raw numbers to a generator works poorly: the translation has no fixed scale while the rotation is constrained, and raw matrices are hard to relate to image positions [12].

**One ray per pixel.** Pixel $(u, v)$ sees the world along a ray from $o$ in the direction $d_{u,v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, obtained by back-projecting the pixel with $K^{-1}$, rotating it into world coordinates and normalizing it to unit length. The **Plücker embedding** of that pixel is

$$
p_{u,v} = \big(o \times d_{u,v},\ d_{u,v}\big) \in \mathbb{R}^6 ,
$$

where $\times$ is the cross product. The second half is the ray's direction; the first half, the **moment**, encodes where the ray is (it is perpendicular to the plane containing the ray and the world origin, and its length is the ray's distance from the origin). Two rays with the same direction but different positions have different moments. Stacking $p_{u,v}$ over all pixels gives a $6 \times h \times w$ map per frame and an $n \times 6 \times h \times w$ tensor for a clip of $n$ frames, which a convolutional or attention module can align with the video latents pixel by pixel [12].

**A tested check.** The snippet computes the embedding for a small pinhole camera, then moves the camera forward and rotates it, and reports which half of the embedding changes.

```python
import numpy as np

def plucker(K, R, o, h=4, w=6):
    """Per-pixel Plücker embedding (moment, direction) for a camera with world rotation R and center o."""
    v, u = np.mgrid[:h, :w] + 0.5
    pix = np.stack([u, v, np.ones_like(u)], -1).reshape(-1, 3)
    d = (R @ np.linalg.inv(K) @ pix.T).T             # ray directions in world coordinates
    d /= np.linalg.norm(d, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return np.cross(o, d), d                         # (h*w, 3) moments and (h*w, 3) directions

K = np.array([[5.0, 0, 3], [0, 5.0, 2], [0, 0, 1]])
I, o = np.eye(3), np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
yaw = np.deg2rad(10)
Ry = np.array([[np.cos(yaw), 0, np.sin(yaw)], [0, 1, 0], [-np.sin(yaw), 0, np.cos(yaw)]])

m0, d0 = plucker(K, I, o)
m1, d1 = plucker(K, I, o + np.array([0, 0, 0.5]))   # move forward along the viewing axis
m2, d2 = plucker(K, Ry, o)                           # turn the camera by 10 degrees
print(f"move forward: direction change {np.abs(d1 - d0).max():.3f}, moment change {np.abs(m1 - m0).max():.3f}")
print(f"turn 10 deg:  direction change {np.abs(d2 - d0).max():.3f}, moment change {np.abs(m2 - m0).max():.3f}")
```

It prints:

```text
move forward: direction change 0.000, moment change 0.223
turn 10 deg:  direction change 0.173, moment change 0.091
```

Moving the camera changes only the moments (where the rays are); turning it changes the directions (and, because the center is off the origin, the moments too). The embedding therefore separates the two kinds of camera motion in a form that is attached to every pixel. Note that the overall scale of the scene is still undetermined from video alone, so camera paths estimated from real clips carry an arbitrary scale that the training data must normalize.

### Blattmann et al., "Stable Video Diffusion: Scaling Latent Video Diffusion Models to Large Datasets" (2023)

#### Challenge

By 2023 many groups turned image latent diffusion models into video models by inserting temporal layers and fine-tuning on small, high-quality video sets, but training recipes varied widely and there was no agreed strategy for curating video data [4].

<Figure src={paperSvdSource} alt="Compare image pretraining and video-data curation through the shown human-preference tests; these are training-stage interventions, not a diagram of a new video sampler." caption="Original paper figure 3 — Compare image pretraining and video-data curation through the shown human-preference tests; these are training-stage interventions, not a diagram of a new video sampler." />

*Figure source: [4], arXiv:2311.15127v1, PDF page 4. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.15127).*

#### Method

Treat training as three explicit stages and study each: (I) text-to-image pretraining (the model starts from a pretrained image latent diffusion model [14]); (II) video pretraining on large amounts of curated video; (III) fine-tuning on a small subset of high-quality videos at higher resolution. For stage II, build the Large Video Dataset (580M annotated clip pairs, 212 years of content) and annotate every clip with dense optical flow, OCR text detection, aesthetic scores and image–text similarities, plus synthetic captions, so that filters can be chosen systematically.

#### Results

An image-pretrained video model was preferred by people over the same model trained from scratch, in both quality and prompt following. Curated pretraining subsets (static clips, text-heavy clips and low-aesthetic clips removed; thresholds selected by human-preference ranking of small models) beat uncurated data of the same size, even when four times smaller, and the advantage persisted after stage III fine-tuning. The resulting base model gave a text-to-video model competitive with closed models in human studies, a strong image-to-video model (14 and 25 frames; image embedding instead of text, noise-augmented frame concatenated to the input, guidance increasing linearly over frames), camera-motion LoRAs, a frame-interpolation model and a multi-view 3D prior.

#### Reading the evidence

Read Section 3 (the three stages and the curation study) first; most of its evidence is in plots of human preference (Elo or win rates), not tables of FVD. Check: (a) which comparisons are made at small scale (most curation ablations) and which with the final model; (b) that human studies compare against specific closed systems at one point in time; (c) the appendix, which holds the filter thresholds, model details and the evaluation protocol.

#### Problems

The dataset is not released, so the curation results cannot be reproduced exactly; human-preference studies depend on prompts and annotators; the autoencoder compresses only in space, which keeps the number of latent frames equal to the number of frames.

#### Conclusion

It is the clearest documented case for the chapter's point that video data curation (especially removing static clips) and staged training are as important as architecture, and it is the reference design for image-to-video conditioning.

### Ge et al., "On the Content Bias in Fréchet Video Distance" (CVPR 2024)

#### Challenge

FVD is the most common distribution metric for video generation, but several papers had reported cases where it contradicted human judgment, with a recurring suspicion that it values the quality of individual frames over the realism of motion [42].

<Figure src={paperFvdBiasSource} alt="Compare spatially corrupted videos with videos whose corruption also changes over time; different feature extractors can respond differently to temporal damage." caption="Original paper figure 2 — Compare spatially corrupted videos with videos whose corruption also changes over time; different feature extractors can respond differently to temporal damage." />

*Figure source: [42], Published paper PDF, PDF page 2. [Original PDF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Ge_On_the_Content_Bias_in_Frechet_Video_Distance_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf).*

#### Method

Measure the bias in controlled settings. Starting from real videos, create two distorted sets with matched per-frame quality: one with the **same** distortion applied to every frame (spatial only) and one with a **different** distortion per frame (spatiotemporal, which adds temporal inconsistency). Matched frame quality is confirmed with FID on the extracted frames; the temporal sensitivity of FVD is then the ratio of its change to the change in FID. Second, probe the metric's "perceptual null space": choose, from many generated videos without motion, a subset that minimizes FVD. Third, swap the I3D features for features of self-supervised video models (VideoMAE v2 [43]) and repeat.

#### Results

With I3D features, FVD increases only slightly under large temporal corruption, and selecting static generated videos can decrease FVD drastically without improving temporal quality, both signs of a bias toward per-frame content. The bias was attributed to the features of a supervised action classifier trained on Kinetics, a dataset in which many actions can be recognized from appearance alone. FVD computed with self-supervised VideoMAE v2 features was much more sensitive to temporal quality in all experiments.

#### Reading the evidence

Start with Figure 1 (the motivating example) and the experimental design figure, then the sensitivity plots, comparing I3D with VideoMAE v2 variants per distortion type and dataset. Check: (a) that the conclusions are about relative sensitivity, not absolute FVD values, which are not comparable across feature extractors; (b) which VideoMAE v2 variant (pretrained only, or fine-tuned on an action dataset) each curve uses; (c) the real-world examples at the end, where the analysis is applied to published models.

#### Problems

The authors note that FVD still requires resizing to a low, square resolution, that long videos remain hard to evaluate because the transformer features have quadratic cost, and that their study covers a set of distortions and datasets, not every failure of generated video.

#### Conclusion

It explains why this chapter insists on temporal and motion measures next to FVD, and why a feature extractor must be reported with every FVD number.
