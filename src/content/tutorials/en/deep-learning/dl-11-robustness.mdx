## What you'll learn

- How to tell apart the three robustness problems of a vision model: **distribution shift** (the data changed), **adversarial examples** (someone changed the input on purpose) and **out-of-distribution or anomalous inputs** (the input does not belong to the task at all), and why each needs its own test.
- How shift is described (covariate, label and concept shift), which benchmarks measure it (corruptions, renditions, natural adversarial images, controlled and in-the-wild datasets), and what the evidence says helps: augmentation, more diverse pre-training, test-time adaptation, domain adaptation and domain generalization.
- How an adversarial threat model is stated (an $\ell_p$ ball, white-box or black-box access), how **FGSM** and **PGD** work in equations and NumPy, what **adversarial training** buys and costs, why weak evaluations overstate robustness (gradient masking), and what a **certificate** from randomized smoothing guarantees.
- How out-of-distribution detection and anomaly detection are defined, how the common scores work (**maximum softmax probability, energy, Mahalanobis distance, k-nearest-neighbour distance**), how they are measured (**AUROC, FPR at 95% TPR, AUPR**), and how industrial defect detection with a **patch memory bank** (the idea behind PatchCore) works.
- Which pitfalls make robustness numbers misleading: near- versus far-OOD, contaminated OOD test sets, model selection on the test distribution, and gains that hold for one kind of shift only.

## The big picture

A model that scores 98% on its test set has only been checked on data that looks like its training data. Deployed models meet other data: photos from a new camera, images edited by an attacker, and objects that were never part of the label set. Robustness is the study of what happens then, and of how to make the answer less alarming.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Think of a student who learned everything from one textbook. Three things can go wrong in the exam. First, the exam is printed in a smudged, faded font: the questions are the same kind, but they look different (**distribution shift**). Second, a prankster changes a few letters in a question so that the student misreads it, choosing exactly the letters that confuse this student most (**adversarial example**). Third, a question from a completely different subject appears: the right answer is "this is not my subject", but the student writes an answer anyway, confidently (**out-of-distribution input**). A good student copes with the smudges, is hard to trick, and knows when to say "I don't know".
</Callout>

**Why it matters.** The three failures cause different damage. Shift silently lowers accuracy in the field, so a model that looked finished keeps making more mistakes after release. Adversarial examples are a security problem: any system whose decision is worth manipulating (content filters, identity checks, traffic-sign recognition) will be attacked. Unanswerable inputs produce confident nonsense, which is the most dangerous output a safety-critical system can give. The fixes differ too, so the first practical skill is to name which of the three problems you face.

## Three robustness problems, one map

**Plain version.** Ask three questions about a failure. Did the data change while the task stayed the same? Did someone change this one input on purpose, by a tiny amount? Is the input outside the task, so that no label is correct? These are distribution shift, adversarial examples and out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs.

**Precise version.** Let a classifier $f$ be trained on samples from a distribution $p_{\text{train}}(\mathbf{x}, y)$ over images $\mathbf{x}$ and labels $y \in \mathcal{Y} = \{1, \dots, K\}$. Write $\operatorname{acc}_p(f) = \Pr_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim p}[f(\mathbf{x}) = y]$ for its accuracy on a distribution $p$.

1. **Distribution shift.** Test data come from $p_{\text{test}} \neq p_{\text{train}}$, but the label set $\mathcal{Y}$ is the same. The question is how much $\operatorname{acc}_{p_{\text{test}}}(f)$ falls below $\operatorname{acc}_{p_{\text{train}}}(f)$, and how to reduce the fall.
2. **Adversarial robustness.** For each test pair $(\mathbf{x}, y)$, an adversary may replace $\mathbf{x}$ by any $\mathbf{x}'$ in an allowed set $\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x})$, usually a small ball around $\mathbf{x}$. The model is robust at $(\mathbf{x}, y)$ if $f(\mathbf{x}') = y$ for **every** $\mathbf{x}' \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x})$. The question is about the worst case, not the average.
3. **OOD and anomaly detection.** Some test inputs have no correct label in $\mathcal{Y}$ (a new object category), or come from a region of input space the training data never covered (a defective part, a corrupted frame). We want a score $s(\mathbf{x})$ that is high for in-distribution (ID) inputs and low for these, so that the system can abstain or raise an alarm.

| | Distribution shift | Adversarial example | OOD / anomaly |
|---|---|---|---|
| What changed | the data distribution | one input, on purpose | the input is outside the task |
| Who causes it | the world (camera, place, time) | an adversary with a goal | the world (new classes, defects) |
| What we want | keep accuracy | keep the label under every allowed change | flag the input, do not answer |
| Typical metric | accuracy on shifted test sets | robust accuracy at a budget $\varepsilon$ | AUROC, FPR at 95% TPR |
| In this chapter | [Distribution shift](#distribution-shift) | [Adversarial examples](#adversarial-examples) | [OOD and anomaly detection](#out-of-distribution-and-anomaly-detection) |

The three overlap at the edges. A heavy corruption can make an input effectively OOD; an adversarial example is a shift chosen by an opponent; and ImageNet-A, a set of natural photos selected because they fool a classifier [1], sits between shift and adversarial examples. Still, the table shows why one robustness number can never cover all three: they ask different questions and need different test data.

## Distribution shift

**Plain version.** The test data are of the same kind as the training data (the same classes, the same task) but look different: other cameras, lighting, styles, places or times. Accuracy drops, often a lot, and the model gives no warning.

### Kinds of shift

The usual vocabulary splits the joint distribution in two ways, $p(\mathbf{x}, y) = p(y \mid \mathbf{x})\, p(\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)\, p(y)$, and names the shift by which factor changes:

- **Covariate shift:** $p(\mathbf{x})$ changes, $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ does not. A new scanner, fog, a different hospital's staining.
- **Label (prior) shift:** $p(y)$ changes, $p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$ does not. A rare disease becomes common during an outbreak.
- **Concept shift:** $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ itself changes. The definition of a class is revised.

[ML 11](/en/learn/ml/evaluation-deployment/#three-kinds-of-shift) develops these with importance weights and the label-shift correction, so we do not repeat them. Two more terms are common in vision. **Domain shift** is covariate shift between named data sources (synthetic versus real, one hospital versus another), and **subpopulation shift** keeps the same kinds of images but changes how often each group appears; WILDS contains both [2]. The survey by Yang et al. adds a distinction that becomes important later in this chapter: a **covariate shift** changes how inputs look while the labels stay valid, whereas a **semantic shift** introduces inputs whose labels are outside $\mathcal{Y}$ [3]. We want models to be *robust* to covariate shift and to *detect* semantic shift.

### Benchmarks that measure robustness to shift

**Plain version.** Because the real deployment data are rarely available in advance, researchers built test sets that change one thing at a time, or that collect naturally different photos of the same classes.

The main families, all labelled with ImageNet classes unless noted:

- **Synthetic corruptions.** **ImageNet-C** applies noise, blur, weather and digital corruptions at five severities, and its authors also proposed ImageNet-P for perturbation sequences [4]. The details and the mean corruption error are in [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#distribution-shift-imagenet-c--r-and--a).
- **Renditions and style.** **ImageNet-R** collects art, cartoons, sculptures and other renditions; the same paper introduced further real-world shift datasets and found that methods that helped with texture and local image statistics did not help with others, such as geographic change [5].
- **Natural adversarial images.** **ImageNet-A** keeps unmodified photos that a ResNet-50 got wrong; they transfer to other models, showing shared weaknesses [1].
- **Controlled collection.** **ObjectNet** photographs objects with randomized backgrounds, rotations and viewpoints, as a test set only, with no training split [6].
- **Re-collection.** **ImageNetV2** rebuilt the test set by following the original collection process; accuracy dropped although the procedure was copied as closely as possible [7] (see [ML 11](/en/learn/ml/evaluation-deployment/#benchmarks-that-probe-shift)).
- **In the wild.** **WILDS** gathers ten datasets with shifts that arose in real applications, such as tissue slides from different hospitals and camera traps at new locations, and reports that standard training gives much lower out-of-distribution than in-distribution performance, with existing robustness methods not closing the gap [2].

**How to read the numbers.** A model with higher clean accuracy usually also has higher shifted accuracy, so "it scores better on ImageNet-R" may only mean "it is a better model". Taori et al. evaluated 204 ImageNet models in 213 test conditions and proposed **effective robustness**: accuracy on the shifted set *beyond* what a fitted trend over standard models predicts from the clean accuracy [8]. Miller et al. found that, for many shifts, shifted and clean accuracy lie close to a straight line across architectures and training choices ("accuracy on the line"), with weaker correlation for some synthetic shifts and for one medical dataset [9]. The consequence for practice: compare robustness interventions at matched clean accuracy, or report both numbers side by side.

### Code: corruptions, augmentation and test-time normalization

The toy trains a small MLP on scikit-learn's 8×8 digits and corrupts the *test* images with three corruptions at severities 1 to 5. The network standardizes each pixel with statistics computed on the training set, the way a batch-normalization layer uses running statistics at test time. We compare three models: the plain one; one trained with noise augmentation (a copy of every training image with random-severity noise); and the plain model with **test-batch normalization**, which recomputes the per-pixel mean and standard deviation on the corrupted test batch instead of using the training statistics.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
X = X / 16.0                                          # 8x8 images, pixels in [0, 1]
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=0)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

corruptions = {                                       # severity s = 1..5
    "noise":    lambda X, s: np.clip(X + rng.normal(0, 0.1 * s, X.shape), 0, 1),
    "blur":     lambda X, s: gaussian_filter(X.reshape(-1, 8, 8), (0, 0.25 * s, 0.25 * s)).reshape(-1, 64),
    "contrast": lambda X, s: 0.5 + (X - 0.5) * (1 - 0.15 * s),
}

def norm(X, ref):                                     # standardize each pixel with ref's statistics
    return (X - ref.mean(0)) / (ref.std(0) + 0.05)

def fit(X, y):
    return MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(64,), alpha=1e-3, max_iter=400, random_state=0).fit(norm(X, Xtr), y)

plain = fit(Xtr, ytr)
s_rand = rng.integers(1, 6, (len(Xtr), 1))            # random noise severity per training image
augmented = fit(np.vstack([Xtr, corruptions["noise"](Xtr, s_rand)]), np.r_[ytr, ytr])

for name, corrupt in corruptions.items():
    rows = {"plain": [], "noise-aug": [], "test-norm": []}
    for s in range(6):
        Xs = Xte if s == 0 else corrupt(Xte, s)
        rows["plain"].append(plain.score(norm(Xs, Xtr), yte))
        rows["noise-aug"].append(augmented.score(norm(Xs, Xtr), yte))
        rows["test-norm"].append(plain.score(norm(Xs, Xs), yte))   # statistics of the test batch
    for k, v in rows.items():
        print(f"{name:8s} {k:9s}", " ".join(f"{a:.2f}" for a in v))
```

(All numbers are from our toy run.) Four lessons carry over to real networks:

- **Mild-looking changes are enough.** A contrast reduction to 70% of the original range (severity 2) halves the plain model's accuracy, although a person reads the digits easily.
- **Augmentation helps, and not only for the corruption it used.** Training with noise also helped with blur and contrast here, which is the hope behind diverse augmentation. It cost a little clean accuracy (0.98 to 0.97), and real benchmarks show that the transfer to *other* shifts is far from guaranteed [5], [8].
- **Re-estimating normalization statistics is a cheap and strong fix when its assumption holds.** The contrast corruption is an affine change of every pixel, which per-pixel standardization undoes exactly, so accuracy stays at 0.98. Noise changes the statistics in a way standardization cannot undo, so the gain is smaller.
- **Test-batch normalization needs a batch.** It used all 540 test images of one corruption at once. With a few images, or with a batch that mixes several conditions, the statistics are wrong (Exercise 1).

### What helps, and what does not

**Plain version.** Five families of fixes exist. Train on more varied images; start from a model pre-trained on very diverse data; adapt the model to the test data while it runs; adapt it in advance with unlabeled data from the target domain; or train on several domains and hope to generalize to new ones. Each has a cost and a condition under which it works.

**Data augmentation.** Augmentation makes the training distribution wider so that the test distribution is more likely to fall inside it. **AugMix** mixes several chains of random augmentations of one image, and adds a Jensen–Shannon consistency loss that asks the network to give similar predictions for the original and the augmented versions [10]. Its authors deliberately removed operations that overlap with ImageNet-C corruptions, so the benchmark measures transfer to unseen corruptions rather than memorization of the test corruptions [10]. Stylized training, which replaces image textures with painting styles, made a ResNet-50 rely more on shape and improved robustness to many distortions [11]. The caution comes from the ImageNet-R study: augmentation and larger models helped on some real-world shifts (style, blur) but not on others (geography), and no evaluated method consistently improved robustness across all of them [5]. General augmentation recipes are in [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#data-augmentation).

**Pre-training on more diverse data.** In the testbed of Taori et al., most robustness interventions gave little or no effective robustness on natural shifts; the main exception was training on larger and more diverse data [8]. Zero-shot CLIP models, trained on image–text pairs from the web, improved effective robustness by a large amount on natural ImageNet shifts, but the CLIP paper also showed that the benefit almost vanished when a classifier was fitted on ImageNet in a fully supervised way [12]. Fang et al. tested five possible causes (dataset size, training distribution, language supervision during training, language supervision at test time, contrastive loss) and found that the **more diverse training distribution** was the main cause [13]. Fine-tuning can therefore erode robustness; WiSE-FT averages the weights of the zero-shot and fine-tuned models to keep most of both [14] (see also LP-FT in [ML 10](/en/learn/ml/foundation-models/#fine-tuning-lp-ft-and-robust-fine-tuning)).

**Test-time adaptation (TTA).** If unlabeled test images arrive in batches, the model can adjust itself before predicting. The simplest form is the one in our toy: Schneider et al. replaced the batch-normalization statistics of 25 pretrained models with statistics of the corrupted test images and improved corruption robustness consistently, even when adapting with a single sample [15]. **TENT** goes one step further: it re-estimates the normalization statistics *and* updates the channel-wise affine parameters of the normalization layers online, by minimizing the entropy of the model's own predictions on each test batch [16]. TTA assumes a stream of test data from a reasonably stable shift; it adds computation at test time and can drift if the stream changes.

*Optional: [Test-time entropy minimization](#test-time-entropy-minimization) (in the Supplementary notes). The TENT objective, which parameters it changes and why, and the failure cases to watch for.*

**Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA).** If unlabeled images from the target domain are available *during training*, the model can be trained to work there without target labels. **Domain-adversarial training (DANN)** adds a small domain classifier on top of the features and a **gradient-reversal layer** between them, so that the features become good for the label task on the labelled source domain and, at the same time, useless for telling source from target [17]. The idea rests on domain-adaptation theory: if a classifier cannot tell the domains apart in feature space, a good source classifier should transfer.

*Optional: [Domain-adversarial training and the gradient-reversal layer](#domain-adversarial-training-and-the-gradient-reversal-layer) (in the Supplementary notes). The DANN objective as a min-max problem, the layer that implements it in one line, and when aligning features hurts.*

**Domain generalization (DG).** Without any target data, the model is trained on several source domains in the hope that it works on an unseen one. The survey by Zhou et al. groups DG methods into domain alignment, meta-learning, data augmentation and ensemble learning, among others [18]. Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz then reran many DG algorithms in one testbed, DomainBed, with equal tuning effort and explicit model-selection rules, and found that plain empirical risk minimization (ERM, ordinary training on the pooled sources) with a strong network and augmentation matched or beat them [19]. Their key point is methodological: a DG method is incomplete without a rule for choosing hyperparameters that does not peek at the test domain.

*Optional: [Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz, "In Search of Lost Domain Generalization"](#gulrajani-and-lopez-paz-in-search-of-lost-domain-generalization-iclr-2021) (in the Paper guides). How DomainBed's three model-selection rules work and how to read its tables.*

**What does not help, or not reliably.** Robustness on synthetic corruptions was only weakly predictive of robustness on natural shifts in the testbed of Taori et al., and the same held for $\ell_p$ adversarial robustness [8]. A method tuned on one benchmark often helps only that benchmark [5]. Architecture changes at fixed data have mattered less than the data itself [8], [13]. The practical rule follows: test on several kinds of shift, including at least one real shift from your own deployment.

### Confidence under shift

**Plain version.** Under shift, a model is wrong more often, but it is usually not *less confident* by the same amount, so its probabilities stop meaning what they say.

Ovadia et al. benchmarked uncertainty methods under dataset shift on several tasks and found that calibration degrades as shift grows, that post-hoc calibration such as temperature scaling fitted on in-distribution validation data falls short under shift, and that methods that average over several models (such as deep ensembles) held up best [20]. Temperature scaling is explained in [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#calibration-and-temperature-scaling), and [ML 11](/en/learn/ml/evaluation-deployment/#code-temperature-scaling-and-what-shift-does-to-it) shows on a toy how a fitted temperature stops working under shift. The practical consequence: a confidence threshold chosen on clean validation data will accept more wrong answers in the field, so monitor the distribution of confidences after deployment ([ML 11](/en/learn/ml/evaluation-deployment/#monitoring-after-deployment)).

## Adversarial examples

**Plain version.** For almost any correctly classified image, there is a nearby image, often indistinguishable to a person, that the network classifies wrongly. An attacker finds it by asking the network itself which direction to move each pixel.

### What an adversarial example is

Szegedy et al. reported that deep networks can be made to misclassify an image by an imperceptible perturbation found by maximizing the network's prediction error, and that the same perturbation often fools a different network trained on a different subset of the data [21]. Goodfellow et al. explained the effect by the near-linearity of networks in high dimensions and introduced the one-step fast gradient sign method [22], which [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#adversarial-examples-fgsm) derives and tests. This section adds the parts needed to *evaluate* and *improve* robustness: threat models, the multi-step attack, adversarial training, honest evaluation and certificates.

<Lab id="adversarial-budget" />

### Threat models

**Plain version.** "Robust" means nothing until you say robust to what: how much may the attacker change, and what does the attacker know?

A **threat model** fixes three things.

**1. The allowed perturbations.** Most work uses an $\ell_p$ ball of radius $\varepsilon$ around the clean image $\mathbf{x} \in [0, 1]^d$:

$$
\mathcal{S}_p(\mathbf{x}) = \{\mathbf{x}' \in [0, 1]^d : \lVert \mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x} \rVert_p \le \varepsilon\},
\qquad
\lVert \boldsymbol{\delta} \rVert_\infty = \max_i |\delta_i|,
\qquad
\lVert \boldsymbol{\delta} \rVert_2 = \Big(\sum_i \delta_i^2\Big)^{1/2},
$$

where $d$ is the number of pixel values, $\boldsymbol{\delta} = \mathbf{x}' - \mathbf{x}$ is the perturbation and $\delta_i$ its $i$-th entry. The $\ell_\infty$ ball lets every pixel change by at most $\varepsilon$ (a small change everywhere); the $\ell_2$ ball bounds the total energy of the change; the $\ell_0$ "norm" counts how many pixels change (a few pixels, changed a lot). On CIFAR-10, $\varepsilon = 8/255$ in $\ell_\infty$ is a standard budget: Madry et al. trained and attacked with $\varepsilon = 8$ on the 0–255 pixel scale [23], and RobustBench keeps leaderboards for $\ell_\infty$ and $\ell_2$ threat models and for common corruptions [24]. A small $\ell_p$ norm is a convenient proxy for "imperceptible", not a definition of it.

**2. The attacker's knowledge.** In a **white-box** attack, the attacker knows the model and its weights and can compute gradients. In a **black-box** attack, the attacker only queries the model. Papernot et al. showed a practical black-box attack: label inputs by querying the target model, train a local substitute model on them, craft adversarial examples on the substitute, and rely on **transfer** to the target [25]. Other black-box attacks estimate the decision boundary from queries alone. Robustness claims should be made against white-box attacks, because what one attacker cannot do today, a better-informed one may do tomorrow.

**3. The goal.** An **untargeted** attack wants any wrong label; a **targeted** attack wants a specific one (a stop sign read as a speed limit). Targeted attacks are harder, so the two give different numbers.

### FGSM and PGD, precisely

**Plain version.** FGSM takes one big step uphill on the loss. PGD takes many small steps uphill and, after each step, pulls the image back into the allowed ball. PGD finds stronger adversarial examples and is the standard first-order attack.

Let $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{x}, y)$ be the cross-entropy loss of the network with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ on input $\mathbf{x}$ with label $y$. **FGSM** [22] for the $\ell_\infty$ ball is

$$
\mathbf{x}_{\text{adv}} = \Pi_{[0,1]^d}\big(\mathbf{x} + \varepsilon\, \operatorname{sign}(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{x}, y))\big),
$$

where $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \mathcal{L}$ is the gradient of the loss with respect to the input pixels, $\operatorname{sign}$ is applied to each entry (giving $-1$, $0$ or $+1$), and $\Pi_{[0,1]^d}$ clips each pixel to the valid range. Moving every pixel by exactly $\pm\varepsilon$ in the direction that increases the loss is the best a *linear* approximation of the loss can do inside the $\ell_\infty$ ball.

**Projected gradient descent (PGD)** [23] repeats a smaller step $T$ times, starting from a random point in the ball:

$$
\mathbf{x}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{u}, \quad \mathbf{u} \sim \mathcal{U}[-\varepsilon, \varepsilon]^d,
\qquad
\mathbf{x}^{(t+1)} = \Pi_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x})}\big(\mathbf{x}^{(t)} + \alpha\, \operatorname{sign}(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{x}^{(t)}, y))\big),
$$

where $\mathbf{u}$ is uniform noise in the ball (the random start), $\alpha$ is the step size, $t = 0, \dots, T-1$ counts the steps, and $\Pi_{\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{x})}$ is the projection onto the allowed set. For the $\ell_\infty$ ball intersected with the valid pixel range, the projection is two clips: first $x'_i \leftarrow \min(\max(x'_i, x_i - \varepsilon), x_i + \varepsilon)$, then $x'_i \leftarrow \min(\max(x'_i, 0), 1)$. The final iterate $\mathbf{x}^{(T)}$ is the adversarial example. PGD is "gradient *ascent* on the loss"; the name comes from optimization, where the same algorithm minimizes. Because the loss surface is not concave, the random start and a few restarts help; Madry et al. observed that the losses reached from many random starts were well concentrated, which supported PGD as a reliable first-order adversary [23].

A second common loss is the **margin loss** of Carlini and Wagner, which directly maximizes the gap between the largest wrong logit and the correct logit instead of the cross-entropy [26]. It matters when the cross-entropy saturates, and it is one reason a serious evaluation runs several attacks.

### Adversarial training

**Plain version.** If the model fails on the worst nearby image, train it on the worst nearby image. During training, attack every batch with PGD and fit the attacked images instead of the clean ones.

Madry et al. wrote robust training as a min-max (saddle point) problem [23]:

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ \rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
\qquad
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim \mathcal{D}} \Big[\max_{\boldsymbol{\delta} \in \mathcal{S}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\delta}, y)\Big],
$$

where $\mathcal{D}$ is the data distribution, $\mathcal{S}$ the set of allowed perturbations (for example, $\lVert \boldsymbol{\delta} \rVert_\infty \le \varepsilon$), the inner maximum is the attacker's best perturbation and the outer minimum is the defender's training. In practice, the inner maximum is approximated by PGD with a few steps and the outer minimum by ordinary stochastic gradient descent on the resulting adversarial batch.

What it costs:

- **Compute.** Every training step needs a $T$-step attack, so training is roughly $T + 1$ times as expensive.
- **Capacity.** Madry et al. found that robust classification needs larger networks than clean classification; small networks trained against PGD could collapse to predicting one class, and training against one-step FGSM overfitted to its narrow set of perturbations and did not give robustness to PGD at large $\varepsilon$ [23].
- **Clean accuracy.** Robust models are usually less accurate on clean data. Tsipras et al. proved that this trade-off exists in a simple, natural setting and argued that robust models learn different features, which happen to align better with human perception [27]. **TRADES** splits the robust error into the natural error plus a boundary error and trains with a loss that trades the two explicitly through one weight [28].

### Code: FGSM, PGD and adversarial training on digits

NumPy computes the input gradient of a one-hidden-layer MLP by hand: for softmax outputs $\mathbf{p}$ and a one-hot label $\mathbf{e}_y$, the gradient of the cross-entropy with respect to the logits is $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{e}_y$, which we pass back through the output layer, the ReLU and the first layer. Both models get the same 50 passes over the training data; the robust one is fitted on PGD examples ($\varepsilon = 0.15$, 5 steps) made against its current weights.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
X = X / 16.0                                          # pixels in [0, 1]
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=0)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def input_grad(m, X, y):
    """Gradient of the cross-entropy loss with respect to the input pixels."""
    (W1, W2), (b1, b2) = m.coefs_, m.intercepts_
    h = np.maximum(X @ W1 + b1, 0)                    # hidden ReLU layer
    z = h @ W2 + b2                                   # logits
    g = np.exp(z - z.max(1, keepdims=True)); g /= g.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    g[np.arange(len(y)), y] -= 1                      # dL/dz = softmax(z) - one-hot(y)
    return ((g @ W2.T) * (h > 0)) @ W1.T              # back through ReLU and first layer

def fgsm(m, X, y, eps):
    return np.clip(X + eps * np.sign(input_grad(m, X, y)), 0, 1)

def pgd(m, X, y, eps, steps=10):
    alpha = 2.5 * eps / steps                         # step size
    Xa = np.clip(X + rng.uniform(-eps, eps, X.shape), 0, 1)        # random start in the ball
    for _ in range(steps):
        Xa = Xa + alpha * np.sign(input_grad(m, Xa, y))             # ascent step on the loss
        Xa = np.clip(np.clip(Xa, X - eps, X + eps), 0, 1)           # project: eps-ball, then [0, 1]
    return Xa

def train(adversarial, epochs=50, eps=0.15):
    m = MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(128,), random_state=0)
    m.partial_fit(Xtr, ytr, classes=np.arange(10))    # first pass on clean images
    for _ in range(epochs - 1):                       # Madry et al.: fit the worst case found by PGD
        m.partial_fit(pgd(m, Xtr, ytr, eps, steps=5) if adversarial else Xtr, ytr)
    return m

standard, robust = train(False), train(True)
for eps in [0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2]:
    acc = [m.score(atk(m, Xte, yte, eps), yte) for m in (standard, robust) for atk in (fgsm, pgd)]
    print(f"eps={eps:.2f}  standard: FGSM {acc[0]:.3f} PGD {acc[1]:.3f}"
          f"   adversarially trained: FGSM {acc[2]:.3f} PGD {acc[3]:.3f}")
```

eps=0.05  standard: FGSM 0.861 PGD 0.854   adversarially trained: FGSM 0.891 PGD 0.889
eps=0.10  standard: FGSM 0.574 PGD 0.528   adversarially trained: FGSM 0.757 PGD 0.746
eps=0.15  standard: FGSM 0.237 PGD 0.178   adversarially trained: FGSM 0.626 PGD 0.593
eps=0.20  standard: FGSM 0.057 PGD 0.007   adversarially trained: FGSM 0.415 PGD 0.361

### Evaluating robustness honestly

**Plain version.** It is easy to build a "defense" that only breaks the attacker's tools, for example by making gradients useless, while the model stays just as easy to fool. The robustness then exists only on paper, until someone uses a better attack.

Athalye, Carlini and Wagner named this **obfuscated gradients**, a kind of gradient masking, and described three types [29]: **shattered gradients** (non-differentiable or numerically unstable operations, such as quantizing the input, give gradients that are zero or wrong); **stochastic gradients** (random input transformations at test time make a single gradient misleading); and **vanishing or exploding gradients** (defenses that run an iterative procedure, such as projecting the input onto a learned manifold, behave like an extremely deep network). For each type they gave a counter-attack: **BPDA** (backward pass differentiable approximation) runs the defense normally in the forward pass but replaces it by a differentiable approximation, often the identity, in the backward pass; **EOT** (expectation over transformation) averages gradients over the random transformations; and **reparameterization** changes variables so that the iterative step disappears from the gradient path. In a case study of nine white-box defenses accepted at ICLR 2018, seven relied on obfuscated gradients, and their attacks fully circumvented six and partially one [29].

The same paper lists warning signs that an evaluation is broken: one-step attacks work better than iterative ones; black-box attacks work better than white-box ones; unbounded attacks do not reach 100% success; random sampling finds adversarial examples that gradient attacks miss; and increasing the budget does not increase success [29]. Our toy passes the checks it can: PGD beats FGSM, and accuracy reaches zero as $\varepsilon$ grows.

To make evaluations less dependent on the evaluator's care, Croce and Hein proposed **AutoAttack**, a parameter-free ensemble of two improved PGD variants (with an automatic step-size schedule and a different loss) and two complementary existing attacks; applied to over 50 published defenses, it gave lower robust accuracy than reported in all but one case [30]. **RobustBench** builds a public leaderboard on AutoAttack with rules on allowed models, and invites adaptive attacks where AutoAttack may overestimate robustness [24]. A reported robust accuracy should therefore state the threat model, $\varepsilon$, the attacks (at least AutoAttack or an adaptive attack designed for the defense), the number of steps and restarts, and the test set.

### Certified robustness

**Plain version.** An attack can only show that a model is *not* robust at a point. A certificate proves that *no* perturbation within a radius changes the answer, whatever attack is used.

**Randomized smoothing** [31] was, according to its authors, the only certified defense shown to be feasible on ImageNet at the time, and it remains the standard reference point. Take any base classifier $f$ and define a smoothed classifier $g$ that returns the class $f$ predicts most often under Gaussian noise:

$$
g(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{Y}} \Pr_{\boldsymbol{\eta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})}\big[f(\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\eta}) = c\big],
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ is isotropic Gaussian noise with standard deviation $\sigma$ per pixel and $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix. Cohen, Rosenfeld and Kolter proved that if the top class $c_A$ has probability at least $\underline{p_A}$ under the noise and every other class at most $\overline{p_B}$, then $g$ predicts $c_A$ for every perturbation $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ with

$$
\lVert \boldsymbol{\delta} \rVert_2 \lt R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\Big(\Phi^{-1}\big(\underline{p_A}\big) - \Phi^{-1}\big(\overline{p_B}\big)\Big),
$$

where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the inverse of the standard normal cumulative distribution function [31]. In practice, $\underline{p_A}$ is a lower confidence bound estimated from Monte Carlo samples, and the method abstains when it cannot certify. The guarantee holds for the $\ell_2$ threat model, with a chosen failure probability, and for the smoothed classifier $g$, not for $f$. Larger $\sigma$ allows larger radii but lowers clean accuracy, the same trade-off in another form.

### Physical and patch attacks

**Plain version.** An attack does not have to be invisible or digital. A printed sticker can fool a camera.

Eykholt et al. generated perturbations that survive changes of viewpoint, distance and lighting, printed them as black-and-white stickers on a real stop sign, and caused a road-sign classifier to output the attacker's target class in most lab images and in many frames of a drive-by video [32]. Brown et al. made **adversarial patches**: universal, printable image regions that, placed anywhere in a scene, make a classifier report a chosen class [33]. Such attacks leave the $\ell_p$ threat model: they are large but localized. They matter for any camera-based system, and they connect to the presentation attacks on face recognition discussed in [DLCV 02A](/en/learn/deep-learning/face-metric-learning/#presentation-attacks).

## Out-of-distribution and anomaly detection

**Plain version.** A classifier always picks one of its known classes, even for a photo of something it has never seen. OOD detection adds a second output, a score that says "this input looks like my training data" or "it does not", so the system can refuse to answer.

### Which task are you solving?

Several closely related tasks go by different names. Yang et al. place them in one framework, **generalized OOD detection**, and distinguish them by what kind of shift must be detected and by whether ID data has one class or many [3]:

| Task | In-distribution data | Must flag | Typical example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **OOD detection** | a $K$-class training set | inputs from other classes (semantic shift), while still classifying ID inputs | a pet classifier sees a car |
| **Open-set recognition** | $K$ known classes | unknown classes, while classifying known ones | [PR 10](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/recognition-capstone/#open-set-recognition-held-out-categories), [DLCV 02A](/en/learn/deep-learning/face-metric-learning/#recognition-as-comparison-the-open-set-problem) |
| **Sensory anomaly detection** | normal images of one kind | inputs that look different (covariate shift) | a scratched part on a production line |
| **Semantic anomaly / novelty detection** | one or a few classes | inputs from any other class | a new species in camera-trap images |

OOD detection and open-set recognition differ mainly in their history and benchmarks; the survey treats open-set recognition as multi-class novelty detection [3]. All of them differ from the **reject option** of [PR 01](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/bayes-decisions/#the-reject-option), which abstains on *ambiguous* inputs between known classes rather than on *novel* ones.

### Scores

**Plain version.** Each detector boils down to one number per input. Output-based scores ask how confident the classifier is; feature-based scores ask how far the input's features lie from the training features.

Let $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^K$ be the logits of a trained classifier, $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ its penultimate features (the input to the last linear layer), and adopt the convention that a **larger score means more in-distribution**. Four standard post-hoc scores, which need no retraining:

**Maximum softmax probability (MSP)** [34]:

$$
s_{\text{MSP}}(\mathbf{x}) = \max_{k} \frac{e^{z_k(\mathbf{x})}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j(\mathbf{x})}},
$$

where $z_k$ is the $k$-th logit. Hendrycks and Gimpel showed that correctly classified examples tend to have higher MSP than misclassified and OOD ones, which made MSP the standard baseline [34].

**Energy score** [35]:

$$
E(\mathbf{x}) = -T \log \sum_{k=1}^{K} e^{z_k(\mathbf{x})/T},
\qquad
s_{\text{energy}}(\mathbf{x}) = -E(\mathbf{x}),
$$

where $T > 0$ is a temperature (usually $T = 1$). The negative energy is a log-sum-exp of the logits, a smooth maximum that keeps the *size* of the logits, which the softmax normalizes away. Liu et al. argued that energy is better aligned with the input density than the softmax confidence, and reported lower FPR at 95% TPR than MSP on their CIFAR benchmarks [35].

**Mahalanobis distance** [36]: fit one Gaussian per class to the training features, with class means $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c$ and one shared ("tied") covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, and score

$$
s_{\text{Maha}}(\mathbf{x}) = -\min_{c} \big(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}) - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c\big)^{\top} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \big(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}) - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c\big),
$$

where the minimum runs over the $K$ classes. This is the linear discriminant analysis of [PR 01](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/bayes-decisions/#the-gaussian-case-lda-and-qda-boundaries) placed on learned features. Lee et al. also added a small input perturbation and combined several layers; we use the last hidden layer only.

**k-nearest-neighbour (kNN) distance** [37]: normalize features to unit length, $\hat{\mathbf{h}} = \mathbf{h} / \lVert \mathbf{h} \rVert_2$, and score

$$
s_{\text{kNN}}(\mathbf{x}) = -\big\lVert \hat{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{x}) - \hat{\mathbf{h}}_{(k)} \big\rVert_2,
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{(k)}$ is the $k$-th nearest normalized training feature to $\hat{\mathbf{h}}(\mathbf{x})$. Sun et al. found that this non-parametric score, with no Gaussian assumption, works well and that feature normalization is critical [37].

These scores leave the classifier unchanged. Training-time methods change it: **Outlier Exposure** trains the classifier to give uniform (low-confidence) predictions on a large auxiliary set of outlier images, which improved detection of unseen OOD data in the original experiments [38].

### Metrics

**Plain version.** Treat detection as a binary decision, ID versus OOD, and ask how well the score separates the two groups, either over all thresholds or at one threshold that keeps most ID inputs.

Treat ID test inputs as positives and OOD inputs as negatives, and accept an input when $s(\mathbf{x}) \ge \tau$. Then $\mathrm{TPR}(\tau)$ is the fraction of ID inputs accepted and $\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)$ the fraction of OOD inputs accepted. Three metrics are standard [35]:

- **AUROC**, the area under the curve of $\mathrm{TPR}$ against $\mathrm{FPR}$ as $\tau$ varies. It equals the probability that a random ID input gets a higher score than a random OOD input; 0.5 is chance, 1 is perfect separation.
- **FPR@95TPR** $= \mathrm{FPR}(\tau_{95})$, where $\tau_{95}$ is the threshold with $\mathrm{TPR}(\tau_{95}) = 0.95$: the fraction of OOD inputs still accepted when 95% of ID inputs are kept. Lower is better. It describes one operating point, the kind a deployed system actually uses.
- **AUPR**, the area under the precision–recall curve, reported with ID inputs as the positive class (AUPR-In) or with OOD inputs as the positive class (AUPR-Out) [34]. It depends on the ratio of ID to OOD test inputs, which should therefore be reported.

ROC and precision–recall curves themselves are explained in [DLCV 02](/en/learn/deep-learning/image-classification/#threshold-free-evaluation-roc-and-precisionrecall-curves).

### Code: four scores, near and far OOD

ID data are the digits 0–4. **Near-OOD** inputs are the unseen digits 5–9: the same kind of image, new classes. **Far-OOD** inputs are 8×8 patches of photographs from scikit-image, rescaled to the same pixel range: a different kind of image altogether. The classifier never sees either during training.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp, softmax
from skimage import data, transform
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
X = X / 16.0
known = y < 5                                         # in-distribution (ID): digits 0-4
Xtr, Xid, ytr, yid = train_test_split(X[known], y[known], test_size=0.3, stratify=y[known], random_state=0)
X_near = X[~known]                                    # near-OOD: unseen digits 5-9
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
photos = [transform.resize(im / 255.0, (256, 256)) for im in (data.camera(), data.moon(), data.coins(), data.text())]
X_far = []                                            # far-OOD: 8x8 patches of photographs
for _ in range(600):
    im = photos[rng.integers(4)]; r, c = rng.integers(0, 224, 2)
    p = transform.resize(im[r:r + 32, c:c + 32], (8, 8), anti_aliasing=True)
    X_far.append(((p - p.min()) / (np.ptp(p) + 1e-8)).ravel())
X_far = np.array(X_far)

m = MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(64,), alpha=1e-3, max_iter=500, random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
(W1, W2), (b1, b2) = m.coefs_, m.intercepts_
feat = lambda X: np.maximum(X @ W1 + b1, 0)           # penultimate (hidden) features
unit = lambda F: F / (np.linalg.norm(F, axis=1, keepdims=True) + 1e-12)
F = feat(Xtr)
mu = np.stack([F[ytr == c].mean(0) for c in range(5)])           # class means
cov = sum(np.cov(F[ytr == c].T, bias=True) * np.sum(ytr == c) for c in range(5)) / len(F)
P = np.linalg.inv(cov + 1e-3 * np.eye(len(cov)))                # shared precision matrix

def scores(X):                                        # every score: larger = "more in-distribution"
    H = feat(X); z = H @ W2 + b2; d = H[:, None] - mu[None]
    return {"MSP": softmax(z, axis=1).max(1),
            "energy": logsumexp(z, axis=1),           # = -E(x) with temperature 1
            "Mahalanobis": -np.einsum("ncd,de,nce->nc", d, P, d).min(1),
            "kNN": -np.sort(np.linalg.norm(unit(H)[:, None] - unit(F)[None], axis=2), 1)[:, 9]}

s_id = scores(Xid)
print(f"ID test accuracy {m.score(Xid, yid):.3f}")
for name, X_ood in [("near", X_near), ("far", X_far)]:
    s_ood = scores(X_ood)
    for k in s_id:
        auroc = roc_auc_score(np.r_[np.ones(len(Xid)), np.zeros(len(X_ood))], np.r_[s_id[k], s_ood[k]])
        fpr95 = np.mean(s_ood[k] >= np.quantile(s_id[k], 0.05))  # OOD accepted when 95% of ID is kept
        print(f"{name:4s} {k:11s} AUROC {auroc:.3f}  FPR@95TPR {fpr95:.3f}")
```

near energy      AUROC 0.955  FPR@95TPR 0.258
near Mahalanobis AUROC 0.961  FPR@95TPR 0.209
near kNN         AUROC 0.970  FPR@95TPR 0.152
far  MSP         AUROC 0.870  FPR@95TPR 0.448

- **"Far" in content is not "easy" for every score.** The photo patches are nothing like digits, yet MSP accepts 45% and energy 66% of them at the 95%-TPR threshold. - **The ranking of scores changes with the OOD set.** Energy beats MSP on the unseen digits but loses to it on the patches. A large benchmark study, OpenOOD v1.5, likewise found no single method that wins across datasets [39].

### Industrial anomaly detection

**Plain version.** On a production line, defects are rare and varied, so you cannot collect examples of every defect. Instead, you learn only what *normal* parts look like, patch by patch, and flag any patch that looks unlike every normal patch you have seen.

**MVTec AD** made this setting a standard benchmark: 5354 high-resolution images of object and texture categories, defect-free images for training, test images with more than 70 types of defects (scratches, dents, contaminations, structural changes), and pixel-precise masks of the defect regions [40]. Methods are scored at two levels: **image-level** (is this part defective?) and **pixel-level** (where is the defect?), usually with AUROC; region-overlap measures such as AU-PRO are also used, for example by MVTec AD 2 [41]. This is *sensory* anomaly detection in the terms of the table above.

**PatchCore** [42] is a strong, simple method built on the kNN idea. It passes defect-free training images through a network pre-trained on ImageNet and keeps **mid-level patch features** (the last layers are too specific to ImageNet classes and too coarse for small defects), each averaged over a small neighbourhood for context. All these features go into a **memory bank**, which **greedy coreset subsampling** shrinks to a small subset that still covers rare normal patterns. At test time, each patch's **distance to its nearest neighbour** in the memory bank forms the anomaly map, and the map's maximum (slightly re-weighted) is the image score. The method was designed for the "cold-start" setting in which only nominal (defect-free) images are available, and it reached state-of-the-art detection and localization on MVTec AD [42].

### Code: a patch memory bank on a brick wall

Without network weights we use raw 7×7 pixel patches as features, which keeps the mechanics of PatchCore (memory bank, coreset, nearest-neighbour distance map, max-pooled image score) and drops the learned features. "Products" are 64×64 crops of scikit-image's brick-wall texture: normal training crops from the top half of the image, test crops from the bottom half, and half of the test crops receive a thin synthetic scratch.

```python
import numpy as np
from skimage import data, draw, transform
from skimage.util import view_as_windows
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
tex = transform.rescale(data.brick() / 255.0, 0.5)   # 256x256 brick-wall "product surface"
S, P = 64, 7                                          # image size, patch size

def crop(top):                                        # normal images: top half; test images: bottom half
    r = rng.integers(0, 128 - S) + (0 if top else 128); c = rng.integers(0, 256 - S)
    return tex[r:r + S, c:c + S].copy()

def scratch(im):                                      # a thin line, 0.5 brighter or darker
    r, c = rng.integers(16, S - 16, 2); a = rng.uniform(0, np.pi); L = rng.integers(8, 16)
    rr, cc = draw.line(r, c, int(r + L * np.sin(a)), int(c + L * np.cos(a)))
    mask = np.zeros_like(im, bool); mask[rr, cc] = True
    im[mask] = np.clip(im[mask] + rng.choice([-0.5, 0.5]), 0, 1)
    return im, mask

def patches(im, step=1):                              # one 7x7 patch centred on each pixel
    return view_as_windows(np.pad(im, P // 2, mode="reflect"), (P, P), step).reshape(-1, P * P)

def greedy_coreset(M, n):                             # keep n patches that cover the bank well
    keep, d = [0], np.linalg.norm(M - M[0], axis=1)
    for _ in range(n - 1):
        i = int(d.argmax()); keep.append(i)
        d = np.minimum(d, np.linalg.norm(M - M[i], axis=1))
    return M[keep]

bank = np.vstack([patches(crop(top=True), step=2) for _ in range(16)])
memory = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=1).fit(greedy_coreset(bank, len(bank) // 10))

is_defect, img_score, pix_score, pix_true = [], [], [], []
for k in range(40):
    im, mask = scratch(crop(top=False)) if k % 2 else (crop(top=False), np.zeros((S, S), bool))
    amap = memory.kneighbors(patches(im))[0].reshape(S, S)   # nearest-neighbour distance per pixel
    is_defect.append(k % 2); img_score.append(amap.max())
    pix_score.append(amap.ravel()); pix_true.append(mask.ravel())
print(f"memory bank {len(bank)} -> {len(bank) // 10} patches")
print(f"image-level AUROC {roc_auc_score(is_defect, img_score):.3f}")
print(f"pixel-level AUROC {roc_auc_score(np.concatenate(pix_true), np.concatenate(pix_score)):.3f}")
```

It prints:

```text
memory bank 16384 -> 1638 patches
image-level AUROC 0.955
pixel-level AUROC 0.996
```

### Pitfalls in OOD and anomaly evaluation

**Plain version.** OOD numbers depend more on *which* OOD data was chosen than on the method, and many published OOD test sets are flawed. Read every OOD result together with its test sets.

- **Near- versus far-OOD.** OpenOOD groups OOD test sets into near-OOD (similar to the ID data) and far-OOD (clearly different), and finds a large gap in detection performance between the two groups for all detectors [39]. Our toy shows that the label can mislead: for output-based scores, "far" photo patches were harder than "near" digits.
- **Contaminated OOD test sets.** Bitterwolf et al. found that many OOD test sets used with ImageNet-1K contain a large share of images that actually show ID classes, in some cases more than half, which distorts the evaluation; they built NINCO, an OOD test set with every image checked to be free of ID objects [43].
- **Pre-training overlap.** A detector built on a model pre-trained on web-scale data may have seen the "unknown" classes during pre-training. Then the test is no longer about the unknown, and the setting must be described accordingly.
- **Thresholds and model selection.** Like any hyperparameter, the threshold and the choice of score must be set on validation data. Tuning them on the OOD test set (or on a proxy that is too similar to it) gives the same optimism as the oracle model selection that DomainBed warns against [19].
- **Covariate shift inside the ID set.** In deployment, an ID image may arrive blurred or under new lighting. OpenOOD v1.5 evaluates "full-spectrum" detection, in which covariate-shifted ID images must be *accepted* while semantic OOD images are rejected, and finds it a significant challenge for all current approaches [39]. This is where the first and third problems of this chapter meet.

## A robustness evaluation plan

**Plain version.** Before claiming a model is robust, write down what it is robust *to*, test that with the strongest tools available, and report the result next to the clean number.

1. **Name the problem.** Shift (which kind?), adversary (which threat model?), or unknown inputs (which kind of novelty?). If several, evaluate each separately.
2. **Build the test data first.** At least one real shifted set from the expected deployment (new site, device or time), synthetic corruptions as a cheap supplement, and a held-out OOD set that is checked for ID contamination.
3. **Use strong evaluation.** For adversarial claims: white-box attacks, PGD with restarts and AutoAttack or an adaptive attack, the warning-sign checks above, and the exact $\varepsilon$ and norm. For shift: effective robustness or matched clean accuracy. For OOD: AUROC and FPR@95TPR on near- and far-OOD sets.
4. **Keep the test data out of the loop.** Choose hyperparameters, thresholds and checkpoints on validation data from the training distribution, or state clearly that an oracle was used.
5. **Report the trade-offs.** Clean accuracy, robust accuracy, calibration, and compute cost together; a gain in one is not free.
6. **Monitor after release.** Track input statistics and confidence distributions, as in [ML 11](/en/learn/ml/evaluation-deployment/#monitoring-after-deployment), and in multi-sensor systems the sensor faults listed in [DLCV 03B](/en/learn/deep-learning/3d-object-detection/#failure-modes).

## Modern view

**Surveys worth reading.**

- **Yang, Zhou, Li and Liu [3]** (IJCV 2024) unify anomaly detection, novelty detection, open-set recognition, OOD detection and outlier detection as sub-tasks of *generalized OOD detection*, organized by whether covariate or semantic shift must be detected and by whether ID data has one or several classes; they then review each area, with most detail on OOD detection methods (classification-based, density-based and distance-based). Read it first to sort out the vocabulary.
- **Zhou et al. [18]** (TPAMI) define domain generalization, relate it to domain adaptation and transfer learning, and review methods grouped into domain alignment, meta-learning, data augmentation, ensemble learning and others, together with theory and applications beyond vision.
- **Liang, He and Tan [44]** (IJCV) categorize test-time adaptation by the form of test data into test-time domain adaptation, test-time batch adaptation and online test-time adaptation, with a taxonomy of algorithms in each group and a discussion of open challenges.
- **Akhtar, Mian, Kardan and Shah [45]** (IEEE Access 2021) review adversarial attacks and defenses in computer vision since 2018: attacks on classifiers, attacks beyond classification, physical-world attacks, theories of why adversarial examples exist, and defenses grouped into model modification, input modification and external detectors, with certified defenses treated separately.
- **Pang, Shen, Cao and van den Hengel [46]** (ACM Computing Surveys 2021) organize deep anomaly detection into three frameworks: deep learning for generic feature extraction, learning representations of normality, and end-to-end anomaly score learning, with eleven finer categories, and discuss each one's assumptions, advantages and disadvantages.
- **Liu et al. [47]** (Machine Intelligence Research 2024) review industrial image anomaly detection by network architecture, level of supervision, loss function, metrics and datasets, and propose a setting closer to real manufacturing.

**The takeaways they share.** Distribution shift, adversarial robustness and OOD detection grew as separate communities with separate benchmarks, and the surveys repeatedly find that benchmark choice, model selection and evaluation protocol drive results as much as methods do. Simple baselines (ERM with good training, MSP or kNN scores, adversarial training with PGD) remain hard to beat when compared fairly.

**State of the art, 2023–2026.** Five trends are visible. (1) **Foundation models changed the starting point.** Large, diverse pre-training gives much of the natural-shift robustness that specialized methods could not [12], [13], and robust fine-tuning such as weight interpolation tries to keep it [14]; OOD benchmarks now include CLIP and DINOv2 backbones [39]. (2) **Generated data for adversarial training.** Training on images from a modern diffusion model, without external real data, set new results on RobustBench for CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 [48]; robust accuracy on these benchmarks still lies far below clean accuracy. (3) **Better OOD evaluation.** Unified benchmarks with near- and far-OOD splits and full-spectrum settings [49], [39] and cleaned test sets [43] replaced ad hoc comparisons. (4) **Zero- and few-shot anomaly detection.** WinCLIP uses CLIP with prompt ensembles over state words and window-level features to classify and segment anomalies with no or very few normal images [50]. (5) **Harder industrial benchmarks.** Because results on MVTec AD saturated, MVTec AD 2 adds transparent and overlapping objects, unusual illumination, high variance of normal data, very small defects and test scenarios with lighting changes, and reports that state-of-the-art methods remain far from solving it [41].

**Open problems.** Robustness to natural shifts that no benchmark anticipated; adversarial robustness beyond small $\ell_p$ balls (patches, physical changes, semantic edits) and at the scale of foundation models; certificates that are tight at useful radii for large images; OOD detection when the backbone may have seen everything during pre-training; detectors that accept covariate-shifted ID inputs while rejecting semantic novelty; and a common evaluation protocol that tests all three problems of this chapter on the same model.

## Key takeaways

- Robustness has three different questions: does accuracy survive a change of data (shift), does the label survive a worst-case small change (adversarial), and can the model say "not my task" (OOD and anomalies). Each needs its own test data and metric.
- Shifts are covariate, label or concept shifts; benchmarks probe corruptions, renditions, natural hard images, controlled collection, re-collection and real deployments. Compare interventions at matched clean accuracy (effective robustness).
- What helps against shift: diverse augmentation, more diverse pre-training data, and, when test data arrive in batches, re-estimated normalization statistics or entropy-based test-time adaptation. Domain adaptation needs unlabeled target data; domain generalization methods rarely beat well-tuned ERM.
- An adversarial claim must state its threat model ($\ell_p$ norm, $\varepsilon$, white- or black-box). PGD with a random start is the standard first-order attack; adversarial training (training on PGD examples) is the most reliable defense and costs compute, capacity and some clean accuracy.
- Weak evaluations overstate robustness: watch for gradient masking, use AutoAttack or adaptive attacks, and remember that certificates such as randomized smoothing prove robustness only for their own norm and radius.
- OOD scores come from outputs (MSP, energy) or features (Mahalanobis, kNN); none wins everywhere. Report AUROC and FPR@95TPR on several OOD sets, near and far, checked for ID contamination.
- Industrial anomaly detection learns normal patches only; a memory bank with nearest-neighbour distance (PatchCore) gives image scores and defect maps, and image-level decisions are harder than pixel-level ranking.

### Effective robustness and accuracy on the line

This note explains how a robustness scatter plot is read, how the baseline trend is fitted, and what "effective robustness" measures beyond plain accuracy.

**The scatter plot.** Evaluate many models $f_1, \dots, f_n$ on a clean test set and on a shifted test set. Plot one point per model, with clean accuracy $\operatorname{acc}_1(f)$ on the horizontal axis and shifted accuracy $\operatorname{acc}_2(f)$ on the vertical axis. A model on the diagonal $\operatorname{acc}_2 = \operatorname{acc}_1$ is unaffected by the shift; real models lie below it.

**The baseline.** Taori et al. fitted a trend line $\beta(\cdot)$ through standard models without any robustness intervention, with both axes in logit scale, $\log(a/(1-a))$ for an accuracy $a$ [8]. Miller et al. used the probit transform $\Phi^{-1}(\operatorname{acc})$, where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the inverse standard normal cumulative distribution function, and found that on many shifts the points of very different models fall close to a straight line in that scale [9]. The transform spreads out accuracies near 0 and 1, where differences are compressed on a linear scale.

**Two numbers per intervention.** For a model $f$, the **effective robustness** is

$$
\rho(f) = \operatorname{acc}_2(f) - \beta\big(\operatorname{acc}_1(f)\big),
$$

the vertical distance of its point above the baseline trend [8]. A model with $\rho = 0$ is exactly as robust as its clean accuracy predicts. For a model $f'$ obtained from $f$ by a robustness intervention (an augmentation, a loss), the **relative robustness** is

$$
\tau(f') = \operatorname{acc}_2(f') - \operatorname{acc}_2(f),
$$

the change in shifted accuracy itself [8]. An intervention can raise $\rho$ while lowering both accuracies, which would be no use; Taori et al. therefore ask for positive values of both.

**Edge cases.** The baseline depends on which models define it (for example, ImageNet-trained convolutional networks), so $\rho$ is relative to a population. The linear relation is an empirical regularity, not a law: Miller et al. reported weaker correlations for some synthetic corruptions of CIFAR-10 and for a medical tissue dataset in WILDS [9]. And a high $\rho$ on one shift says nothing about another; zero-shot CLIP's large effective robustness on natural ImageNet shifts largely disappeared after supervised fitting on ImageNet [12].

**How to use it.** When a paper reports "better robustness", look for the scatter plot. If the new model lies on the same line as the baselines, the gain is explained by its clean accuracy.

### Test-time entropy minimization

This note describes what TENT optimizes during testing, which parameters it touches and why, and when it can fail.

**Setting.** A model with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ was trained on a source distribution. At test time, unlabeled batches $\mathbf{X}_t = \{\mathbf{x}_{t,1}, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{t,B}\}$ arrive from a shifted distribution, one batch at a time ($t$ indexes the batch, $B$ is the batch size). No source data and no labels are available; this is *fully* test-time adaptation [16].

**Objective.** For a prediction $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})$ over $K$ classes, the Shannon entropy is

$$
H(\hat{\mathbf{p}}) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} \hat{p}_k \log \hat{p}_k,
$$

which is 0 for a one-hot prediction and $\log K$ for a uniform one. TENT minimizes the mean entropy of the predictions on the current batch, $\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b} H(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t,b})$, with one gradient step per batch. In the paper's default online scheme, the current batch's normalization statistics are used at once, while the affine update follows the prediction for the current batch and so takes effect from the next batch on [16]. The motivation in the paper is empirical: lower-entropy predictions tend to be more accurate, and stronger corruption tends to raise entropy [16].

**Which parameters.** Only the normalization layers change. For each channel $j$, a batch-normalization layer computes $\gamma_j (h_j - \mu_j)/\sigma_j + \beta_j$, where $h_j$ is the channel's activation, $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ its mean and standard deviation, and $\gamma_j$, $\beta_j$ a learned scale and shift. TENT (i) replaces $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ by the statistics of the current test batch, as Schneider et al. do [15], and (ii) updates only $\gamma_j$ and $\beta_j$ by the entropy gradient, keeping all convolution weights fixed [16]. These channel-wise parameters are a tiny fraction of the model, which makes online updates cheap and limits how far the model can move.

**Failure cases.** Entropy minimization rewards confidence, not correctness. If the batch is small or mixes several conditions, the statistics are poor (Exercise 1); if the shift is so strong that most predictions are wrong, minimizing entropy makes the model confidently wrong; and if the stream changes, accumulated updates can drift. Label shift inside a batch (one class dominating) also biases the batch statistics. The TTA survey discusses such settings and the methods proposed for them [44].

### Domain-adversarial training and the gradient-reversal layer

This note writes DANN's objective, shows how one layer implements its min-max structure, and lists when feature alignment can hurt.

**Model.** A feature extractor $G_f(\cdot; \boldsymbol{\theta}_f)$ maps an image to features; a label predictor $G_y(\cdot; \boldsymbol{\theta}_y)$ maps features to class probabilities; a domain classifier $G_d(\cdot; \boldsymbol{\theta}_d)$ maps features to the probability that the image comes from the target domain [17]. Training data are labelled source images $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ and unlabeled target images $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=n+1}^{N}$, with domain labels $d_i = 0$ for source and $d_i = 1$ for target.

**Objective.** With $\mathcal{L}_y$ the classification loss on source images and $\mathcal{L}_d$ the domain-classification loss on all images,

$$
E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_f, \boldsymbol{\theta}_y, \boldsymbol{\theta}_d) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathcal{L}_y^{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_f, \boldsymbol{\theta}_y) - \lambda\, \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}_d^{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_f, \boldsymbol{\theta}_d),
$$

where $\lambda \ge 0$ weights the two terms. DANN seeks a saddle point: $(\boldsymbol{\theta}_f, \boldsymbol{\theta}_y)$ minimize $E$ (good labels, and features that make the domain classifier *fail*), while $\boldsymbol{\theta}_d$ maximizes $E$, that is, minimizes its own domain loss [17]. (We write the loss in this simplified form; the paper's normalization of the terms differs in detail.)

**The gradient-reversal layer.** Place a layer $R$ between $G_f$ and $G_d$ that is the identity in the forward pass and multiplies the gradient by $-1$ in the backward pass:

$$
R(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v}, \qquad \frac{\partial R}{\partial \mathbf{v}} = -\mathbf{I},
$$

where $\mathbf{v}$ is the feature vector and $\mathbf{I}$ the identity matrix. Then ordinary backpropagation on the label loss plus $\lambda$ times the domain loss trains $G_d$ to classify domains and, through the reversed gradient, trains $G_f$ to confuse it [17]. No alternating optimization is needed; in a modern framework the layer is a two-line custom function.

**Edge cases and pitfalls.**

- *Label shift between domains.* If class proportions differ between source and target, perfectly aligned feature distributions force some target images of one class onto source regions of another class. Alignment of marginal feature distributions is not alignment of class-conditional ones.
- *Trivial alignment.* Features can become domain-invariant by discarding information the label task needs. The label loss counteracts this only on the source domain.
- *Model selection.* Without target labels, choosing $\lambda$ and the stopping point is hard, the same problem that DomainBed raises for domain generalization [19].

### Attack settings that decide an evaluation

This note lists the choices inside an attack that change the reported robust accuracy, and what AutoAttack automates.

**Step size and number of steps.** PGD with $T$ steps of size $\alpha$ can move at most $T\alpha$ in each coordinate. With $T\alpha \lt \varepsilon$, it cannot reach the corners of the $\ell_\infty$ ball, where FGSM-like solutions lie. A common rule, used in our snippet, is $\alpha = 2.5\,\varepsilon / T$: Madry et al. used it with $T = 100$ in their evaluations at other budgets and in $\ell_2$, so that the boundary of the ball can be reached from any starting point inside it [23]. Too large a step makes PGD oscillate between corners; too small a step stalls. AutoAttack's PGD variant replaces the fixed step by a schedule: at preset checkpoints it halves the step size if the loss has not increased often enough since the last checkpoint [30].

**Random restarts.** The loss is not concave in $\boldsymbol{\delta}$, so different random starts reach different local maxima. An evaluation should take, for each test image, the best result over several restarts; an image counts as robust only if every restart fails.

**The loss.** Cross-entropy can saturate: when the model is very confident, its gradient is tiny and the attack makes little progress, which is one mechanism of gradient masking. The Carlini–Wagner margin loss,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CW}}(\mathbf{x}', y) = \max_{k \ne y} z_k(\mathbf{x}') - z_y(\mathbf{x}'),
$$

where $z_k$ are the logits, becomes positive exactly when the attack succeeds and does not saturate in the same way [26]. AutoAttack adds the Difference of Logits Ratio loss, which is invariant to shifting and rescaling the logits, whereas cross-entropy is invariant only to shifting [30].

**$\ell_2$ PGD.** For the $\ell_2$ ball, the step follows the gradient direction normalized to a fixed length instead of its sign [23], $\mathbf{x}' \leftarrow \mathbf{x}' + \alpha\, \mathbf{g}/\lVert \mathbf{g} \rVert_2$ with $\mathbf{g} = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \mathcal{L}$, and the projection rescales $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ to length $\varepsilon$ when it is longer: $\boldsymbol{\delta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\delta} \cdot \min(1, \varepsilon / \lVert \boldsymbol{\delta} \rVert_2)$, followed by clipping to $[0, 1]$. (Clipping after rescaling keeps the perturbation inside the ball because clipping can only shorten it.)

**Targeted versus untargeted, and which test images.** Untargeted robust accuracy is the fraction of test images for which no attack in the ensemble finds any wrong label. Report it on the full test set (or a stated subset), together with clean accuracy on the same images; robust accuracy cannot exceed clean accuracy, because a misclassified clean image counts as already broken.

**Checklist.** Norm and $\varepsilon$ (and the pixel scale it refers to); attacks used; steps, step size and restarts; losses; whether the attack was adapted to the defense; number of test images. AutoAttack's value is that most of these choices are fixed and the same for everyone [30], which is why RobustBench uses it [24].

### Randomized smoothing in practice

This note goes through the certification procedure of Cohen et al., explains why the certified radius is capped, and gives a tested snippet on digits.

**The procedure.** Given a base classifier $f$, noise level $\sigma$, an input $\mathbf{x}$, sample sizes $n_0$ and $n$, and a failure probability $\alpha$, the CERTIFY procedure is [31]:

1. Draw $n_0$ noisy copies $\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\eta}$, classify them with $f$, and take the most frequent class $\hat{c}_A$ (a guess at the top class).
2. Draw $n$ fresh noisy copies and count how many are classified $\hat{c}_A$; call the count $k$.
3. Compute a one-sided $(1 - \alpha)$ lower confidence bound $\underline{p_A}$ on the binomial probability from $k$ successes in $n$ trials; Cohen et al. use the Clopper–Pearson bound.
4. If $\underline{p_A} > 1/2$, return $\hat{c}_A$ and the radius $R = \sigma\, \Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A})$; otherwise abstain.

Step 4 uses the bound $\overline{p_B} = 1 - \underline{p_A}$ on all other classes, which turns the radius of the main text into $\frac{\sigma}{2}\big(\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A}) - \Phi^{-1}(1 - \underline{p_A})\big) = \sigma\, \Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A})$, because $\Phi^{-1}(1 - p) = -\Phi^{-1}(p)$. With probability at least $1 - \alpha$, a returned class is what the smoothed classifier $g$ predicts everywhere within radius $R$ [31]. Using separate samples for steps 1 and 2 keeps the bound valid: selecting the class and estimating its probability from the same samples would bias the estimate.

**Why the radius is capped.** Even if all $n$ noisy copies agree ($k = n$), the Clopper–Pearson lower bound is $\underline{p_A} = \alpha^{1/n}$, so $R \le \sigma\, \Phi^{-1}(\alpha^{1/n})$. With $n = 2000$, $\alpha = 0.001$ and $\sigma = 0.5$, this cap is about $1.35$ (our calculation). Larger radii need many more samples, and the paper notes that $R$ grows only very slowly with $n$ [31].

**Training the base classifier.** The certificate holds for any $f$, but it is useful only if $f$ classifies noisy inputs well, so $f$ is trained with Gaussian noise of the same $\sigma$ added to its inputs.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta, norm
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
X = X / 16.0
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=0)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sigma = 0.5
Xnoisy = np.vstack([Xtr + rng.normal(0, sigma, Xtr.shape) for _ in range(4)])   # train f under noise
f = MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(128,), alpha=1e-3, max_iter=200, early_stopping=True, random_state=0).fit(Xnoisy, np.tile(ytr, 4))

def certify(x, n0=100, n=2000, alpha=0.001):
    votes0 = np.bincount(f.predict(x + rng.normal(0, sigma, (n0, 64))), minlength=10)
    c = votes0.argmax()                                   # guess the top class with a small sample
    k = np.sum(f.predict(x + rng.normal(0, sigma, (n, 64))) == c)
    p_low = beta.ppf(alpha, k, n - k + 1)                 # one-sided Clopper-Pearson lower bound
    return (c, sigma * norm.ppf(p_low)) if p_low > 0.5 else (-1, 0.0)   # -1 means abstain

res = [certify(x) for x in Xte[:200]]
for r in [0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0]:
    ok = np.mean([(c == t) and (R >= r) for (c, R), t in zip(res, yte[:200])])
    print(f"certified accuracy at l2 radius {r:.2f}: {ok:.3f}")
print("abstained:", sum(c == -1 for c, _ in res), "of", len(res))
```

It prints:

```text
certified accuracy at l2 radius 0.00: 0.825
certified accuracy at l2 radius 0.25: 0.590
certified accuracy at l2 radius 0.50: 0.290
certified accuracy at l2 radius 0.75: 0.070
certified accuracy at l2 radius 1.00: 0.000
abstained: 32 of 200
```

(Our toy run.) **Certified accuracy** at radius $r$ is the fraction of test images that are certified with $R \ge r$ *and* correctly classified. At $r = 0$ it is the accuracy of the smoothed classifier counting abstentions as errors. To put the radii in scale: an $\ell_2$ change of 0.5 on these $[0, 1]$ images equals, for example, a change of 0.0625 in every one of the 64 pixels. Note that the noise is not clipped to $[0, 1]$; the theorem does not need it.

**Edge cases.** The certificate is for $\ell_2$; an $\ell_\infty$ ball of radius $\varepsilon$ in $d$ dimensions contains $\ell_2$ perturbations of length up to $\varepsilon\sqrt{d}$, so $\ell_2$ certificates translate into weak $\ell_\infty$ ones for large images. The guarantee is about $g$, which is only available through sampling, so prediction is also randomized and costs $n$ forward passes per image.

### PatchCore step by step

This note gives the details of PatchCore's patch features, coreset selection and scoring that the main text summarized [42].

**Patch features with local context.** Let $\phi_j(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{c^* \times h^* \times w^*}$ be the feature map at level $j$ of an ImageNet-pre-trained network, with $c^*$ channels on an $h^* \times w^*$ grid, and $\phi_j(\mathbf{x}, h, w) \in \mathbb{R}^{c^*}$ the feature vector at grid position $(h, w)$. For an odd neighbourhood size $p$, the neighbourhood of $(h, w)$ is

$$
\mathcal{N}_p^{(h,w)} = \{(a, b) : |a - h| \le \lfloor p/2 \rfloor,\ |b - w| \le \lfloor p/2 \rfloor\},
$$

and the locally aware feature is an aggregation, adaptive average pooling in the paper, of the feature vectors in that neighbourhood. By default, features from blocks 2 and 3 of the network are combined, so that each patch feature mixes finer and coarser information [42]. The set of all such features over all normal training images forms the memory bank $\mathcal{M}$.

**Coreset selection.** The goal is a subset $\mathcal{M}_C \subset \mathcal{M}$ of a chosen size such that every feature in $\mathcal{M}$ is close to some feature in $\mathcal{M}_C$. PatchCore uses the minimax facility location objective

$$
\mathcal{M}_C^{*} = \arg\min_{\mathcal{M}_C \subset \mathcal{M}}\ \max_{\mathbf{m} \in \mathcal{M}}\ \min_{\mathbf{n} \in \mathcal{M}_C} \lVert \mathbf{m} - \mathbf{n} \rVert_2,
$$

the largest distance from any stored feature to its nearest kept feature [42]. This problem is NP-hard, so it is approximated greedily: start from one feature, then repeatedly add the feature whose distance to the current subset is largest (the `greedy_coreset` function in the main snippet). The paper also applies a random linear projection to reduce the dimension during selection, to save time. A random subsample of the same size would keep mostly common patches and drop rare normal ones, which then look anomalous at test time.

**Scoring.** For a test image with patch features $\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}})$, find the test patch that is farthest from its nearest memory feature,

$$
\mathbf{m}^{\text{test},*}, \mathbf{m}^{*} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{m}^{\text{test}} \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}})}\ \arg\min_{\mathbf{m} \in \mathcal{M}_C} \lVert \mathbf{m}^{\text{test}} - \mathbf{m} \rVert_2,
\qquad
s^{*} = \lVert \mathbf{m}^{\text{test},*} - \mathbf{m}^{*} \rVert_2 .
$$

The image score $s$ re-weights $s^*$ by how isolated $\mathbf{m}^*$ is within the memory bank: if the nearest memory feature is itself far from its own neighbours $\mathcal{N}_b(\mathbf{m}^*)$ (the $b$ nearest memory features), it is a rare normal pattern, and the score is increased [42]:

$$
s = \Big(1 - \frac{\exp \lVert \mathbf{m}^{\text{test},*} - \mathbf{m}^{*} \rVert_2}{\sum_{\mathbf{m} \in \mathcal{N}_b(\mathbf{m}^{*})} \exp \lVert \mathbf{m}^{\text{test},*} - \mathbf{m} \rVert_2}\Big)\, s^{*} .
$$

The per-patch distances, placed back at their grid positions, upsampled to the input resolution by bilinear interpolation and smoothed with a Gaussian, give the segmentation map [42].

**Pitfalls.** The memory bank grows with the number of training images and the feature resolution, so the coreset size trades speed against coverage. Normal variation that never appears in training (a new batch of material, new lighting) is flagged as anomalous, which is correct for a sensory anomaly detector but may not be what the factory wants; MVTec AD 2 adds lighting-change test scenarios for this reason [41]. And the ImageNet features may not suit very different imaging (X-ray, dark-field illumination).

### Madry et al., "Towards Deep Learning Models Resistant to Adversarial Attacks" (ICLR 2018)

#### Challenge

By 2017 many defenses against adversarial examples had been proposed and quickly broken, and there was no agreed definition of what a defense should achieve [23].

<Figure src={paperMadrySource} alt="Compare ordinary and robust decision boundaries around allowed perturbation neighborhoods; the figure illustrates a specified threat model rather than universal immunity." caption="Original paper figure 3 — Compare ordinary and robust decision boundaries around allowed perturbation neighborhoods; the figure illustrates a specified threat model rather than universal immunity." />

*Figure source: [23], arXiv:1706.06083v4, PDF page 9. [Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.06083).*

#### Method

State robustness as an optimization problem. The adversarial (robust) risk is the expected loss when an adversary may perturb each input within a set $\mathcal{S}$, and training minimizes it: the min-max problem in the main text. This one formula defines the attack (the inner maximization), the defense (the outer minimization) and the guarantee (a small value of the objective means low loss against every perturbation in $\mathcal{S}$). The authors argue that PGD with random starts is a strong, in some sense "universal", first-order adversary, and train against it.

#### Results

(1) The inner problem is tractable in practice: PGD from many random starts reaches loss values that are well concentrated, with no extreme outliers, suggesting that PGD finds near-maximal values within the reach of first-order methods. (2) Training against PGD reliably reduced the adversarial loss during training. (3) Capacity matters: larger networks were more robust, small networks trained against PGD could collapse, and FGSM-based training overfitted to its own narrow perturbations ("label leaking") without robustness to PGD at large $\varepsilon$. (4) The resulting MNIST ($\varepsilon = 0.3$) and CIFAR-10 ($\varepsilon = 8$ on the 0–255 scale) models resisted a range of white-box and transfer attacks, and the authors published them as open challenges.

#### Reading the evidence

Read Section 2 (the saddle-point formulation) and Section 3 (the loss-landscape experiments) first. In the result tables, each row is an attack: its source (white-box on the same model, or black-box from an independently trained copy, a naturally trained copy, or a different architecture), its method (FGSM, PGD with given steps and restarts, or PGD with the Carlini–Wagner loss), and the resulting accuracy. Check: (a) the threat model, $\ell_\infty$ with the stated $\varepsilon$ only; (b) that the strongest attack per column is the number to quote; (c) the additional experiments at other $\varepsilon$ values and with $\ell_2$-bounded attacks, which show how far robustness extends beyond the training threat model.

#### Problems

Robustness is shown only against the attacks tried and only for one norm and radius; it is empirical, not certified. Training cost grows with the number of PGD steps, and clean accuracy drops. Later work (TRADES, generated-data training, AutoAttack evaluations) refined both training and evaluation, but the formulation remained the reference point.

#### Conclusion

The chapter's adversarial-training code is exactly this recipe on a small scale, and the min-max formula is the common language of almost every later robustness paper.

### Athalye et al., "Obfuscated Gradients Give a False Sense of Security" (ICML 2018)

#### Challenge

Many defenses reported high robustness to white-box iterative attacks. Were they robust, or did they only prevent the attacker's gradient from being useful [29]?

<Figure src={paperAdaptiveAttack} alt="Teaching evaluation diagram — attack failure triggers a diagnosis and an adaptive attack; success or failure is checked on the actual defense under its original threat model." caption="Teaching evaluation diagram — attack failure triggers a diagnosis and an adaptive attack; success or failure is checked on the actual defense under its original threat model." />

#### Method

Identify the mechanism, gradient masking in the form of *obfuscated gradients*, and build attacks that do not rely on the masked gradient. The paper names three types (shattered, stochastic, and vanishing or exploding gradients), lists behaviors that reveal them (the warning signs in the main text), and for each type gives a counter-attack: BPDA, EOT and reparameterization.

#### Results

In a case study of the non-certified white-box defenses accepted at ICLR 2018, seven of nine relied on obfuscated gradients; the new attacks circumvented six completely and one partially, in the threat model each paper had considered [29]. Two certified defenses and one with only black-box claims were left out of the case study. The paper also stresses an asymmetry: to break a defense one attack suffices, while a defender must show that no attack within the threat model succeeds.

#### Reading the evidence

Read Section 3 (the three types and their symptoms) and Section 4 (the attacks) first, then Table 1, which lists each defense with its dataset, its perturbation bound and the accuracy left under the new attacks. Note the two adversarial-training defenses at the bottom of the table: the authors found that Madry et al.'s PGD training does *not* rely on obfuscated gradients. Check: (a) that each attack keeps the original paper's threat model and $\varepsilon$, so the numbers are a fair re-evaluation; (b) the per-defense sections, which show how BPDA's approximation was chosen in each case; (c) the evaluation recommendations in the discussion.

#### Problems

The analysis is a case study of one conference's defenses; it shows that these defenses fail, not that all defenses of a type must fail. Adaptive attacks require effort and expertise, which is the gap that AutoAttack later tried to close for standard settings [30].

#### Conclusion

It is the reason this chapter asks for white-box, adaptive and ensemble attacks before any robustness claim is believed, and the source of the warning signs used in Exercise 2.

#### Following an adaptive gradient

BPDA keeps the defense's actual forward transformation $h(x)$ but substitutes a differentiable approximation $g$ in the backward pass. If $v$ is the loss gradient arriving at that transformation's output, the approximate input gradient is $J_g(x)^\top v$, where $J_g$ is the Jacobian of $g$. For example, a rounding operation can remain in the forward pass while an identity approximation supplies its backward derivative. The attacker must evaluate the resulting candidate on the real rounded model, not on the surrogate alone [29].

For a randomized transformation $\tau$, EOT estimates the expected-loss gradient with samples:

$$
\nabla_x\mathbb E_{\tau}\mathcal L(f(\tau(x)),y)
\approx\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nabla_x\mathcal L(f(\tau_k(x)),y).
$$

$f$ is the defended predictor, $y$ the target label used by the attack objective $\mathcal L$, $K$ the sample count and $\tau_k$ sampled transformations. Differentiation/exchange of expectation needs the relevant regularity; nondifferentiable transformations may additionally require a backward approximation. Increasing attack effort changes the reliability of this empirical probe, not the permitted perturbation set [29].

### Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz, "In Search of Lost Domain Generalization" (ICLR 2021)

#### Challenge

Many domain generalization algorithms reported gains over plain training, but under different datasets, architectures and, above all, different (often unstated) rules for choosing hyperparameters, sometimes using the test domain [19].

<Figure src={paperDomainbedProtocol} alt="Teaching protocol diagram — source training and source validation select a frozen model before target testing; oracle target-validation results are a separate disclosed setting." caption="Teaching protocol diagram — source training and source validation select a frozen model before target testing; oracle target-validation results are a separate disclosed setting." />

#### Method

Treat model selection as part of the problem. A DG algorithm must come with a rule for choosing hyperparameters and checkpoints that uses only training domains. The paper defines three rules: a **training-domain validation set** (hold out part of each training domain), **leave-one-domain-out cross-validation** (train with one training domain held out, in turn, and average), and **test-domain validation**, an *oracle* that peeks at labelled test-domain data and must be disclosed as such. It then implements DomainBed, a testbed with seven multi-domain datasets (including Colored MNIST, Rotated MNIST, VLCS, PACS, Office-Home, TerraIncognita and DomainNet), nine algorithms and the three selection rules, with a random hyperparameter search of 20 trials per algorithm and test domain, and repeats the whole study three times.

#### Results

With a ResNet-50, strong data augmentation and careful tuning, ERM matched or beat all previously published results under training-domain validation, and no algorithm beat ERM by more than about a point on average under any selection rule. Training-domain validation worked better than leave-one-domain-out. The oracle rule gave higher numbers, which shows both possible headroom and how optimistic oracle-selected results are.

#### Reading the evidence

The main table reports, for each selection rule, the average accuracy over test domains per dataset, with standard errors over the three repetitions. Read across a row to compare an algorithm with ERM on the same footing, and compare the same algorithm between tables to see the effect of the selection rule. Check: (a) the standard errors before believing any difference; (b) the appendix, which collects results reported in the literature for more than thirty algorithms, to see how far published numbers depart from the controlled ones.

#### Problems

The conclusion is about these datasets, these algorithms and this tuning budget; a method may still help elsewhere. The datasets are small-to-medium image benchmarks; foundation-model backbones, which changed the field later, are not part of the study.

#### Conclusion

It is the clearest example of the chapter's recurring lesson: robustness gains disappear when baselines are tuned well and when test data stay out of model selection.
