- Derive least squares, ridge regression and their Bayesian interpretation, and see why least squares is a fragile classifier.

- Derive the maximum-margin SVM, its Lagrangian dual and its support vectors, and see all of these methods as different losses on the same linear score.

## The big picture

Linear models are the first serious classifiers in every pattern-recognition course, and they remain the first thing to try on a new representation. If a straight boundary separates two categories in feature space, a large nonlinear classifier may add cost without useful accuracy. If a linear baseline fails, its errors help identify missing features or nonlinear structure. In modern deep learning, the final layer of almost every classifier is a linear model on learned features, and "how well does a linear classifier do on these features?" is the standard test of a representation.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Each measurement gets a vote. Multiply each measurement by how strongly it votes, add everything up, and compare the total with a threshold: above means "yes", below means "no". All the methods in this chapter use exactly this voting machine. They only disagree about **how to choose the vote strengths**: make the totals match the right answers (least squares), spread the two groups apart (Fisher), fix mistakes one at a time (perceptron), predict honest probabilities (logistic regression), or leave the widest possible gap between the groups (SVM).
</Callout>

A linear score is $f(x)=w^\top\phi(x)+b$. The **feature map** $\phi$ might contain raw intensities, squared values, gradients or a frozen neural embedding. "Linear" refers to the parameters $w$ and $b$, not necessarily to the original measurement $x$: with $\phi(x)=(x,x^2)$ a linear model draws a curved boundary in $x$. This chapter follows Duda, Hart and Stork [1, Ch. 5], Bishop [2, Ch. 3–4, 7] and Theodoridis and Koutroumbas [3, Ch. 3], with Hastie et al. [4] for the statistical view. Kernels, which make $\phi$ implicit and very rich, are the topic of [PR 04](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/pr-04-kernels-nonlinear-classifiers/).

## Linear discriminant functions

**Plain version.** A two-class linear classifier is a flat cut through feature space: a line in 2-D, a plane in 3-D, a hyperplane in general. The weight vector says which way the cut faces; the bias says where it sits.

**Precise version.** For two classes, decide $c_1$ if $g(x)=w^\top x+b>0$ and $c_0$ otherwise. The decision boundary $g(x)=0$ is a hyperplane $H$. Three facts follow from elementary geometry [1, 2]:

- $w$ is **normal** to $H$: for two points $x_a,x_b$ on $H$, $w^\top(x_a-x_b)=0$.
- The **signed distance** from any $x$ to $H$ is $r=g(x)/\|w\|$. So $g(x)$ is proportional to how far, and on which side, $x$ lies.
- The distance from the origin to $H$ is $-b/\|w\|$; the bias shifts the plane without rotating it.

It is convenient to absorb the bias by **augmenting** the input with a constant: $\tilde x=(x,1)$ and $\tilde w=(w,b)$, so that $g(x)=\tilde w^\top\tilde x$. We do this silently in code. With labels $y\in\{-1,+1\}$, a sample is correctly classified exactly when its **functional margin** $y\,g(x)$ is positive. Every method below can be written in terms of this quantity.

**More than two classes.** Two-class machines can be combined in two obvious ways: **one-versus-rest** ($K$ classifiers, each separating one class from all others) and **one-versus-one** ($K(K-1)/2$ classifiers, one per pair, followed by voting). Both can leave regions of input space where the votes are ambiguous [2, Section 4.1.2]. The clean solution is a **linear machine**: one discriminant per class, $g_k(x)=w_k^\top x+b_k$, and assign $x$ to the class with the largest score. Its decision regions are always convex and simply connected, because if $x_a$ and $x_b$ both have $g_k$ largest, so does every point between them (each $g_j$ is linear along the segment). The Gaussian LDA of PR 01, multiclass logistic regression and the final layer of a neural network are all linear machines.

## Least squares and ridge regression

**Plain version.** For predicting a quantity, choose the weights that make the squared prediction errors as small as possible. If there are too many weights for the data, also penalize large weights.

**Precise version.** Let row $n$ of the **design matrix** $\Phi\in\mathbb R^{N\times M}$ be the feature vector $\phi(x_n)^\top$, including a constant coordinate for the intercept, and let $t\in\mathbb R^N$ hold the targets. Least squares minimizes

$$
\mathcal L(w)=\frac12\|\Phi w-t\|_2^2 ,
$$

whose gradient $\Phi^\top(\Phi w-t)$ vanishes at the **normal equations** $\Phi^\top\Phi w=\Phi^\top t$. Computing $(\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}$ explicitly is unnecessary and squares the condition number; QR- or SVD-based solvers (`np.linalg.lstsq`) handle near-dependent columns more reliably. Assuming independent Gaussian noise of common variance on the targets, this objective is exactly the negative log-likelihood, so least squares is the maximum-likelihood estimate (PR 02). The noise assumption explains the statistical interpretation; it does not make squared error suitable for every target.

**Ridge regression** [8] adds a penalty $\tfrac\lambda2\|w\|^2$, giving

$$
(\Phi^\top\Phi+\lambda I)\,w=\Phi^\top t .
$$

The matrix on the left is invertible for any $\lambda>0$, even when $M>N$. Usually the intercept is excluded from the penalty. Feature scaling matters, because the same penalty has different effects on coordinates measured in different units; fit scaling statistics on training data only.

### A Bayesian connection

With a Gaussian prior $w\sim\mathcal N(0,\alpha^{-1}I)$ (precision $\alpha$) and Gaussian noise of precision $\beta$, the posterior over weights is Gaussian with covariance and mean [2, Section 3.3]

$$
S=(\alpha I+\beta\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1},
\qquad m=\beta S\Phi^\top t .
$$

For a new feature vector $\phi_*$, the predictive distribution is

$$
p(t_*\mid x_*,D)=
\mathcal N\bigl(t_*\mid m^\top\phi_*,\ \beta^{-1}+\phi_*^\top S\phi_*\bigr).
$$

The first variance term is observation noise; the second expresses uncertainty about the weights, which is larger along feature directions that the training data rarely visited. The posterior mean $m$ equals the ridge solution with $\lambda=\alpha/\beta$: ridge is a MAP estimate, the same story as the Gaussian mean in PR 02.

## Least squares for classification

**Plain version.** You can treat class labels as numbers ($+1$ and $-1$, or one-hot vectors) and fit them with least squares. It is fast and has a closed form, but it is the wrong tool: it punishes points for being *too* correct.

**Precise version.** For $K$ classes, encode the targets as **one-hot** vectors $t_n\in\{0,1\}^K$ and fit a linear machine $g(x)=W^\top\tilde x$ by least squares, $W=(\tilde X^\top\tilde X)^{-1}\tilde X^\top T$, then classify by the largest output. For two classes with suitable target values ($N/N_1$ for class 1 and $-N/N_0$ for class 0), the least-squares direction is exactly Fisher's direction derived in the next section [2, Section 4.1.5], so the method is not unreasonable.

It has two well-known failure modes:

1. **Sensitivity to points far on the correct side.** Squared error penalizes $g(x)=5$ for a target of $+1$ as severely as $g(x)=-3$. Figure 1 adds twenty red points that are correctly classified but far away; least squares rotates its boundary to reduce their "error" and starts misclassifying blue points. Logistic regression barely moves.
2. **Masking with three or more classes.** When classes lie roughly along a line, the output for the middle class can be dominated everywhere by one of its neighbors, so the middle class is never predicted [4, Section 4.2].

In probabilistic terms, the Gaussian noise model behind least squares is simply wrong for binary targets. The remedy is to use a loss designed for classification, which the rest of the chapter does.

## Fisher's linear discriminant

**Plain version.** Project all points onto one line and look at the two classes' 1-D histograms. A good line makes the class means far apart *and* each class narrow. Fisher's direction is the line that maximizes this ratio.

**Precise version.** Fisher [5] proposed choosing a projection $z=w^\top x$ that maximizes the separation of the projected class means relative to the spread within each class. Let $m_k=\frac1{N_k}\sum_{n\in c_k}x_n$ be the class means. The projected means are $w^\top m_k$, and the projected within-class scatter of class $k$ is $\sum_{n\in c_k}(w^\top x_n-w^\top m_k)^2=w^\top S_kw$, with $S_k=\sum_{n\in c_k}(x_n-m_k)(x_n-m_k)^\top$. **Fisher's criterion** is

$$
J(w)=\frac{(w^\top m_1-w^\top m_0)^2}{w^\top S_0w+w^\top S_1w}
=\frac{w^\top S_Bw}{w^\top S_Ww},
$$

where

$$
S_B=(m_1-m_0)(m_1-m_0)^\top,\qquad S_W=S_0+S_1
$$

are the **between-class** and **within-class scatter matrices**. $J$ does not depend on the length of $w$, only on its direction.

**Derivation.** Setting the gradient of the ratio to zero gives

$$
(w^\top S_Bw)\,S_Ww=(w^\top S_Ww)\,S_Bw .
$$

Both bracketed terms are scalars. Moreover $S_Bw=(m_1-m_0)\,\bigl[(m_1-m_0)^\top w\bigr]$ always points along $m_1-m_0$. Hence $S_Ww\propto m_1-m_0$, and since only the direction matters,

$$
w^\star\propto S_W^{-1}(m_1-m_0).
$$

If $S_W$ is a multiple of the identity (isotropic, uncorrelated classes), Fisher's direction is just the mean difference. Otherwise $S_W^{-1}$ rotates it away from directions in which the classes are spread out. Figure 2 shows the effect for two elongated, correlated classes: projecting on the mean difference gives heavily overlapping histograms ($J=0.70$), while Fisher's direction separates them well ($J=3.18$).

<Figure src={fisherFig} alt="Left: two elongated correlated Gaussian classes with the mean-difference direction (gray, nearly horizontal) and the Fisher direction (black, tilted). Middle and right: histograms of the projections, heavily overlapping for the mean difference and well separated for Fisher." caption="Figure 2 — Two correlated classes projected onto the mean difference (middle, J = 0.70) and onto Fisher's direction (right, J = 3.18). Per-class covariances were used for S_W; with equal class sizes this only rescales J." />

**Relationship with Gaussian LDA.** Fisher's criterion makes no distributional assumption; it is a criterion for a *representation*. Yet its direction coincides with the Gaussian LDA direction $\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0)$ of [PR 01](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/pr-01-bayes-decisions/) when $S_W$ is (proportional to) the pooled within-class covariance. The two differ only in the threshold: Fisher leaves the cut point on the projected axis open, while Gaussian LDA fixes it from the class means and priors. So "LDA" is used for both. (In topic modelling, LDA also means latent Dirichlet allocation, which is unrelated.) Fisher projections do not by themselves provide calibrated probabilities.

**More than two classes.** With $K$ classes and overall mean $m$, define $S_W=\sum_kS_k$ and $S_B=\sum_kN_k(m_k-m)(m_k-m)^\top$. The projection $W\in\mathbb R^{d\times(K-1)}$ that maximizes the multiclass criterion is spanned by the leading eigenvectors of $S_W^{-1}S_B$ [1, 2]. Because $S_B$ is a sum of $K$ rank-one terms constrained by the overall mean, its rank is at most $K-1$, so Fisher can produce at most $K-1$ useful features. This is the supervised dimensionality reduction of [PR 05](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/pr-05-features-dimensionality/).

### Code: Fisher's direction

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import LinearDiscriminantAnalysis

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
cov = np.array([[3.0, 2.4], [2.4, 2.4]])
X0 = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], cov, 200)
X1 = rng.multivariate_normal([2, 0], cov, 200)
m0, m1 = X0.mean(0), X1.mean(0)
S_W = np.cov(X0.T) + np.cov(X1.T)       # within-class scatter (per-class covariances)
w = np.linalg.solve(S_W, m1 - m0)                          # Fisher direction

def J(v):                       # Fisher criterion for two classes
    return (v @ (m1 - m0)) ** 2 / (v @ S_W @ v)

lda = LinearDiscriminantAnalysis().fit(np.vstack([X0, X1]), np.repeat([0, 1], 200))
cos = w @ lda.coef_[0] / np.linalg.norm(w) / np.linalg.norm(lda.coef_[0])
angles = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 3601)
best = max(J(np.array([np.cos(a), np.sin(a)])) for a in angles)
print(f"J(mean difference) = {J(m1 - m0):.4f}, J(Fisher) = {J(w):.4f}, "
      f"grid maximum = {best:.4f}")
print(f"cosine between Fisher direction and LDA coef_: {cos:.6f}")
```

The closed form attains the grid maximum ($J=3.1841$), more than four times the mean-difference value ($0.6953$), and its direction agrees with scikit-learn's Gaussian LDA coefficients to six decimals (cosine $1.000000$).

## The perceptron

**Plain version.** Start with any line. Go through the training points; whenever one is on the wrong side, nudge the line towards classifying it correctly. If the classes can be separated by a line, this process is guaranteed to stop.

**Precise version.** Rosenblatt's perceptron [6] uses labels $y_n\in\{-1,+1\}$ and augmented features $\tilde x_n$. It minimizes the **perceptron criterion**

$$
J_P(w)=-\sum_{n\in\mathcal M}y_n\,w^\top\tilde x_n ,
$$

where $\mathcal M$ is the set of misclassified samples (those with $y_nw^\top\tilde x_n\le0$). Each term is non-negative, and $J_P=0$ exactly when every sample is correctly classified. Stochastic gradient descent on one misclassified sample at a time gives the update

$$
w\leftarrow w+\eta\,y_n\tilde x_n .
$$

With $w$ initialized at zero, the learning rate $\eta>0$ only rescales $w$ and does not change the sequence of decisions, so $\eta=1$ is common. The [IP 13 perceptron section](/en/learn/image-processing/dip-13-pattern-classification/) presents the same rule in the language of image patterns and connects it to multilayer networks.

**Convergence theorem.** Suppose the data are **linearly separable with margin** $\gamma>0$: some unit vector $u$ satisfies $y_nu^\top\tilde x_n\ge\gamma$ for all $n$. Let $R=\max_n\|\tilde x_n\|$. Then the perceptron (started at $w_0=0$) makes at most $(R/\gamma)^2$ updates. The proof takes three lines. After $t$ updates:

- each update adds $y_n\tilde x_n^\top u\ge\gamma$ to $w^\top u$, so $w_t^\top u\ge t\gamma$;
- each update happens on a mistake, where $y_nw^\top\tilde x_n\le0$, so $\|w_{t}\|^2=\|w_{t-1}\|^2+2y_nw_{t-1}^\top\tilde x_n+\|\tilde x_n\|^2\le\|w_{t-1}\|^2+R^2$, hence $\|w_t\|^2\le tR^2$;
- by Cauchy–Schwarz, $t\gamma\le w_t^\top u\le\|w_t\|\le\sqrt t\,R$, so $t\le R^2/\gamma^2$.

The bound does not depend on the number of samples or the dimension, only on how "fat" the gap between the classes is relative to the data's size.

**Limitations.** If the classes overlap, the perceptron never stops; the weights keep cycling (variants that keep the best weights seen so far are a common practical fix). Even when it converges, it stops at the *first* separating hyperplane it reaches, which depends on initialization and data order (Figure 3, left). And its score is not a probability. In terms of losses, the perceptron uses $\max(0,-y\,g(x))$, which is zero for any correct classification, however marginal. Both logistic regression and the SVM fix this by asking for more than "just barely correct".

### Code: perceptron updates versus the bound

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.svm import SVC

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
X = np.vstack([rng.normal([-1.0, -0.7], 0.6, (30, 2)),
               rng.normal([1.0, 0.7], 0.6, (30, 2))])
y = np.r_[-np.ones(30), np.ones(30)]
Phi = np.c_[X, np.ones(len(X))]          # augmented features (bias as last weight)

def perceptron(Phi, y, rng, max_epochs=1000):
    w, updates = np.zeros(Phi.shape[1]), 0
    for epoch in range(max_epochs):
        errors = 0
        for i in rng.permutation(len(y)):
            if y[i] * (w @ Phi[i]) <= 0:  # misclassified or on the boundary
                w += y[i] * Phi[i]        # learning rate 1 (scale is irrelevant)
                errors += 1
        updates += errors
        if errors == 0:
            return w, updates
    return w, updates

runs = [perceptron(Phi, y, np.random.default_rng(s)) for s in range(200)]
updates = np.array([u for _, u in runs])
print(f"updates over 200 random orders: mean {updates.mean():.1f}, "
      f"max {updates.max()}")
print("all runs separate the data:",
      all(np.all(np.sign(Phi @ w) == y) for w, _ in runs))

# convergence bound (R / gamma)^2, with gamma from the max-margin separator
svm = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1e6).fit(X, y)
u = np.r_[svm.coef_[0], svm.intercept_[0]]
gamma = np.min(y * (Phi @ u)) / np.linalg.norm(u)
R = np.max(np.linalg.norm(Phi, axis=1))
print(f"bound (R/gamma)^2 = {(R / gamma) ** 2:.1f}")
```

Over 200 random presentation orders the perceptron needs on average $2.7$ updates (at most $10$), and every run separates the data. The worst-case bound is $691$: it is a guarantee, not a prediction. The bound needs a valid margin $\gamma$; we take it from a hard-margin SVM (the subject of the SVM section below). Because the SVM does not penalize the bias, the true maximum margin in the augmented space may be slightly larger, which would give a slightly tighter bound.

## Logistic regression

**Plain version.** Instead of a hard yes/no, output a probability by squashing the linear score through an S-shaped curve. Fit the weights so that the probabilities assigned to the true labels are as high as possible.

**Precise version.** With labels $y\in\{0,1\}$, binary logistic regression [7] models

$$
P(y=1\mid x)=\sigma\bigl(w^\top\tilde x\bigr),
\qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac1{1+e^{-z}} .
$$

The score $w^\top\tilde x$ is the **log-odds** $\ln\frac{P(y=1\mid x)}{P(y=0\mid x)}$. This form is not arbitrary. PR 01 showed that for Gaussian classes with a shared covariance the posterior is exactly $\sigma(w^\top x+w_0)$ with $w=\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0)$; the same holds for many other class-conditional families. Logistic regression keeps the functional form of that posterior but fits $w$ directly, with $d+1$ parameters instead of the $2d+d(d+1)/2+1$ of the Gaussian model. Ng and Jordan [18] analysed this generative–discriminative trade-off (PR 02).

**Training.** The negative log-likelihood of the Bernoulli model is the **cross-entropy**

$$
E(w)=-\sum_{n=1}^N\Bigl[y_n\ln p_n+(1-y_n)\ln(1-p_n)\Bigr],\qquad p_n=\sigma(w^\top\tilde x_n).
$$

Because $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, its gradient and Hessian are remarkably simple [2, Section 4.3]:

$$
\nabla E=\sum_n(p_n-y_n)\,\tilde x_n=\tilde X^\top(p-y),
\qquad
H=\tilde X^\top R\,\tilde X,\quad R=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(p_n(1-p_n)\bigr).
$$

The gradient has the same "prediction minus target, times input" form as least squares. $H$ is positive semidefinite, so $E$ is convex and has no spurious local minima. Newton's method, $w\leftarrow w-H^{-1}\nabla E$, can be rewritten as a weighted least-squares problem with weights $R$ that change every iteration: **iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS)**. In code, compute losses from logits with a stable formula such as $\ln(1+e^z)=$ `np.logaddexp(0, z)` rather than taking logarithms of rounded probabilities.

**Separable data.** If a hyperplane separates the training classes, scaling $w$ up pushes every $p_n$ closer to its label, so $E$ keeps decreasing towards zero and the unregularized solution does not exist: $\|w\|\to\infty$. In practice, always regularize, e.g. add $\tfrac\lambda2\|w\|^2$ (scikit-learn's `C` is $1/\lambda$). Remarkably, *how* the weights diverge is well defined: plain gradient descent on separable data converges in direction to the maximum-margin SVM solution [19], which the Modern view returns to.

**Multiclass.** Replace the sigmoid with the **softmax** $P(y=k\mid x)=\exp(w_k^\top\tilde x)/\sum_j\exp(w_j^\top\tilde x)$ and the cross-entropy with its $K$-class version. This is a linear machine with probabilistic outputs, and it is exactly the last layer plus loss of a standard neural classifier. Probability outputs are not automatically calibrated under label noise or distribution shift (PR 01).

### Code: Newton's method (IRLS) and separable data

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = np.vstack([rng.normal(-1, 1, (100, 2)), rng.normal(1, 1, (100, 2))])
y = np.r_[np.zeros(100), np.ones(100)]
Phi = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X]

def nll(w, lam):
    z = Phi @ w
    # log(1 + e^z) - y z, written stably with logaddexp
    return np.sum(np.logaddexp(0, z) - y * z) + 0.5 * lam * np.sum(w[1:] ** 2)

# Newton's method (iteratively reweighted least squares)
lam, w = 1.0, np.zeros(3)
for it in range(20):
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(Phi @ w)))
    grad = Phi.T @ (p - y) + lam * np.r_[0, w[1:]]
    H = Phi.T @ (Phi * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]) + lam * np.diag([0, 1, 1])
    step = np.linalg.solve(H, grad)
    w -= step
    if np.linalg.norm(step) < 1e-10:
        break
sk = LogisticRegression(C=1 / lam, tol=1e-10, max_iter=1000).fit(X, y)
print(f"Newton converged in {it + 1} iterations, loss {nll(w, lam):.4f}")
print("Newton:      ", np.round(w, 4))
print("scikit-learn:", np.round(np.r_[sk.intercept_, sk.coef_[0]], 4))
```

Newton's method converges in $8$ iterations to the same weights as scikit-learn, $(0.0777, 1.7205, 1.5300)$ for (intercept, $w_1$, $w_2$). The penalty excludes the intercept, matching scikit-learn's convention.

The next snippet runs plain gradient descent on *unregularized* logistic loss for data that a line through the origin separates. It tracks the weight norm and the cosine between $w$ and the max-margin direction.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.svm import LinearSVC

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
X = np.vstack([rng.normal([2.0, 1.0], 0.6, (40, 2)),
               rng.normal([-2.0, -1.0], 0.6, (40, 2))])
y = np.r_[np.ones(40), -np.ones(40)]              # separable through the origin

# max-margin direction without bias (hard margin approximated by large C)
svm = LinearSVC(fit_intercept=False, loss="hinge", C=1e4, max_iter=200000).fit(X, y)
u = svm.coef_[0] / np.linalg.norm(svm.coef_[0])

w, lr = np.zeros(2), 0.1
for t in range(1, 100001):
    m = y * (X @ w)
    grad = -(X * (y / (1 + np.exp(m)))[:, None]).mean(0)   # logistic loss gradient
    w -= lr * grad
    if t in (10, 100, 1000, 10000, 100000):
        print(f"step {t:6d}: ||w|| = {np.linalg.norm(w):6.2f}, "
              f"cos(w, SVM) = {w @ u / np.linalg.norm(w):.5f}")
```

The norm grows without bound but only slowly ($0.68$ after 10 steps, $8.64$ after $10^5$), while the cosine with the SVM direction creeps from $0.87$ to $0.98$. This is the logarithmically slow convergence to the max-margin direction predicted by Soudry et al. [19].

## Support vector machines: maximum margin

**Plain version.** Among all lines that separate the classes, pick the one with the widest empty "street" around it. Only the points on the edges of the street matter; move any other point (without crossing the street) and the answer does not change.

**Precise version.** Use labels $y_n\in\{-1,+1\}$ and score $f(x)=w^\top x+b$ (bias kept separate here). For a separating hyperplane, the distance of $x_n$ to the boundary is $y_nf(x_n)/\|w\|$. Since $(w,b)$ and $(cw,cb)$ define the same plane, fix the scale so that the closest points satisfy $y_nf(x_n)=1$ (the **canonical** form). The margin on each side is then $1/\|w\|$ and the street has width $2/\|w\|$. Maximizing it is equivalent to the convex quadratic program [9, 10]

$$
\min_{w,b}\ \frac12\|w\|^2
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
y_n(w^\top x_n+b)\ge1,\quad n=1,\ldots,N .
$$

**Lagrangian and dual.** Introduce multipliers $a_n\ge0$, one per constraint:

$$
\mathcal L(w,b,a)=\frac12\|w\|^2-\sum_n a_n\bigl[y_n(w^\top x_n+b)-1\bigr].
$$

Setting $\partial\mathcal L/\partial w=0$ and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial b=0$ gives

$$
w=\sum_n a_ny_n\,x_n,\qquad \sum_n a_ny_n=0 .
$$

Substituting back eliminates $w$ and $b$ and leaves the **dual problem**

$$
\max_{a}\ \sum_n a_n-\frac12\sum_n\sum_m a_na_my_ny_m\,x_n^\top x_m
\quad\text{subject to}\quad a_n\ge0,\ \ \sum_n a_ny_n=0 .
$$

Three consequences matter [2, 11]:

- **Support vectors.** The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions include **complementary slackness**, $a_n\bigl[y_nf(x_n)-1\bigr]=0$. So $a_n>0$ only for points exactly on the margin. These are the **support vectors**; all other points have $a_n=0$ and can be removed without changing the solution. The bias follows from any support vector $s$: $b=y_s-w^\top x_s$ (average over all of them for numerical stability).
- **Sparsity of the prediction.** $f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathcal S}a_ny_n\,x_n^\top x+b$ depends only on support vectors $\mathcal S$.
- **Only inner products.** The data appear in the dual and in the prediction only through inner products $x_n^\top x_m$. Replacing them with a kernel $k(x_n,x_m)$ gives a nonlinear SVM without ever computing $\phi$. That is the **kernel trick**, developed in [PR 04](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/pr-04-kernels-nonlinear-classifiers/).

**Soft margin and the hinge loss.** Real classes overlap, so the constraints cannot all hold. Cortes and Vapnik [10] added **slack variables** $\xi_n\ge0$, constraints $y_nf(x_n)\ge1-\xi_n$, and a penalty $C\sum_n\xi_n$. At the optimum $\xi_n=\max(0,1-y_nf(x_n))$, so the soft-margin SVM is equivalent to the unconstrained problem

$$
\min_{w,b}\ \frac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_n\max\bigl(0,\ 1-y_nf(x_n)\bigr).
$$

The second term is the **hinge loss**: zero for points beyond the margin, linear inside it and on the wrong side. In the dual the only change is a box constraint $0\le a_n\le C$. So a linear SVM is "ridge-regularized hinge-loss minimization", directly comparable to regularized logistic regression. The roles of $C$, kernel bandwidth and feature scaling, and the fact that an SVM score is not a probability, are treated in PR 04.

### Code: the dual in practice

This check fits a (nearly) hard-margin linear SVM and verifies the dual relations numerically: $w=\sum_na_ny_nx_n$, $\sum_na_ny_n=0$, and $y_nf(x_n)=1$ on the support vectors.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.svm import SVC

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
X = np.vstack([rng.normal([-1.5, -1.0], 0.6, (30, 2)),
               rng.normal([1.5, 1.0], 0.6, (30, 2))])
y = np.r_[-np.ones(30), np.ones(30)]

svm = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1e6).fit(X, y)          # huge C ~ hard margin
alpha_y = svm.dual_coef_[0]                          # alpha_n * y_n for SVs
w_dual = alpha_y @ svm.support_vectors_              # w = sum_n alpha_n y_n x_n
f = X @ svm.coef_[0] + svm.intercept_[0]

print("support vectors:", len(svm.support_), "of", len(y))
print("w from dual:", np.round(w_dual, 4), " coef_:", np.round(svm.coef_[0], 4))
print("sum_n alpha_n y_n =", round(float(alpha_y.sum()), 8))
print("y f(x) on support vectors:", np.round(y[svm.support_] * f[svm.support_], 4))
print("smallest y f(x) over all points:", round(float(np.min(y * f)), 4))
print("margin width 2/||w|| =", round(2 / np.linalg.norm(svm.coef_[0]), 4))
```

Only $3$ of the $60$ points are support vectors (the circled points in Figure 3). The weight vector rebuilt from the dual coefficients matches `coef_` exactly, the dual coefficients sum to zero, the support vectors sit at $y f(x)\approx1$ (to solver tolerance), no point is inside the margin, and the margin width is $1.60$. scikit-learn's `SVC` wraps LIBSVM [16]; for large sparse problems, `LinearSVC` and `LogisticRegression(solver="liblinear")` use LIBLINEAR [14], and stochastic sub-gradient solvers such as Pegasos [15] scale to very large datasets.

## One picture: linear classifiers as losses

**Plain version.** All the methods in this chapter score a point with the same linear function. They differ in how much they charge for each value of the margin $y\,g(x)$: nothing once correct (perceptron), nothing once safely correct (SVM), a smoothly decreasing amount (logistic), or a parabola centred at exactly $1$ (least squares).

**Precise version.** With labels $y\in\{-1,+1\}$ and margin $m=y\,g(x)$, each method minimizes a (possibly regularized) average of a **surrogate loss** $\varphi(m)$ that stands in for the non-convex zero-one loss $\mathbb 1[m\le0]$ (Figure 4):

| Method | Loss $\varphi(m)$ | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Perceptron | $\max(0,-m)$ | zero for any correct point; scale-free, so $w=0$ is a trivial minimizer |
| Hinge (SVM) | $\max(0,1-m)$ | zero beyond the margin; sparse solutions (support vectors) |
| Logistic | $\ln(1+e^{-m})$ | smooth, never exactly zero; gives probabilities |
| Squared | $(1-m)^2=(y-g(x))^2$ | penalizes $m>1$: "too correct" points pull the boundary |

<Figure src={lossFig} alt="Plot of five loss functions against the margin m: the zero-one step, perceptron loss max(0, -m), hinge max(0, 1 - m), logistic log2(1 + exp(-m)) and squared (1 - m)^2, which rises again for m greater than 1." caption="Figure 4 — Surrogate losses as functions of the margin m = y f(x). The logistic loss is scaled by 1/ln 2 so that it passes through (0, 1). Only the squared loss increases for m > 1, which explains Figure 1." />

Which surrogates lead to the Bayes-optimal classifier when there is enough data? Bartlett, Jordan and McAuliffe [12] answered this precisely: a convex surrogate is **classification-calibrated** (minimizing its expected value yields the Bayes decision $\operatorname{sign}(2\eta(x)-1)$, with $\eta(x)=P(y=1\mid x)$) if and only if it is differentiable at $0$ with $\varphi'(0)\lt0$. Hinge, logistic, squared and exponential losses all qualify; the perceptron loss does not. They also relate the excess surrogate risk to the excess classification risk. Rosasco et al. [13] compared the convergence rates implied by different convex losses and concluded that, for classification, the hinge loss converges about as fast as the logistic loss and much faster than the square loss. The losses still differ in *what* they estimate: minimizing the expected logistic loss pointwise gives $g(x)=\ln\frac{\eta(x)}{1-\eta(x)}$, so its scores are log-odds (probabilities after a sigmoid), whereas the pointwise minimizer of the expected hinge loss is $\operatorname{sign}(2\eta(x)-1)$, which carries no probability information. That is why SVM scores need separate calibration.

### Code: compare linear classifiers on digits

The built-in digits dataset (8×8 images, 10 classes) requires no download. This is a single reproducible hold-out demonstration, not a benchmark ranking. Every scaler is fitted inside a pipeline, on training data only.

```python
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import LinearDiscriminantAnalysis
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression, Perceptron, RidgeClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import LinearSVC

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=7)
models = {
    "least squares (ridge)": make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), RidgeClassifier(alpha=1.0)),
    "perceptron": make_pipeline(StandardScaler(),
                                Perceptron(max_iter=1500, random_state=7)),
    "logistic": make_pipeline(StandardScaler(),
                              LogisticRegression(C=1, max_iter=1500)),
    "linear SVM": make_pipeline(StandardScaler(),
                                LinearSVC(C=0.1, max_iter=20000, random_state=7)),
    "Gaussian LDA": LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(solver="lsqr", shrinkage="auto"),
}
for name, model in models.items():
    model.fit(Xtr, ytr)
    print(f"{name:22s} test accuracy: {accuracy_score(yte, model.predict(Xte)):.4f}")
```

On this split, logistic regression reaches $97.1\%$, the linear SVM $96.4\%$, the perceptron $95.1\%$, and ridge-regularized least squares and shrinkage LDA $94.7\%$ each. All five are linear machines on the same 64 pixels; the differences come from the losses and regularization alone. Changing regularization after seeing these test results would turn this test set into a validation set. A real model-selection study needs a separate validation split or cross-validation inside the training partition ([PR 09](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/pr-09-ensembles-validation/)).

## Modern view

Linear classifiers have not been replaced by deep learning; they have moved to the top of it. Three lines of recent work explain why they still matter, and the classic survey literature remains useful for the methods themselves.

**Surveys and solvers.** Burges's tutorial [11] remains the most readable derivation of SVMs, from VC-dimension motivation through the dual and kernels. Cervantes et al. [17] survey SVM classification as of 2020: variants, applications, and challenges such as training cost on large datasets, class imbalance and model selection. On the computational side, LIBLINEAR [14] made linear SVMs and logistic regression with millions of sparse features routine, LIBSVM [16] is the reference kernel-SVM implementation behind scikit-learn's `SVC`, and Pegasos [15] showed that stochastic sub-gradient descent on the SVM primal reaches a given accuracy with a run time that, for linear kernels, does not depend directly on the training-set size, the same idea as training neural networks with SGD.

**Linear probes as the yardstick of representations.** Alain and Bengio [21] proposed training independent linear classifiers ("probes") on every layer of a network, and observed that linear separability increases monotonically with depth. Kornblith, Shlens and Le [22] compared 16 networks on 12 datasets and found that, with features used as fixed extractors under a logistic-regression classifier, ImageNet accuracy and transfer accuracy are strongly correlated, though some regularizers that slightly improve ImageNet accuracy produce much worse penultimate-layer features. CLIP [23] compares its zero-shot classifier with logistic-regression probes on frozen features, and DINOv2 [24] evaluates its self-supervised features largely with linear classifiers on frozen backbones. Kumar et al. [25] showed that full fine-tuning can distort good pretrained features and underperform a linear probe out of distribution, and proposed fitting a linear probe first and then fine-tuning (LP-FT). In other words: a well-regularized logistic regression on top of a frozen foundation model is a strong baseline, and often the right first experiment.

**Implicit bias: logistic regression secretly finds the SVM.** Soudry et al. [19] proved that gradient descent on unregularized logistic (or cross-entropy) loss over linearly separable data converges *in direction* to the hard-margin SVM solution, extending the result to other losses with exponential tails, to multiclass problems and to a restricted deep-network setting. Convergence is only logarithmic, which helps explain why continuing to train after zero training error can keep improving the margin. The gradient-descent code above shows the effect in two dimensions.

**Neural collapse: Fisher's picture at the end of training.** Papyan, Han and Donoho [20] observed, across three architectures and seven datasets, that in the terminal phase of training (after zero training error) the last-layer features of each class collapse to their class mean, the class means arrange themselves into a maximally separated, symmetric configuration (a simplex equiangular tight frame), the classifier weights align with the class means, and the decision rule becomes the nearest class mean. In this chapter's language, the within-class scatter $S_W$ shrinks towards zero, Fisher's criterion grows without bound, and the network's last layer becomes the minimum-distance classifier of PR 01.

**Open problems.** When should one trust a linear probe as a measure of representation quality, given that probe results depend on regularization and data size? How do implicit-bias results extend to realistic, non-separable, data-augmented training? And when do probabilities from linear heads stay calibrated under distribution shift? These questions link the oldest classifier in this track to current research.

## Key takeaways

- A linear discriminant $g(x)=w^\top x+b$ is a hyperplane with normal $w$; $g(x)/\|w\|$ is the signed distance. Use one discriminant per class (a linear machine) for multiclass problems.
- Least squares and ridge have closed forms and a Bayesian MAP interpretation, but least squares is a poor classifier: it punishes points that are "too correct" and can mask classes.
- Fisher's direction $w\propto S_W^{-1}(m_1-m_0)$ maximizes between-class over within-class spread; it equals the Gaussian LDA direction, which adds a threshold from means and priors.
- The perceptron converges in at most $(R/\gamma)^2$ updates on separable data, but stops at an arbitrary separator and cycles on overlapping data.
- Logistic regression fits the log-odds by convex cross-entropy minimization (Newton/IRLS); without regularization its weights diverge on separable data, in the direction of the max-margin solution.
- The hard-margin SVM maximizes the margin $2/\|w\|$; its dual shows that only support vectors matter and that data enter only through inner products, which opens the door to kernels (PR 04). The soft margin is ridge-regularized hinge loss.
- All these methods are one linear score with different surrogate losses; hinge, logistic and squared losses are classification-calibrated, but only the logistic loss estimates probabilities.

1. **Linear in the parameters.** Show that fitting $g(x)=w_0+w_1x+w_2x^2$ by least squares is a linear regression problem, and describe the decision regions in $x$ if the same features are used for a two-class linear classifier.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Build rows $(1,x,x^2)$ of a design matrix; the model is linear in $(w_0,w_1,w_2)$. A quadratic in $x$ changes sign at most twice, so the region for one class can be an interval and the other class its complement (two half-lines).

</details>

2. **Units and ridge.** Two features measure the same distance, one in metres and one in millimetres. Explain what ridge regression does without feature scaling, and why standardizing fixes it.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The millimetre coefficient can be 1,000 times smaller while describing the same effect, so it is penalized far less; ridge will load almost all weight onto the millimetre feature. After standardization both coordinates are penalized on the same scale.

</details>

3. **Fisher by hand.** Two classes have means $m_0=(0,0)$, $m_1=(2,0)$ and within-class scatter $S_W=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$. Compute Fisher's direction and compare it with the mean difference. When would the two directions coincide?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

$S_W^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$, so $w\propto S_W^{-1}(2,0)^\top=\tfrac13(4,-2)\propto(2,-1)$, rotated away from the mean difference $(1,0)$ because the classes are stretched along $(1,1)$. The directions coincide exactly when $m_1-m_0$ is an eigenvector of $S_W$, e.g. for $m_1=(2,2)$.

</details>

4. **A perceptron that never stops.** Construct a one-dimensional dataset on which the perceptron updates forever, and explain which assumption of the convergence theorem fails.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Put two points at the same coordinate with opposite labels (or any overlapping classes). No $u$ with $\gamma>0$ exists, so the bound does not apply; each update that fixes one point breaks the other.

</details>

5. **SVM by hand.** Find the hard-margin SVM for the two points $x_1=(1,1)$ with $y_1=+1$ and $x_2=(-1,-1)$ with $y_2=-1$. Give $w$, $b$, the dual variables and the margin width, and check the KKT conditions.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

By symmetry $b=0$ and $w=c(1,1)$. The constraint $y_1w^\top x_1=1$ gives $2c=1$, so $w=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ and the width is $2/\|w\|=2\sqrt2$, the distance between the points. From $w=a_1x_1-a_2x_2$ and $a_1=a_2$, $a_1=a_2=\tfrac14$. Both points are support vectors.

</details>

6. **Logistic versus hinge.** Using the digits pipeline, fit `LogisticRegression` and `LinearSVC` for several values of `C` with 5-fold cross-validation on the training set only. Compare their best validation accuracies, then compare their scores: which one gives meaningful probabilities, and how would you obtain probabilities from the other?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Accuracies are typically close, as the loss analysis predicts. Only logistic regression's `predict_proba` estimates posteriors; for the SVM, fit a calibration map (Platt scaling or isotonic regression, e.g. `CalibratedClassifierCV`) on held-out data.

</details>
