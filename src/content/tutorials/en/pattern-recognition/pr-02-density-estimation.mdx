## What you'll learn

- Why a Bayes classifier needs density estimates, and the difference between parametric and non-parametric estimation.
- How to derive maximum-likelihood (ML) estimates for a Gaussian, why the covariance becomes singular with few samples, and how shrinkage fixes it.
- How MAP and full Bayesian estimation use a prior over parameters, worked out for a Gaussian mean and a Bernoulli rate.
- How histograms, Parzen windows (kernel density estimation) and $k$-nearest-neighbor estimates follow from one formula, and how to choose the bandwidth or $k$.
- How the $k$-NN classifier falls out of $k$-NN density estimation, what the Cover–Hart bound says, and why all local methods suffer from the curse of dimensionality.

## The big picture

[PR 01](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/bayes-decisions/) showed that the best possible classifier needs the class priors $P(c_k)$ and the class-conditional densities $p(x\mid c_k)$. In practice nobody hands us these distributions; we have a finite set of labelled examples. This chapter is about turning examples into densities. Once we have $\hat p(x\mid c_k)$ and $\hat P(c_k)$, we can plug them into the Bayes rule. This is the **generative** route to classification. The next chapter takes the **discriminative** route and fits the decision boundary directly.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Put a sticker on a park map wherever you have seen a bird. Now guess where the next bird will be. You could draw **one big oval** around most stickers (a parametric model: you only decide the oval's center and shape). You could lay a **grid** over the map and count stickers per square (a histogram). Or you could put a **little sand hill** on every sticker and look at where the sand piles up highest (a kernel estimate). Each choice is a different way to guess, and each can go wrong: the oval is too simple if birds like two different ponds, and the sand hills are too bumpy if you only have five stickers.
</Callout>

There are two families of estimators [1, 2, 3]:

- **Parametric** methods assume a functional form, such as a Gaussian, and estimate a fixed number of parameters. They need little data when the form is right and give systematically wrong answers when it is wrong.
- **Non-parametric** methods let the data decide the shape. Their complexity grows with the number of samples. They are flexible but need much more data, especially in high dimensions.

Both families face the same central question: how much structure can the available data support? A flexible estimator can describe the training data beautifully and still predict new data poorly. Every method below has a "smoothness knob" (the number of parameters, the prior, the bin width, the bandwidth, $k$), and choosing it well matters more than choosing the method.

## Maximum-likelihood estimation

**Plain version.** Choose the parameter values under which the observed data would have been most probable.

**Precise version.** Let $D=\{x_1,\ldots,x_N\}$ be samples drawn independently from a density $p(x\mid\theta)$ with unknown parameter vector $\theta$. The **likelihood** of $\theta$ is $p(D\mid\theta)=\prod_n p(x_n\mid\theta)$, and the ML estimate maximizes its logarithm:

$$
\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}}
=\arg\max_\theta\ \ell(\theta),\qquad
\ell(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^N\log p(x_n\mid\theta).
$$

The independence assumption is about *sampling* (different observations do not influence each other), not about independence between the coordinates of one feature vector. For a classifier we fit one model per class, using only that class's samples, and estimate the prior as the class frequency $\hat P(c_k)=N_k/N$.

### The Gaussian case

For a $d$-dimensional Gaussian $\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ the log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\mu,\Sigma)=-\frac N2\log|\Sigma|-\frac12\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x_n-\mu)+\text{const}.
$$

Setting the gradient with respect to $\mu$ to zero gives $\Sigma^{-1}\sum_n(x_n-\mu)=0$. Differentiating with respect to $\Sigma^{-1}$ (using $\partial\log|A|/\partial A=A^{-\top}$ and $\partial\, a^\top Aa/\partial A=aa^\top$) gives the estimates

$$
\hat\mu=\frac1N\sum_n x_n,\qquad
\hat\Sigma=\frac1N\sum_n(x_n-\hat\mu)(x_n-\hat\mu)^\top.
$$

The sample mean is unbiased. The ML covariance is slightly **biased**: $\mathbb E[\hat\Sigma]=\tfrac{N-1}N\Sigma$, because $\hat\mu$ was fitted to the same data and sits closer to them than the true mean does. The familiar sample covariance divides by $N-1$ to remove this bias. For large $N$ the difference is negligible.

### When ML breaks: too many parameters

A full covariance has $d(d+1)/2$ distinct entries. With $d=64$ features that is $2{,}080$ numbers per class, plus $64$ for the mean. Worse, $\hat\Sigma$ is a sum of $N$ rank-one matrices centered at $\hat\mu$, so its rank is at most $N-1$. **When a class has no more samples than dimensions, its ML covariance is singular**: it has zero variance in some directions, $\log|\hat\Sigma|=-\infty$, and the Gaussian discriminant of PR 01 is undefined. Even when $N$ is a bit larger than $d$, the smallest eigenvalues are systematically underestimated, and the inverse $\hat\Sigma^{-1}$ hugely amplifies noise along those directions.

Regularization **shrinks** the covariance towards a simpler target:

$$
\tilde\Sigma=(1-\alpha)\hat\Sigma+\alpha\tau I,\qquad
\tau=\operatorname{tr}(\hat\Sigma)/d,\qquad 0\le\alpha\le1 .
$$

Here $\tau I$ is a spherical matrix with the same average variance, and $\alpha$ is the shrinkage intensity. Shrinkage pulls large eigenvalues down and small ones up, trading a little bias for a much better-conditioned estimate. Ledoit and Wolf [14] derived a closed-form estimate of the $\alpha$ that minimizes the expected squared error, so it needs no cross-validation. Other common restrictions are a **diagonal** covariance (ignore correlations; this is Gaussian naive Bayes, below) and a **shared** covariance across classes (pool the evidence; this is LDA from PR 01). These are different modelling assumptions, not interchangeable numerical tricks.

### Code: when the ML covariance fails

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal
from sklearn.covariance import EmpiricalCovariance, LedoitWolf

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d = 40
A = rng.normal(size=(d, d))
true_cov = A @ A.T / d + 0.5 * np.eye(d)          # a well-conditioned truth
test = rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(d), true_cov, 2000)

for n in [30, 60, 200, 1000]:
    train = rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(d), true_cov, n)
    mu = train.mean(0)
    S_ml = EmpiricalCovariance().fit(train).covariance_   # divisor n
    S_lw = LedoitWolf().fit(train)
    rank = np.linalg.matrix_rank(S_ml)
    ll_ml = (multivariate_normal(mu, S_ml, allow_singular=True).logpdf(test).mean()
             if rank == d else -np.inf)
    ll_lw = multivariate_normal(mu, S_lw.covariance_).logpdf(test).mean()
    print(f"n={n:4d}: rank(S_ML)={rank:2d}, test log-lik ML={ll_ml:8.2f}, "
          f"Ledoit-Wolf={ll_lw:8.2f} (shrinkage {S_lw.shrinkage_:.2f})")
```

With $d=40$ and $n=30$ the ML covariance has rank $29$ and assigns zero density to almost every test point (log-likelihood $-\infty$). At $n=60$ it is invertible but poor (test log-likelihood $-94.6$ versus $-63.0$ for Ledoit–Wolf). The automatically chosen shrinkage falls from $0.65$ to $0.08$ as $n$ grows, and at $n=1000$ the two estimates almost coincide. The rule of thumb: the fewer samples per parameter, the more you should shrink.

## MAP and Bayesian estimation

**Plain version.** Maximum likelihood trusts the data completely. Bayesian estimation starts from a prior belief about the parameters and lets the data gradually overrule it. With little data the prior matters; with lots of data it hardly does.

**Precise version.** Treat $\theta$ as a random variable with a **parameter prior** $p(\theta)$. Bayes' rule gives the parameter posterior

$$
p(\theta\mid D)=\frac{p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{\int p(D\mid\theta')\,p(\theta')\,d\theta'}\;\propto\;p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta).
$$

A parameter prior is different from the class prior of PR 01. The class prior says how common a category is; a parameter prior says which model settings are plausible before seeing this training set. Three ways to use the posterior are common:

- **MAP estimation** keeps only its mode: $\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\arg\max_\theta\,[\log p(D\mid\theta)+\log p(\theta)]$. The log prior acts as a regularizer; a Gaussian prior on weights is exactly an $L_2$ (ridge) penalty, which PR 03 uses.
- **Full Bayesian prediction** averages over all parameter values instead of choosing one:

$$
p(x_{\mathrm{new}}\mid D)=\int p(x_{\mathrm{new}}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid D)\,d\theta .
$$

  This **posterior predictive** distribution carries parameter uncertainty into the prediction.
- **Plug-in prediction** uses $p(x_{\mathrm{new}}\mid\hat\theta)$ with $\hat\theta$ from ML or MAP. It ignores parameter uncertainty and is therefore overconfident when data are scarce.

One subtle point: MAP is not invariant to re-parameterization. The mode of a density over $\sigma$ is not the square root of the mode of the corresponding density over $\sigma^2$, because densities pick up a Jacobian factor when the variable changes. ML estimates and full posteriors do not have this problem.

### Worked example: the mean of a Gaussian

Let $x_n\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with known $\sigma^2$, and put a Gaussian prior on the unknown mean, $\mu\sim\mathcal N(m_0,s_0^2)$. Multiplying the likelihood and the prior and completing the square in $\mu$ shows that the posterior is again Gaussian, $\mu\mid D\sim\mathcal N(m_N,s_N^2)$, with [1, 2]

$$
\frac1{s_N^2}=\frac1{s_0^2}+\frac N{\sigma^2},\qquad
m_N=s_N^2\left(\frac{m_0}{s_0^2}+\frac{N\bar x}{\sigma^2}\right),
$$

where $\bar x$ is the sample mean. Two readings make this formula memorable. **Precisions add**: the posterior precision $1/s_N^2$ is the prior precision plus $N$ times the precision of one observation. **The mean is a precision-weighted average** of the prior mean and the sample mean. As $N\to\infty$, $m_N\to\bar x$ (the ML estimate) and $s_N\to0$. Since the posterior is Gaussian, its mode equals its mean, so here $\hat\mu_{\mathrm{MAP}}=m_N$. The posterior predictive is $\mathcal N(m_N,\ \sigma^2+s_N^2)$: wider than the plug-in $\mathcal N(m_N,\sigma^2)$ by the remaining uncertainty about $\mu$.

A prior whose posterior stays in the same family is called **conjugate**. Conjugate pairs (Gaussian–Gaussian, Beta–Bernoulli, Dirichlet–categorical, Normal–inverse-Wishart for a mean and covariance) give closed-form updates; other priors require the approximate inference methods of [PR 08](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/approximate-inference/).

### Worked example: a Bernoulli rate

For a Bernoulli rate $q$ (for instance the defect rate on a line) with prior $\operatorname{Beta}(a,b)$, observing $s$ successes in $N$ trials gives

$$
q\mid D\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a+s,\ b+N-s),
\qquad
p(x_{\mathrm{new}}=1\mid D)=\frac{a+s}{a+b+N}.
$$

The prior acts like $a$ imaginary successes and $b$ imaginary failures. With one success and no failures, ML predicts probability one, which is absurd. A uniform prior $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ predicts $2/3$; this is Laplace's rule of succession. The same idea, called **additive** or **Laplace smoothing**, keeps a naive Bayes text classifier from assigning zero probability to a document because one word never appeared with a class in training.

### Code: ML, MAP and the posterior predictive

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
sigma = 1.0                 # known noise standard deviation
m0, s0 = 0.0, 0.5           # prior on the mean: N(m0, s0^2)
x = rng.normal(0.8, sigma, 5)
n = len(x)

mu_ml = x.mean()
post_var = 1 / (1 / s0**2 + n / sigma**2)
post_mean = post_var * (m0 / s0**2 + x.sum() / sigma**2)   # = MAP for a Gaussian
pred_var = sigma**2 + post_var                              # predictive variance

# brute-force check on a grid: posterior is proportional to likelihood x prior
grid = np.linspace(-3, 3, 200001)
log_post = (-0.5 * ((x[:, None] - grid) ** 2).sum(0) / sigma**2
            - 0.5 * (grid - m0) ** 2 / s0**2)
w = np.exp(log_post - log_post.max())
w /= w.sum()
print(f"ML {mu_ml:.3f} | MAP/posterior mean {post_mean:.3f} "
      f"(grid {np.sum(w * grid):.3f}) | posterior sd {np.sqrt(post_var):.3f}")
print(f"predictive sd {np.sqrt(pred_var):.3f} vs plug-in sd {sigma:.3f}")
```

With five samples the ML mean is $0.577$, the posterior mean is $0.320$ (the grid integration agrees), and the posterior standard deviation is $0.333$. The predictive standard deviation, $1.054$, is $5\%$ wider than the plug-in value: small here, but it grows quickly as data get scarcer or dimension grows.

## Classifiers built from densities

**Plain version.** Fit one density per class, multiply by the class frequency, and pick the class with the largest product. How you model each density decides what the classifier can learn.

**Gaussian plug-in classifiers.** Estimate $\hat\mu_k$, $\hat\Sigma_k$ and $\hat P(c_k)$ and insert them into the Gaussian discriminant of PR 01. Separate covariances give QDA, a shared (pooled) covariance gives LDA, and the boundary geometry is exactly as derived there. For computation, use a Cholesky factorization or a linear solve rather than an explicit inverse.

**Naive Bayes.** Assume that the features are **conditionally independent given the class**:

$$
p(x\mid c_k)=\prod_{j=1}^d p(x_j\mid c_k).
$$

This needs only $d$ one-dimensional densities per class instead of one $d$-dimensional density, so it can be estimated from very little data. It does *not* assume that features are independent in the overall population; it assumes that the class explains all of their dependence. Gaussian naive Bayes gives each coordinate a univariate Gaussian per class; its boundary is quadratic when the per-class variances differ. When features are strongly correlated (or duplicated), naive Bayes counts the same evidence several times, so its posteriors become overconfident even when its decisions remain reasonable.

**Generative versus discriminative.** Ng and Jordan [15] compared naive Bayes with logistic regression, its discriminative counterpart. They showed that logistic regression has a lower (or equal) error in the limit of infinite data, but naive Bayes approaches its own, higher, asymptotic error much faster: with a number of samples logarithmic in the dimension, rather than linear. So the generative model often wins with few samples and the discriminative one with many. This is a useful mental model for the trade-off between the two routes.

## Non-parametric estimation: one formula, three estimators

**Plain version.** To estimate density at a point, look at a small region around it and count how many samples fall inside. Density is "count divided by (number of samples times region size)". You can fix the region and count, or fix the count and grow the region.

**Precise version.** The probability that a sample falls in a small region $\mathcal R$ around $x$ is $P=\int_{\mathcal R}p(x')\,dx'\approx p(x)\,V$, where $V$ is the volume of $\mathcal R$. If $k$ of the $N$ samples fall inside, $k\approx NP$, so [1, 2]

$$
\hat p(x)=\frac{k}{N\,V}.
$$

Two approximations are hidden here, and they pull in opposite directions. $P\approx p(x)V$ needs a *small* region (otherwise $\hat p$ is a blurred average of $p$: **bias**). $k\approx NP$ needs *many* samples inside (otherwise the count is noisy: **variance**). Duda, Hart and Stork [1] show that the estimate converges to $p(x)$ as $N\to\infty$ if the region shrinks ($V_N\to0$) while the count grows ($k_N\to\infty$) more slowly than $N$ ($k_N/N\to0$). There are two ways to satisfy this:

- **fix $V$ and count $k$**: histograms and Parzen windows (kernel density estimation);
- **fix $k$ and measure $V$**: $k$-nearest-neighbor density estimation.

### Histograms

Divide the axis into bins of width $w$ and set $\hat p(x)=n_i/(Nw)$ for $x$ in bin $i$ with count $n_i$. Histograms are simple and fast, but have three weaknesses visible in Figure 2. The estimate is discontinuous at bin edges. It depends on the **origin** of the grid as well as the width: shifting all bins by $0.3$ changes the picture. And in $d$ dimensions the number of bins grows like $(1/w)^d$, so most bins are empty long before $d$ reaches ten. Scott's analysis [8] shows that the bin width minimizing the mean integrated squared error shrinks only like $N^{-1/3}$, so more data buy finer bins slowly.

### Parzen windows and kernel density estimation

The **Parzen window** or **kernel density estimate (KDE)**, introduced by Rosenblatt [5] and analysed by Parzen [6], centers a window on every sample instead of fixing a grid:

$$
\hat p_h(x)=\frac1{N\,h^d}\sum_{n=1}^N
K\!\left(\frac{x-x_n}{h}\right).
$$

Here $K$ is the **kernel**, a function with $K(u)\ge0$ and $\int K(u)\,du=1$ (so $\hat p_h$ is a valid density), and $h>0$ is the **bandwidth**, which plays the role of the bin width. With a box kernel ($K=1$ inside the unit cube) the formula is literally "count the samples within a cube of side $h$ around $x$", the fixed-$V$ estimator above. A Gaussian kernel $K(u)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}e^{-\|u\|^2/2}$ replaces the hard count with a smooth weight, giving a smooth estimate (Figure 3, left). In practice the shape of the kernel matters much less than the bandwidth.

**Bias and variance.** For a smooth density in one dimension, the bias of a KDE grows like $h^2$ (times the curvature $p''(x)$) and the variance like $1/(Nh)$. Minimizing their sum gives an optimal bandwidth proportional to $N^{-1/5}$ and a mean integrated squared error that falls like $N^{-4/5}$, slower than the $N^{-1}$ of a correctly specified parametric model [7, 8]. In $d$ dimensions the optimal bandwidth scales like $N^{-1/(d+4)}$ and the error like $N^{-4/(d+4)}$. The slowdown with $d$ is one face of the curse of dimensionality.

**Choosing the bandwidth.** This is the important practical decision. Common options:

- **Rules of thumb.** Silverman's rule $h=0.9\min(\hat\sigma,\ \mathrm{IQR}/1.34)\,N^{-1/5}$ [7], where $\hat\sigma$ is the sample standard deviation and IQR the interquartile range, is optimal if the data are Gaussian. For multimodal data it oversmooths.
- **Cross-validation.** Choose $h$ to maximize the log-likelihood of held-out samples (likelihood cross-validation), or to minimize an unbiased estimate of the integrated squared error (least-squares cross-validation, analysed by Scott and Terrell [9]).
- **Plug-in methods.** Sheather and Jones [10] estimate the unknown curvature term in the optimal-bandwidth formula from the data and plug it in; their selector is a widely used reliable default in one dimension.

Whatever rule you use, choose $h$ without looking at the final test data. Figure 4 shows why the choice matters: on a bimodal sample, $h=0.08$ produces spurious bumps, $h=1.2$ merges the two modes into one, and the validation log-likelihood correctly prefers $h=0.2$.

### Code: bandwidth selection and a Beta posterior

This example uses scikit-learn's `KernelDensity` [26] on a synthetic bimodal distribution, separate validation samples, Silverman's rule for comparison, and the closed-form Beta posterior from the previous section.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta
from sklearn.neighbors import KernelDensity

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
def sample(n):
    means = np.where(rng.random(n) < 0.6, -1.5, 1.5)
    return (means + rng.normal(0, 0.4, n)).reshape(-1, 1)
train, validation = sample(180), sample(100)
bandwidths = [0.08, 0.2, 0.5, 1.2]
scores = []
for h in bandwidths:
    model = KernelDensity(bandwidth=h).fit(train)
    scores.append(model.score(validation) / len(validation))
best = bandwidths[int(np.argmax(scores))]

# Silverman's rule of thumb for comparison
x = train.ravel()
iqr = np.subtract(*np.percentile(x, [75, 25]))
h_silverman = 0.9 * min(x.std(ddof=1), iqr / 1.34) * len(x) ** (-1 / 5)

posterior = beta(2, 1)  # uniform prior + one success
print("validation mean log densities:",
      {h: round(float(s), 3) for h, s in zip(bandwidths, scores)})
print("selected bandwidth:", best, "| Silverman's rule:", round(h_silverman, 3))
print("posterior mean:", posterior.mean())
print("95% credible interval:", posterior.ppf([0.025, 0.975]))
assert np.isclose(posterior.mean(), 2 / 3)
```

Validation selects $h=0.2$, while Silverman's rule gives $h\approx0.475$. The rule assumes a single Gaussian; the spread of this sample comes mostly from the gap *between* the two modes, so the rule oversmooths. The Beta posterior after one success under a uniform prior has mean $2/3$ and a $95\%$ credible interval of roughly $[0.16, 0.99]$: one observation tells us very little.

A credible interval describes uncertainty under the assumed prior and likelihood; it is not automatically a calibrated frequentist coverage guarantee. Likewise, a good density score does not by itself guarantee a good classification boundary: some of the modelled variation may be irrelevant to the labels.

### $k$-nearest-neighbor density estimation

Fix $k$ instead of $V$: around $x$, grow a ball until it contains exactly $k$ samples, and let $V_k(x)$ be its volume. Then [11]

$$
\hat p_k(x)=\frac{k}{N\,V_k(x)} .
$$

Loftsgaarden and Quesenberry [11] showed that this estimate is consistent when $k\to\infty$ and $k/N\to0$. Its bandwidth adapts automatically: narrow where data are dense, wide where they are sparse. But Figure 3 (right) shows its flaws. For small $k$ it is very spiky, because $V_k$ is tiny near clusters of samples. In the tails, $V_k$ grows only linearly with the distance in 1-D, so $\hat p_k$ decays like $1/|x|$, and the estimate **does not integrate to one** (its integral diverges). It is therefore a poor *density* estimate, yet, as the next section shows, an excellent basis for *classification*.

## The $k$-nearest-neighbor classifier

**Plain version.** To classify a new point, find the $k$ training points closest to it and take a majority vote. No model is trained; the training set *is* the model.

**Precise version.** Draw a ball around $x$ that contains $k$ samples in total, of which $k_c$ belong to class $c$. Applying the $k/(NV)$ estimate separately to each class, with $N_c$ samples in class $c$, gives

$$
\hat p(x\mid c)=\frac{k_c}{N_c V},\qquad
\hat P(c)=\frac{N_c}{N},\qquad
\hat p(x)=\frac{k}{NV}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\hat P(c\mid x)=\frac{\hat p(x\mid c)\hat P(c)}{\hat p(x)}=\frac{k_c}{k}.
$$

The volume cancels, and the posterior estimate is simply the fraction of neighbors from each class. The Bayes rule then becomes the **majority vote**. With $k=1$ it is the **nearest-neighbor rule**.

**How good is it?** Cover and Hart [12] proved that, as $N\to\infty$, the error $P_{1\mathrm{NN}}$ of the 1-NN rule satisfies

$$
P^*\le P_{1\mathrm{NN}}\le P^*\Bigl(2-\frac{K}{K-1}P^*\Bigr)\le 2P^*,
$$

where $P^*$ is the Bayes error and $K$ the number of classes. With unlimited data, half of the information needed for optimal classification is contained in the single nearest neighbor. As $k$ grows (with $k/N\to0$), the $k$-NN error approaches $P^*$ itself. This is an asymptotic statement; with finite data and many dimensions, things are much less rosy.

**Choosing $k$.** Small $k$ gives low bias but high variance: the 1-NN boundary in Figure 5 wraps around individual noisy points. Large $k$ smooths the boundary but eventually ignores real structure. Choose $k$ by cross-validation. Two practical details: use an odd $k$ for two classes to avoid ties, and **standardize the features** first, because the Euclidean distance is dominated by whichever feature has the largest numeric range. In PR 04, [kernels and nearest neighbors](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/kernels-nonlinear-classifiers/) are compared with other nonlinear classifiers, and learned distance metrics are discussed there and in later chapters.

### Code: k-NN versus Gaussian classifiers

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, train_test_split
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis

X, y = make_moons(1000, noise=0.3, random_state=0)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.5, random_state=0)

search = GridSearchCV(KNeighborsClassifier(),
                      {"n_neighbors": [1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 31, 61, 101]}, cv=5)
search.fit(Xtr, ytr)                     # k chosen on training folds only
print("cross-validated k:", search.best_params_["n_neighbors"])
models = {"1-NN": KNeighborsClassifier(1),
          f"{search.best_params_['n_neighbors']}-NN": search.best_estimator_,
          "Gaussian naive Bayes": GaussianNB(),
          "QDA": QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis()}
for name, m in models.items():
    m.fit(Xtr, ytr)
    print(f"{name:22s} test error {1 - m.score(Xte, yte):.3f}")
```

Cross-validation picks $k=31$, with a test error of $10.6\%$, against $12.4\%$ for 1-NN. The Gaussian models, whose single-ellipse class shapes cannot follow the moons, are worse ($17.4\%$ for naive Bayes, $17.6\%$ for QDA). On this 2-D problem, the non-parametric method wins because the parametric form is wrong. The next section shows why the advantage shrinks as dimension grows.

## The curse of dimensionality

**Plain version.** Local methods need neighbors that are close. In high dimensions, there is no such thing as close: to collect even a small fraction of the data you must reach across most of the space, and the nearest and farthest points end up at almost the same distance.

**Precise version.** The phrase "curse of dimensionality" covers several related effects; Bishop [2, Section 1.4] gives geometric illustrations. Three are easy to check (Figure 6):

1. **Neighborhoods must be huge.** For data uniform in the unit cube $[0,1]^d$, a sub-cube that contains a fraction $r$ of the data has edge length $r^{1/d}$. To capture $10\%$ of the data, the edge is $0.32$ in 2-D, $0.79$ in 10-D and $0.98$ in 100-D. A "local" neighborhood spans almost the full range of every feature, so the estimate is no longer local.
2. **Volume is in the corners and the shell.** The volume of the unit ball divided by the volume of its enclosing cube $[-1,1]^d$ tends to zero rapidly, and for a ball almost all volume lies in a thin shell near its surface. Samples from high-dimensional Gaussians sit at nearly the same distance from the mean, not near it.
3. **Distances concentrate.** Beyer et al. [13] proved that, under broad conditions on the data distribution, the ratio of the farthest to the nearest neighbor distance tends to one as $d$ grows, so "nearest neighbor" stops being meaningful. In the simulation below, the ratio of nearest to farthest distance rises from $0.02$ in 2-D to about $0.7$ in 100-D.

### Code: distance concentration

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for d in [1, 2, 10, 100, 1000]:
    pts = rng.random((1000, d))          # 1000 uniform points in [0, 1]^d
    q = rng.random((50, d))              # 50 query points
    dist = np.sqrt(((q[:, None, :] - pts[None]) ** 2).sum(-1))
    ratio = (dist.min(1) / dist.max(1)).mean()
    edge = 0.1 ** (1 / d)                # sub-cube edge holding 10% of the volume
    print(f"d={d:5d}: nearest/farthest = {ratio:.3f}, "
          f"edge for 10% of data = {edge:.3f}")
```

The nearest/farthest ratio is $0.017$ in 2-D, $0.27$ in 10-D, $0.70$ in 100-D and $0.89$ in 1000-D.

**Why does anything work in high dimensions, then?** Real data are not uniform. Images, sounds and text lie close to low-dimensional structures (manifolds, clusters) inside their huge ambient spaces, and their informative directions are far fewer than their raw coordinates. Practical defences are therefore: reduce or learn the representation first ([PR 05: features and dimensionality](/en/learn/pattern-recognition/features-dimensionality/)), constrain the model (shared or diagonal covariances, naive Bayes), and regularize. Nearest-neighbor methods work very well today precisely *because* they are applied to learned embeddings rather than raw pixels, as the Modern view explains.

## Modern view

Density estimation has split into two streams since the textbooks were written: classical statistics refined KDE and covariance estimation, while deep learning built density models that scale to images, and nearest-neighbor search became infrastructure.

**Kernel density estimation, revisited.** Chen's tutorial [16] is a gentle modern introduction: convergence rates, bandwidth selection, confidence bands and how to handle their bias, and recent work on estimating geometric and topological features of a density (modes, level sets, ridges) with KDE. Wang and Scott [17] review non-parametric density estimators designed for high-dimensional data, including applications such as modal clustering, and list open research directions. Scott's monograph [8] and Silverman's classic [7] remain the references for the theory.

**High-dimensional covariance.** The singular-covariance problem of this chapter is a whole research area. Fan, Liao and Liu [18] review estimators of large covariance and precision (inverse covariance) matrices, focusing on rank-based methods and factor-model-based methods; Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage [14] is still the default regularizer in libraries.

**Deep density models.** Normalizing flows build a flexible density by passing a simple base distribution through a chain of invertible, differentiable maps; the change-of-variables formula then gives an exact log-likelihood. Papamakarios et al. [19] and Kobyzev, Prince and Brubaker [20] are the two standard reviews: the first emphasizes design principles, expressive power and computational trade-offs, the second the construction of flows and open questions. Autoregressive models, VAEs and diffusion models are other ways to learn high-dimensional densities; see [ML 07: generative models](/en/learn/ml/generative-models/) and [ML 08: diffusion and flow matching](/en/learn/ml/diffusion-flow-matching/). Murphy's two-volume text [4] covers both the classical estimators of this chapter and these modern models in one notation.

**Nearest neighbors at scale.** $k$-NN is now everywhere, but on learned embeddings instead of raw features. Johnson, Douze and Jégou [21] showed how to run billion-scale similarity search on GPUs, and the Faiss library paper [22] describes the trade-off space of vector search (accuracy, speed, memory) and the indexing methods behind modern vector databases. Papernot and McDaniel's **deep $k$-NN** [23] compares a test input with its training neighbors in every layer's representation, using label disagreement among neighbors as a confidence and robustness signal. For out-of-distribution detection, Sun et al. [24] use the plain $k$-th nearest-neighbor distance in feature space, without any distributional assumption, and report large improvements over a parametric Mahalanobis-distance baseline. That parametric baseline, from Lee et al. [25], fits class-conditional Gaussians with shared covariance to network features, which is LDA from PR 01. The parametric versus non-parametric choice of this chapter is being replayed on top of deep features.

**Open problems.** Reliable density estimation in high dimension remains hard. Nalisnick et al. [27] showed that flow-based models, VAEs and PixelCNNs trained on one image dataset can assign *higher* likelihood to a different dataset (for example, a model trained on a dataset of everyday objects scores a dataset of house-number photos higher), so likelihood alone is not a safe novelty score. Bandwidth or $k$ selection for embeddings, and calibrated uncertainty for non-parametric estimates, are active questions.

## Key takeaways

- Generative classifiers need $\hat p(x\mid c_k)$ and $\hat P(c_k)$; the estimator's assumptions decide the boundary's shape.
- ML estimates for a Gaussian are the sample mean and the divisor-$N$ covariance; with $N\le d$ samples the covariance is singular, and shrinkage (e.g. Ledoit–Wolf) or shared/diagonal structure is needed.
- MAP keeps the posterior mode; full Bayesian prediction integrates over parameters and is wider than plug-in prediction when data are scarce. Conjugate priors give closed forms: precisions add for a Gaussian mean, counts add for a Beta–Bernoulli rate.
- Non-parametric estimates all follow from $\hat p=k/(NV)$: histograms and Parzen/KDE fix $V$, $k$-NN fixes $k$. The bandwidth or $k$ matters more than the kernel; choose it by cross-validation or a justified rule, never on test data.
- The $k$-NN classifier estimates $P(c\mid x)\approx k_c/k$; asymptotically 1-NN is within a factor two of the Bayes error.
- In high dimensions neighborhoods stop being local and distances concentrate; local methods work in practice only on low-dimensional or learned representations.

## Exercises

1. **Counting parameters.** Count the mean and covariance parameters of one 64-dimensional Gaussian. Explain why fitting it to 30 samples fails, and give two ways to fix it.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

$64$ means plus $64\cdot65/2=2{,}080$ covariance entries. With 30 samples, $\hat\Sigma$ has rank at most 29, so it is singular. Fixes: shrinkage towards $\tau I$ (Ledoit–Wolf), a diagonal covariance (naive Bayes), a covariance shared across classes, or dimensionality reduction first.

</details>

2. **Beta–Bernoulli update.** Start with $\operatorname{Beta}(2,3)$, observe three successes and two failures, and compute the posterior, the MAP estimate and the posterior predictive probability of success.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(5,5)$. MAP (mode) $=(5-1)/(5+5-2)=0.5$. Predictive $=5/10=0.5$. Compare with ML, $3/5=0.6$.

</details>

3. **Precision-weighted means.** For the Gaussian mean with prior $\mathcal N(0,1)$ and noise variance $\sigma^2=4$, after how many samples does the data weight equal the prior weight? What is the posterior standard deviation then?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Prior precision $1$; each sample adds $1/4$. Equal weight at $N=4$. Posterior precision $2$, so $s_N=1/\sqrt2\approx0.71$.

</details>

4. **Duplicated features.** Duplicate one feature in Gaussian naive Bayes. Predict what happens to accuracy and to the confidence of the posteriors, then check with scikit-learn on `load_breast_cancer`.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

The extra coordinate adds no new information, but the factorized likelihood counts it twice, squaring its likelihood ratio. Decisions may change a little; posteriors move closer to 0 or 1 (more overconfident). Compare `predict_proba` histograms and the log-loss before and after.

</details>

5. **k-NN density is not a density.** Show that in 1-D the $k$-NN estimate $\hat p_k(x)$ decays like $1/|x|$ far from the data, and conclude that $\int\hat p_k(x)\,dx$ diverges. Why does this not hurt the $k$-NN classifier?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Far from all samples, the distance to the $k$-th neighbor is about $|x|$, so $V_k\approx2|x|$ and $\hat p_k\approx k/(2N|x|)$, whose integral is logarithmically divergent. The classifier only uses ratios $k_c/k$ at the same point, where the common volume cancels.

</details>

6. **Bandwidth rules.** Generate 500 samples from a mixture of $\mathcal N(-2,0.5^2)$ and $\mathcal N(2,0.5^2)$. Compare Silverman's rule, likelihood cross-validation (`GridSearchCV` over `KernelDensity`) and, if you have `statsmodels`, a plug-in selector. Which oversmooths, and why?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Silverman's rule uses the overall spread (about $2.1$ here), which is dominated by the gap between the modes, so it gives a bandwidth several times too large for each mode. Cross-validated likelihood picks something close to the within-mode optimum.

</details>
