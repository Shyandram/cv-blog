## The big picture

An optimization model can fit a curve to observations, estimate a distribution or decide which observations to collect next. These tasks share a common pattern: choose a representation, state how disagreement is measured, and specify which choices are allowed.

Boyd and Vandenberghe's Chapters 6–7 [1] connect approximation and statistical estimation. Read them together: the same squared residual can mean a geometric approximation error, a likelihood under a noise model, or one component of a constrained design objective. Those interpretations impose different assumptions even when the numerical formula is identical.

A successful solver answers the stated optimization problem. It does not prove that the measurement assumptions are true, that a fitted curve predicts new data, or that a sensor allocation is physically achievable. This chapter keeps those questions visible alongside the mathematics.

The examples use synthetic observations and tiny measurement models. They show how the pieces work without hiding downloads, pretrained systems or application-specific assumptions inside the code.

## Residual norms express priorities

**Plain version.** A residual is the difference between a prediction and an observation. Choosing a norm determines which patterns of error are expensive.

For a linear model,

$$
r=Ax-b,\qquad \min_x\|Ax-b\|.
$$

$A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ is a fixed design matrix, $b\in\mathbb R^m$ the observations, $x\in\mathbb R^n$ the parameters and $r\in\mathbb R^m$ the residual vector. The chosen norm acts on residuals, not directly on parameters.

The Euclidean norm or its square emphasizes large residuals through their squared contribution. The absolute-value norm adds residual magnitudes. The maximum norm minimizes the largest absolute residual and can be appropriate when every measured error must remain below a uniform tolerance [1, Section 6.1].

$$
\|r\|_2^2=\sum_i r_i^2,\qquad
\|r\|_1=\sum_i|r_i|,\qquad
\|r\|_\infty=\max_i|r_i|.
$$

$r_i$ is residual coordinate $i$. All three objectives are convex when $r$ is affine in $x$. Squaring a nonnegative Euclidean norm preserves its minimizers, but changes objective values, derivatives and the tradeoff with any additional penalty.

For absolute residuals, introduce $t_i\geq r_i$ and $t_i\geq-r_i$, then minimize $\sum_i t_i$. For a uniform tolerance introduce one scalar $t$ and impose $-t\leq r_i\leq t$. These are LP representations when there are only affine additional restrictions.

### Units and weighted errors

Measurements with different units cannot be added sensibly without a stated scaling convention. A residual of one pixel and a residual of one second are not automatically comparable.

A weighted least-squares objective can be written

$$
(Ax-b)^\top W(Ax-b),\qquad W\succeq0.
$$

$W$ is a fixed positive-semidefinite weighting matrix. If $W$ is the inverse of a known positive-definite noise covariance, the objective has a statistical interpretation. If weights encode priorities, explain that interpretation instead.

Off-diagonal entries can describe correlated measurement errors. Replacing a full covariance by diagonal weights is a modeling approximation, not just an implementation detail. Whitening with a matrix factor transforms the residual before fitting and should preserve the intended quadratic form.

A large residual is not automatically an outlier. It could reveal a wrong model, a poorly scaled feature, genuine rare behavior or a corrupted observation. Inspect the generating process before deciding which errors should lose influence.

## Least-norm solutions and regularization

**Plain version.** Matching the observations may leave many parameter choices. A norm on parameters chooses among them or trades fit against complexity.

A least-norm problem has the form

$$
\min_x\|x\|\quad\text{subject to}\quad Ax=b.
$$

$x$ is the parameter vector, and $A,b$ are fixed. This differs from a residual-norm problem: here exact consistency is required, and the objective selects a small parameter vector [1, Section 6.2].

If the equality is inconsistent, the problem is infeasible. Replacing it with a soft residual term changes the question. In an underdetermined consistent system, the Euclidean least-norm solution can be expressed through a suitable pseudoinverse, but numerical rank and singular values still matter.

Regularized approximation instead balances two goals:

$$
\min_x\frac12\|Ax-b\|_2^2+\lambda R(x),\qquad \lambda\geq0.
$$

$R$ is a convex penalty and $\lambda$ its nonnegative weight. The factor one-half simplifies derivatives. Changing that factor while keeping $\lambda$ fixed changes the tradeoff.

For $R(x)=\frac12\|x\|_2^2$, the objective is a ridge or quadratic regularization model [1, Section 6.3]. Positive $\lambda$ makes the Hessian $A^\top A+\lambda I$ positive definite. For $R(x)=\|x\|_1$, the penalty encourages exact zero coordinates through its nonsmooth geometry.

An intercept is often treated separately because penalizing it has a different meaning from penalizing feature effects. Feature units also change a parameter norm. Standardizing columns or using an explicitly scaled penalty makes this choice visible.

### Constraints and penalties are related but different

The constrained model $\min\|Ax-b\|_2^2$ subject to $R(x)\leq\rho$ directly specifies an allowable complexity budget. Its Lagrange multiplier can connect it to a penalty formulation under appropriate conditions.

That connection does not mean a particular numerical $\lambda$ corresponds to an obvious $\rho$ in advance. Nonunique solutions and inactive restrictions can complicate the correspondence. Choose the form that expresses the actual requirement.

Regularization can improve conditioning, but that numerical advantage is distinct from predictive improvement. A well-conditioned biased model may predict poorly. Select tuning parameters with a valid evaluation split when prediction is the goal, and keep the fitting objective separate from the final evaluation metric.

## Robust losses and robust measurement models

**Plain version.** “Robust” can mean reducing the influence of unusually large residuals, or protecting against uncertainty in the measurement operator. These are different models.

One convex residual loss is the Huber function:

$$
\phi_\delta(r)=
\begin{cases}
\frac12r^2,&|r|\leq\delta,\\
\delta|r|-\frac12\delta^2,&|r|>\delta.
\end{cases}
$$

$r$ is a scalar residual and $\delta>0$ the transition scale. The derivative equals $r$ near zero and saturates at magnitude $\delta$ outside. Summing this loss over affine residuals gives a convex problem [1, Section 6.1].

The transition has the units of the residual. Multiplying all observations by a scale while leaving $\delta$ unchanged alters which points receive reduced influence. The loss is not immune to every corruption pattern; high-leverage errors in the design matrix require separate attention.

### Code: fit a line with two losses

The data and shift are generated locally. The smooth optimization call follows the official SciPy interface [2]. We compare solutions and verify the robust objective's gradient instead of asserting that one estimator always wins.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

rng = np.random.default_rng(15)
t = np.linspace(-1, 1, 40)
A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(t), t])
b = 0.4 + 1.5*t + rng.normal(0, 0.08, len(t))
b[[5, 12, 30]] += 2.5
least_squares = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)[0]
delta = 0.2

def huber_objective(x):
    r = A @ x - b
    loss = np.where(abs(r) <= delta, 0.5*r*r,
                    delta*abs(r) - 0.5*delta**2)
    return loss.sum(), A.T @ np.clip(r, -delta, delta)

fit = minimize(huber_objective, least_squares, jac=True,
               method="BFGS", options={"gtol": 1e-9})
assert np.linalg.norm(huber_objective(fit.x)[1]) < 1e-6
print("squared-loss coefficients:", np.round(least_squares, 4))
print("Huber-loss coefficients:", np.round(fit.x, 4))
print("robust objective:", round(fit.fun, 6))
```

### Uncertain operators

Suppose the matrix itself may vary in a specified set $\mathcal A$. A worst-case approximation model is

$$
\min_x\sup_{A\in\mathcal A}\|Ax-b\|.
$$

$x$ is the decision, $b$ the fixed target, and $\mathcal A$ the allowed measurement matrices. Each fixed-matrix norm objective is convex in $x$, so their pointwise supremum is convex [1, Section 6.4].

Convexity does not guarantee that evaluating this supremum is easy. A finite uncertainty set can yield finitely many epigraph restrictions. Structured ellipsoidal uncertainty can permit conic reformulations. A vaguely described “small perturbation” is not enough to select the correct counterpart.

An expected squared-error model is different. If $A=\bar A+U$, with $\mathbb E[U]=0$, then

$$
\mathbb E\|Ax-b\|_2^2
=\|\bar Ax-b\|_2^2+x^\top P x,\qquad
P=\mathbb E[U^\top U].
$$

$\bar A$ is the mean operator, $U$ its random variation and $P$ a positive-semidefinite second-moment matrix. The cross term vanishes under the zero-mean assumption. Here regularization follows from a specified uncertainty model, rather than an arbitrary complexity preference.

Worst-case and expected protection answer different questions. An uncertainty set is not a probability distribution. A covariance model does not promise control of every possible realization.

## Function fitting with shape constraints

**Plain version.** We can ask a fitted function to be increasing or convex while keeping the optimization convex in its coefficients.

For fixed basis functions $\psi_j(t)$,

$$
f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^n x_j\psi_j(t).
$$

$t$ is the input, $\psi_j$ are known basis functions, and $x_j$ are fitted coefficients. Evaluating at fixed sample inputs makes the predictions affine in $x$, even when the basis functions are nonlinear in $t$.

This distinction matters: convexity in the optimization variables is not the same as convexity in the function's input. A polynomial regression may be a convex coefficient-fitting problem while the fitted polynomial is neither increasing nor convex.

At ordered knots $t_1<\cdots<t_m$, let $z_i=f(t_i)$ be fitted values. Monotonicity can be imposed by $z_{i+1}\geq z_i$. Convexity of the piecewise-linear interpolant requires

$$
\frac{z_{i+1}-z_i}{t_{i+1}-t_i}
\leq
\frac{z_{i+2}-z_{i+1}}{t_{i+2}-t_{i+1}}.
$$

$z_i$ are variables and the knot spacings are fixed positive numbers. These slope-order inequalities are linear in fitted values. Unlike sampling a derivative of an arbitrary flexible function, they guarantee the shape of this particular interpolant throughout its knot interval.

### Code: monotone absolute-error fitting as an LP

We fit five ordered measurements. The auxiliary variables represent absolute residual bounds. SciPy's LP bounds are explicitly specified [3], including unrestricted fitted values.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

observed = np.array([0., 2., 1., 3., 4.])
n = len(observed)
I = np.eye(n)
order = np.zeros((n-1, n))
for i in range(n-1):
    order[i, i], order[i, i+1] = 1, -1
G = np.vstack([np.hstack([I, -I]),
               np.hstack([-I, -I]),
               np.hstack([order, np.zeros((n-1, n))])])
h = np.r_[observed, -observed, np.zeros(n-1)]
fit = linprog(np.r_[np.zeros(n), np.ones(n)], A_ub=G, b_ub=h,
              bounds=[(None, None)]*n + [(0, None)]*n,
              method="highs")
assert fit.success
z = fit.x[:n]
assert np.all(np.diff(z) >= -1e-9)
assert np.isclose(abs(z-observed).sum(), fit.fun)
print("monotone fitted values:", z)
print("total absolute residual:", fit.fun)
```

Absolute-error solutions may be nonunique. The code verifies the property and objective rather than requiring one particular plateau value. Adding a strictly convex secondary objective would select one solution, but would be a different stated problem.

Constraints outside the observed interval require care. Piecewise-linear interpolation supplies a clear in-range function; extrapolation is an additional rule. Shape restrictions can improve plausibility while introducing bias if the underlying process violates them.

## Likelihood and convex parameterizations

**Plain version.** A likelihood model can generate a convex objective, but the chosen parameterization and domain determine whether that is true.

For independent observations $y_i$ under a distribution with natural parameter $\theta$,

$$
p(y\mid\theta)=
\exp\bigl(\theta^\top T(y)-A(\theta)+c(y)\bigr).
$$

$T(y)$ is a sufficient-statistic vector, $A(\theta)$ the log-partition function and $c(y)$ a parameter-independent base term. For an exponential-family model, $A$ is convex on its natural domain.

Ignoring terms independent of $\theta$, the negative log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\theta)=N A(\theta)-\theta^\top\sum_{i=1}^N T(y_i).
$$

$N$ is the number of observations. This is convex in the natural parameter. An arbitrary nonlinear change of parameter does not necessarily preserve convexity [1, Section 7.1].

For Bernoulli observations with success probability $p\in(0,1)$ and $k$ successes out of $N$, the negative log-likelihood is $-k\log p-(N-k)\log(1-p)$. Its interior optimum is $p=k/N$ when both outcomes are observed.

If every observation is a success or every observation a failure, the optimum over the open domain may only be approached at a boundary. Allowing boundary distributions or adding a prior changes the existence question. A program that clips probabilities should record that clipping as a domain choice.

Maximum a posteriori estimation adds the negative log prior. A convex negative log prior preserves convexity in the selected coordinates. “Bayesian” alone does not guarantee a convex optimization problem, and a point estimate does not represent the entire posterior uncertainty.

### Nonparametric probabilities

For discrete outcomes, estimate a probability vector $q$ on the simplex:

$$
q_j\geq0,\qquad \sum_jq_j=1.
$$

$q_j$ is the probability of outcome $j$. Given counts $n_j$, the negative log-likelihood is $-\sum_j n_j\log q_j$, with the appropriate extended-value convention for positive counts at zero probability.

Known moments become affine restrictions, such as $\sum_jq_j a_j=\mu$ for fixed outcome features $a_j$ and known mean $\mu$. Maximizing entropy, equivalently minimizing $\sum_jq_j\log q_j$, selects a distribution subject to such constraints [1, Section 7.2].

Moment restrictions should come from the same population and units as the data. Inconsistent empirical constraints can make the simplex model infeasible. Softening them adds a modeling decision about acceptable uncertainty, rather than repairing an algebraic nuisance.

## Detector design as a linear program

**Plain version.** Choosing decisions after observing discrete evidence can be optimized directly. Allowing randomized decisions makes the feasible set convex.

Suppose $p_{kj}$ is the known probability of observation $k$ under hypothesis $j$. Let $T_{ik}$ be the probability of deciding hypothesis $i$ after seeing $k$.

$$
T_{ik}\geq0,\qquad \sum_iT_{ik}=1,\qquad
D_{ij}=\sum_kT_{ik}p_{kj}.
$$

$D_{ij}$ is the probability of deciding $i$ when $j$ is true. $T$ is the decision variable; the conditional probabilities $p_{kj}$ are fixed. Each entry of $D$ is linear in $T$.

Weighted error costs, lower bounds on correct detection and upper bounds on false decisions can therefore be expressed as linear objectives and restrictions [1, Section 7.3]. A prior distribution on hypotheses can weight overall expected error, but should not be silently substituted for a conditional-error guarantee.

For binary hypotheses, likelihood-ratio threshold rules emerge from suitable optimal detector formulations. A boundary observation may need randomization to meet an exact error budget. Deterministic rules are a restricted subset; forcing them introduces discrete choices.

An optimized detector is only as valid as its conditional distributions. Distribution shift, estimated likelihoods and calibration uncertainty belong in the assessment. Numerical optimality under a specified probability table does not validate that table.

## Moment and exponential risk bounds

**Plain version.** Sometimes we know moments but not the whole distribution. Bounds can control a tail event conservatively, with assumptions stated explicitly.

For a scalar random variable $Y$ with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, Chebyshev's inequality gives

$$
\Pr(|Y-\mu|\geq a)\leq\frac{\sigma^2}{a^2},\qquad a>0.
$$

$a$ is a deviation threshold. The bound uses only the first two moments and can be loose. An upper bound above one can be replaced by one; it is not an estimated event probability [1, Section 7.4].

For a threshold $b$, an exponential or Chernoff bound uses

$$
\Pr(Y\geq b)\leq
\inf_{s>0}\exp(-sb)\,\mathbb E[\exp(sY)].
$$

$s$ is a positive auxiliary parameter, and the expectation must be finite where used. Optimizing its logarithm gives $\inf_{s>0}\{\log\mathbb E[\exp(sY)]-sb\}$.

The log moment-generating function is convex in $s$ on its domain. This does not automatically make every joint design-and-risk problem convex in all its variables. Check how the random variable depends on decisions and which distribution assumptions are retained.

Tail bounds can support conservative design constraints. They do not replace checking whether moments were reliably estimated. Heavy tails, dependence and finite-sample uncertainty can make an assumed moment-generating function unsuitable.

## Choosing the next measurements

**Plain version.** Estimation quality depends on what we measure, not only on how we fit. Convex experiment design allocates continuous measurement effort before any discrete rounding.

For candidate measurement vectors $a_j\in\mathbb R^n$, independent unit-variance noise and normalized weights $w_j$, define

$$
M(w)=\sum_jw_ja_ja_j^\top,\qquad
w_j\geq0,\quad\sum_jw_j=1.
$$

$M$ is the information matrix per normalized measurement effort. The covariance of a corresponding linear estimator scales with its inverse when the matrix is positive definite, the measurement model is correct and total effort is accounted for.

D-optimal design minimizes $-\log\det M$, which targets an uncertainty-volume criterion. A-optimal design minimizes $\operatorname{tr}(M^{-1})$, targeting total coordinate variance. E-optimal design maximizes the smallest eigenvalue of $M$, targeting the weakest measured direction [1, Section 7.5].

These are different scalar summaries. Units and coordinate changes can affect their interpretation differently. A small total variance may hide one poorly measured direction; a small uncertainty volume does not promise a bound on every coordinate.

### Code: two design criteria with exact checks

Use candidate vectors $(2,0)$ and $(0,1)$, with first weight $w$ and second weight $1-w$. The information matrix is diagonal: $M=\operatorname{diag}(4w,1-w)$. Both criteria diverge at an unobserved direction.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

def d_loss(w):
    return -np.log(4*w) - np.log(1-w)

def a_loss(w):
    return 1/(4*w) + 1/(1-w)

d = minimize_scalar(d_loss, bounds=(1e-6, 1-1e-6), method="bounded")
a = minimize_scalar(a_loss, bounds=(1e-6, 1-1e-6), method="bounded")
assert np.isclose(d.x, 0.5, atol=1e-5)
assert np.isclose(a.x, 1/3, atol=1e-5)
print("D-optimal first weight:", round(d.x, 6))
print("A-optimal first weight:", round(a.x, 6))
```

With a fixed number of experiments, actual counts are integers. Continuous weights are a relaxation or a randomized sampling policy. Rounding can destroy a budget or leave a direction unmeasured, so reassess the resulting matrix after converting weights into a schedule.

If candidate vectors do not span the parameter space, no weighting makes the information positive definite. Adding a prior matrix may regularize the design, but changes its interpretation to prior-plus-measurement information.

### A fitting and design audit

Start with the application quantity that must be accurate. Decide whether it is a prediction, parameter component, worst residual or a distributional probability. Then state the representation and units before selecting a numerical loss.

For each constraint, ask whether it is physically required, statistically estimated or introduced for numerical convenience. Those roles imply different responses to infeasibility. A physical limit may need a revised design; an empirical moment may need uncertainty; redundant algebra may need removal.

After fitting, verify domain membership, shape restrictions and solver residuals. Then assess generalization or uncertainty using an appropriate independent procedure. An optimum of a training objective and a reliable future prediction are separate claims.

For design, inspect the information spectrum, available budget, noise assumptions and schedule conversion. Report the final implementable schedule alongside the relaxed objective. This keeps the most consequential approximation visible.

### What an uncertainty ellipsoid actually says

An inverse information matrix describes a covariance only under the corresponding measurement and estimator assumptions. Turning it into a region with a stated coverage probability needs a distributional argument and a correctly chosen threshold. Drawing an ellipse from that matrix alone does not establish a confidence level.

For a positive-definite covariance $C$, an ellipsoid can be written as $(x-\hat x)^\top C^{-1}(x-\hat x)\leq c$. Here $\hat x$ is a center and $c>0$ is a threshold. The matrix specifies shape; the threshold specifies size. Coverage additionally depends on the actual distribution of estimation error.

A prior covariance, a sampling covariance and a numerical Hessian inverse need not have the same interpretation. They can coincide under particular models, but should not be exchanged without checking those models. Likewise, an estimator's optimization tolerance and its sampling uncertainty have different units and sources.

When measurements are repeated, independent equal-variance assumptions justify a simple additive information model. Correlated repeats can provide less new information than their count suggests. A design that ignores that correlation may confidently allocate effort to redundant measurements.

This distinction affects stopping precision. Solving far below the uncertainty in the input coefficients may offer little practical value, while violating a hard physical bound by a seemingly small amount may still be unacceptable. Set optimization tolerances against the relevant application scale, rather than choosing precision solely from the number of available floating-point digits.

Finally, uncertainty visualization should state whether it displays an analytical model, an estimated covariance or repeated synthetic experiments. The figures in this chapter show illustrative fits and objective curves. They do not display empirically calibrated coverage regions or claim a particular confidence probability.

## Modern view

**Plain version.** Current work combines structured convex estimation with large-scale first-order computation and learned convex components. The convexity guarantee belongs to the stated variables and domains.

Pokutta's 2024 survey [4] organizes Frank–Wolfe methods around linear minimization over a convex feasible set, convergence guarantees and useful variants. Simplex-constrained experiment design is a natural setting for this viewpoint: an extreme-point search can avoid a full projection. The objective still needs the required smoothness and positive-definite domain control.

Schaller, Bemporad and Boyd's 2025 manuscript [5] studies learning parametric convex functions for subsequent optimization. It connects fitting with disciplined curvature rules: a learned component can preserve convexity in decision variables for valid fixed parameters. Training the component is a separate problem and need not itself be convex.

These sources cover complementary questions: how to move through a structured feasible set, and how to construct functions usable inside such a set. Neither replaces uncertainty modeling, held-out evaluation or the physical feasibility of a design.

Current challenges include reliable uncertainty under model mismatch, informative measurements under resource limits, shape restrictions that remain appropriate outside the sample interval, and scalable updates when observations arrive sequentially. Distinguish a provably convex estimator from a complete validated statistical pipeline.

## Key takeaways

- Residual norms, parameter norms and evaluation metrics answer different questions.
- Regularization weights depend on objective normalization and feature units.
- Robust residual losses and uncertain-operator models protect against different errors.
- Fixed nonlinear bases can still yield convex coefficient fitting.
- Likelihood convexity depends on parameterization and domain.
- Randomized discrete-evidence detectors can be formulated as LPs.
- Experiment-design criteria differ, and continuous weights require an implementation plan.
