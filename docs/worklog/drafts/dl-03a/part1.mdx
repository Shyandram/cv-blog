## The big picture

YOLOv4 is a paper about a recipe. Its authors did not invent one big new idea; they collected dozens of published tricks for training and building detectors, tested which of them help a fast detector on one ordinary GPU, and assembled the winners into one model. That makes it the best single paper for learning how a modern one-stage detector works: almost every part of a current YOLO already appears in it, in a form simple enough to write down.

<Callout type="tip" title="Explain like I'm five">
Imagine a cook who wants the best fast lunch that any home kitchen can make. Cookbooks list hundreds of tips. Some tips change only how you shop and prepare (they cost nothing at the table); others add a small step while cooking (they cost a few seconds per plate). The cook tries the tips one by one, keeps the ones that make the lunch tastier without making it slower, and writes down the final recipe. YOLOv4 is that recipe book for a detector: the "free" tips change only training, the "special" tips add a little work at test time.
</Callout>

Why it matters: the detectors that run in cameras, robots, phones and factories are mostly one-stage detectors, and most of them descend from the YOLO line. YOLOv4 sits in the middle of that line. Read closely, it explains the parts that its predecessors introduced (grids, anchors, multi-scale heads) and the parts that its successors replaced (anchors, NMS, the coupled head). This chapter therefore introduces YOLOv4 in detail in the main text and gives every predecessor and successor its own introduction in the Paper guides.

This chapter assumes [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/). Its [Core building blocks](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#core-building-blocks) define box regression targets, IoU and its variants, anchors and label assignment, non-maximum suppression (NMS), feature pyramids and the focal loss; its sections on [one-stage detectors](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#one-stage-detectors-yolo-ssd-and-retinanet), [anchor-free detectors](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#anchor-free-detectors-points-instead-of-boxes) and [DETR](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#set-prediction-with-transformers-detr-and-its-descendants) introduce the families we build on. ## The family at a glance

**Plain version.** "YOLO" is a name used by many teams over a decade, not one model. Before reading YOLOv4, it helps to see who came before it, who came after it, and which versions have a paper at all.

### A timeline

Figure 1 places the versions by the year they first appeared in public, grouped by who released them, and marks whether each has a peer-reviewed paper. Three facts are easy to miss. First, **version numbers do not form one lineage**: after YOLOv3, its original authors stopped, and later numbers were taken by different groups. YOLOv4 came from a new team, two of whose authors later wrote YOLOv7 and YOLOv9 [@yolov7], [@yolov9]. Second, several widely used versions (YOLOv5, YOLOv8, YOLO11) were released as **software without a paper**; their documentation says so [@yolov5], [@yolov8], [@yolo11]. Third, the name is unregulated: in August 2026 a preprint titled "YOLOv14" was posted to arXiv and withdrawn by its authors about two weeks later [@yolov14], so a new number by itself says nothing about quality.

## Challenge

**Plain version.** In early 2020, accurate detectors were slow or needed many GPUs to train, and fast detectors were less accurate. YOLOv4 asks for both at once: a detector that runs in real time on an ordinary GPU *and* can be trained on one ordinary GPU, and it searches for that detector by testing published tricks systematically.

### A fast detector that one ordinary GPU can train

The paper (Bochkovskiy, Wang and Liao, arXiv, April 2020) [@yolov4] starts from a practical complaint: the most accurate networks of the time did not run in real time and needed many GPUs and large mini-batches to train. Its stated goal is a detector that is fast "in production systems", meaning fast in measured frames per second on a GPU rather than small in a theoretical operation count, and that anyone can train and test on a single 1080 Ti or 2080 Ti card [@yolov4].

Two predecessors frame the problem. [YOLOv3](#yolov3-three-scales-and-darknet-53) [@yolov3] was fast but, by 2020, clearly less accurate than the best detectors on the strict COCO metric: at 608 × 608 it reached 33.0% AP in the YOLOv4 comparison table [@yolov4]. EfficientDet [@efficientdet] was accurate and efficient in floating-point operations, but the paper's measurements show that low operation counts do not translate into high GPU frame rates (see [Backbone](#backbone-cspdarknet53)). YOLOv4 wants the accuracy of the second at the speed of the first.

### What "real-time" means and how YOLOv4 times it

A detector is real-time when the time it needs per frame is shorter than the time between frames. **Latency** is the time from receiving one frame to having its final boxes; **throughput** is how many frames are finished per second when many are processed together. For one frame,

$$
T_{\text{e2e}} = T_{\text{pre}} + T_{\text{net}} + T_{\text{post}},
$$

where $T_{\text{pre}}$ is pre-processing (decoding, resizing and normalizing the image, copying it to the accelerator), $T_{\text{net}}$ is the forward pass of the network, and $T_{\text{post}}$ is post-processing (decoding boxes, the confidence threshold, NMS). A camera at $f$ frames per second leaves $1/f$ seconds per frame: about 33.3 ms at 30 FPS. With batches of $B$ frames, throughput is $B / T_{\text{batch}}(B)$, where $T_{\text{batch}}(B)$ is the time for one batch; it grows with $B$, but every frame then waits for its whole batch, so a live camera cares about latency at **batch size 1**. Converting latency to FPS as $1000/T$ (with $T$ in milliseconds) is only meaningful at batch 1.

YOLOv4's speed tables follow this convention. Their captions state that the results are compared "with batch=1 without using tensorRT", that is, without NVIDIA's optimizing inference engine [@tensorrt], and the tables are split by GPU architecture (Maxwell, Pascal and Volta) because different papers timed their models on different cards [@yolov4]. They mark "real-time" as 30 FPS or more. The paper does not state the numeric precision or whether NMS is inside the timed interval; the YOLOX report later timed its comparisons "with FP16-precision and batch=1 on a V100 to align the settings of YOLOv4" [@yolox]. Keep these conditions in mind when you read the numbers in [Results](#results).

### Why the paper tests "freebies" and "specials"

Many published "features" claim to improve CNNs, but often only for one model, one task or small datasets. The paper therefore sorts candidate improvements into two bags and tests them [@yolov4]:

- **Bag of freebies (BoF):** methods that change only the training strategy or only increase the training cost, so inference stays exactly as fast. Data augmentation, regularization, label smoothing, box-regression losses and assignment rules belong here.
- **Bag of specials (BoS):** plug-in modules and post-processing steps that add a small inference cost but can improve accuracy noticeably. Receptive-field blocks, attention modules, feature-fusion paths, activation functions and NMS variants belong here.

The challenge, then, is not to invent one module but to find the combination that is best for a detector that must stay fast and must train on one GPU. The rest of this introduction follows the paper's own order: the architecture it selects, the freebies and specials it adds, the experiments that justify the choices, and what remains open.

## Method

**Plain version.** YOLOv4 is a YOLOv3-style detector with a stronger backbone (CSPDarknet53), a stronger neck (an SPP block plus a modified PANet), the unchanged YOLOv3 head, and a long list of training and inference tricks. This section explains each part: what it does, why the paper chose it and exactly how it works.

### The detector anatomy: input, backbone, neck and head

**Plain version.** Almost every detector has three parts. A **backbone** turns the image into feature maps at several resolutions. A **neck** mixes information between those resolutions. A **head** looks at every cell of every map and predicts boxes and classes.

The paper's Figure 2 and Section 2.1 give this vocabulary, which later papers adopted [@yolov4]:

- **Input:** an image, image patches or an image pyramid. YOLOv4 uses a single image resized to a square network resolution, typically 416, 512 or 608 pixels per side.
- **Backbone:** a network pre-trained on ImageNet [@imagenet] classification. GPU detectors use backbones such as VGG [@vgg] or ResNet [@resnet] variants; CPU detectors use mobile backbones.
- **Neck:** layers between backbone and head that collect feature maps from different stages, usually with several top-down and bottom-up paths, such as FPN [@fpn] and PAN [@pan]; "additional blocks" such as SPP [@spp] enlarge the receptive field.
- **Head:** the predictor. A **dense prediction** head (one-stage detector) predicts at every location of the feature maps, with anchors (YOLO, SSD, RetinaNet) or without (CenterNet, FCOS). A **sparse prediction** head (two-stage detector, the R-CNN family) predicts only for a set of proposals.

YOLOv4's choice, in the paper's words reduced to a list, is: backbone CSPDarknet53 [@cspnet], neck SPP [@spp] and PAN [@pan], head YOLOv3 [@yolov3]. The head is **anchor-based** and **one-stage**: at three output strides it predicts, for each cell and each of three anchors, four box numbers, one objectness score and one score per class.

For a square input of side $L$ pixels and output strides 8, 16 and 32, the three output grids have $L/8$, $L/16$ and $L/32$ cells per side, and the number of predictions per image is

$$
N_{\text{pred}} = A \left[ \left(\tfrac{L}{8}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{L}{16}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{L}{32}\right)^2 \right],
$$

where $A = 3$ is the number of anchors per cell. At $L = 608$ the grids are $76^2 + 38^2 + 19^2 = 7{,}581$ cells, so $N_{\text{pred}} = 22{,}743$; at $L = 416$ they give 10,647 predictions. Each prediction is a vector of $4 + 1 + K$ numbers for $K$ classes; with COCO's 80 classes each output layer has $3 \times 85 = 255$ channels, the value in the released configuration [@darknet]. All of these predictions must be filtered down to a few boxes by a confidence threshold and NMS, which is why post-processing cost matters for real-time use.

### Backbone: CSPDarknet53

**Plain version.** The backbone must see far enough to cover large objects and their context, have enough capacity for many objects at once, and run fast on a GPU. The paper compared three candidates and picked CSPDarknet53: YOLOv3's Darknet-53 with "cross-stage partial" connections that send only half of each stage's channels through the expensive blocks.

**Why this backbone.** Section 3.1 argues that a detector, unlike a classifier, needs a higher input resolution (for small objects), more layers (so the receptive field covers the larger input) and more parameters (to detect many objects of different sizes in one image). Table 1 of the paper compares three backbones at 512 × 512, with frame rates measured on an RTX 2070 [@yolov4]:

| Backbone | Receptive field | Parameters | BFLOPs | FPS (RTX 2070) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CSPResNeXt50 | 425 × 425 | 20.6 M | 31 | 62 |
| CSPDarknet53 | 725 × 725 | 27.6 M | 52 | 66 |
| EfficientNet-B3 | 1311 × 1311 | 12.0 M | 11 | 26 |

Two lessons sit in this small table. First, operation counts do not predict GPU speed: EfficientNet-B3 needs about a fifth of CSPDarknet53's operations but runs at less than half its frame rate. Second, the paper reports that CSPResNeXt50 is the better ImageNet classifier while CSPDarknet53 is the better COCO detector backbone, so "best classifier" and "best detector backbone" differ; the ablations in [Backbones and pre-trained weights](#backbones-and-pre-trained-weights) test this directly. CSPDarknet53 has 29 convolutional layers of size $3 \times 3$ against 16 for CSPResNeXt50, hence its larger receptive field.

**What cross-stage partial connections do.** CSPNet [@cspnet] starts from DenseNet-style stages, in which each layer receives the concatenation of all earlier outputs. Its analysis is that, in such a stage, the same gradient information is reused to update many layers, so layers learn duplicated information, while all channels pass through the expensive blocks. CSPNet splits the work. Write the base feature map of a stage as two channel groups,

$$
x_0 = [\,x_0',\; x_0''\,],
$$

where $[\cdot, \cdot]$ is concatenation along channels. Only $x_0''$ goes through the stage's $k$ blocks, producing $x_1, \dots, x_k$; their output passes through a transition layer (a $1 \times 1$ convolution) to give $x_T$; then $x_T$ is concatenated with the untouched part $x_0'$ and passed through a second transition layer:

$$
x_T = w_T * [\,x_0'', x_1, \dots, x_k\,], \qquad x_U = w_U * [\,x_0', x_T\,],
$$

where $*$ is convolution and $w_T, w_U$ are the transition weights. The CSPNet paper gives three purposes: the number of gradient paths doubles (one through the blocks, one around them), the computation in the blocks is roughly halved because only half the channels enter them, and memory traffic falls [@cspnet]. It also compares fusion orders and finds that the "fusion last" order, in which the block output is transformed before it is merged with $x_0'$, truncates the duplicated gradient flow and costs little accuracy [@cspnet]. Applied to Darknet-53, each residual stage becomes such a split–process–merge stage.

**Activation.** The backbone uses Mish (see [Activation: Mish](#activation-mish)); in the released configuration, Mish appears in the backbone and the leaky rectifier in the neck and head [@darknet].

### Neck, part 1: the SPP block

**Plain version.** Objects can be much larger than what one cell "sees". The SPP block lets each cell of the coarsest feature map look at a large neighbourhood cheaply, by max-pooling over windows of several sizes and stacking the results as extra channels.

Spatial pyramid pooling was introduced to turn feature maps of any size into a fixed-length vector for classification [@spp]. That version cannot be used inside a fully convolutional detector. YOLOv3's SPP variant, which YOLOv4 adopts, keeps the spatial size: it applies max pooling with kernel sizes $k \in \{1, 5, 9, 13\}$ and stride 1, with padding so that the output has the same height and width, and concatenates the four results along channels [@yolov4]. For an input of shape $C \times H \times W$,

$$
\text{SPP}(F) = \big[\, F,\ \text{MaxPool}_{5}(F),\ \text{MaxPool}_{9}(F),\ \text{MaxPool}_{13}(F) \,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{4C \times H \times W},
$$

where $\text{MaxPool}_k$ is $k \times k$ max pooling with stride 1 and "same" padding, and $k = 1$ is the identity. The block is placed on the stride-32 map, where $H = W = 19$ for a 608 input, so a $13 \times 13$ window covers most of the map. The paper quotes YOLOv3-608 with SPP gaining 2.7% AP50 on COCO at 0.5% extra computation, and states that SPP "significantly increases the receptive field" while causing almost no reduction in speed [@yolov4]. The released configuration contains exactly the three max-pooling layers of sizes 5, 9 and 13 [@darknet].

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import maximum_filter

def spp(x, kernels=(5, 9, 13)):
    """YOLO-style SPP. x: (C, H, W). Stride-1 max pooling with 'same' padding keeps H and W;
    the input and the pooled copies are concatenated along the channel axis."""
    pooled = [maximum_filter(x, size=(1, k, k), mode="constant", cval=-np.inf) for k in kernels]
    return np.concatenate([x] + pooled, axis=0)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(size=(512, 19, 19))          # e.g. the stride-32 map of a 608 x 608 input
print("SPP:", x.shape, "->", spp(x).shape)

one = np.zeros((1, 19, 19)); one[0, 9, 9] = 1.0   # a single strong activation
y = spp(one)
print("cells reached by one activation per group:", [int((g > 0).sum()) for g in y])
```

```text
SPP: (512, 19, 19) -> (2048, 19, 19)
cells reached by one activation per group: [1, 25, 81, 169]
```

The channel count quadruples while the grid stays $19 \times 19$, and one strong activation now influences up to $13 \times 13 = 169$ cells of the next layer. A $1 \times 1$ convolution after the block reduces the channels again.

### Neck, part 2: PANet path aggregation

**Plain version.** Deep feature maps know *what* is in the image; shallow ones know *where* edges are. A feature pyramid sends meaning downward to the fine maps; PANet adds a second path that sends precise location information back up to the coarse maps. YOLOv4 uses this two-way neck instead of YOLOv3's one-way one, and merges the paths by concatenation instead of addition.

FPN [@fpn] (explained in [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#feature-pyramids-fpn)) builds levels $P_3, P_4, P_5$ by upsampling coarse features and adding them to finer ones (a top-down path). PANet [@pan] observed that the path from low-level features to the top of the pyramid is still very long, and added a **bottom-up path augmentation**. Starting from $N_2 = P_2$ in the original paper, each new level is

$$
N_{i+1} = \text{conv}_{3 \times 3}\Big( \text{conv}_{3 \times 3}^{\,\text{stride } 2}(N_i) + P_{i+1} \Big),
$$

where $\text{conv}^{\text{stride } 2}_{3 \times 3}$ halves the resolution of $N_i$, $+$ is element-wise addition with the FPN level $P_{i+1}$ of the same resolution, and the outer $3 \times 3$ convolution produces the new map [@pan]. In YOLOv4 the pyramid runs over strides 8, 16 and 32, and the three outputs $N_3, N_4, N_5$ feed the three heads. Section 3.1 says the authors chose PANet "instead of the FPN used in YOLOv3" [@yolov4].

**YOLOv4's modification.** Figure 6 of the paper replaces the shortcut (addition) in PAN with **concatenation**: $[\,\text{down}(N_i),\ P_{i+1}\,]$ along channels, followed by convolutions [@yolov4]. Concatenation keeps both inputs separate and lets the next convolution learn how to weight them, at the price of more channels. The paper lists this among the changes made "to make the designed detector more suitable for training on single GPU", but it does not report a separate ablation of addition versus concatenation.

### Head: the YOLOv3 head and box decoding

**Plain version.** At each cell, for each of three template boxes (anchors), the head outputs four numbers that move and stretch the template, one "is there an object?" score and one score per class. YOLOv4 keeps this head and fixes one numerical problem: in YOLOv3, a box centre close to a cell border was very hard to predict.

**Decoding.** The head follows [YOLOv2](#yolov2-and-yolo9000-anchors-priors-and-joint-training) [@yolov2] and [YOLOv3](#yolov3-three-scales-and-darknet-53) [@yolov3]. For a cell whose top-left corner is at $(c_x, c_y)$ in grid units and an anchor of width $p_w$ and height $p_h$, the network outputs $(t_x, t_y, t_w, t_h)$ and the box is

$$
b_x = \sigma(t_x) + c_x, \qquad b_y = \sigma(t_y) + c_y, \qquad b_w = p_w\, e^{t_w}, \qquad b_h = p_h\, e^{t_h},
$$

where $\sigma(t) = 1/(1 + e^{-t})$ is the logistic sigmoid, $(b_x, b_y)$ is the box centre in grid units (multiply by the stride for pixels) and $(b_w, b_h)$ its size. An objectness output $t_o$ gives $\sigma(t_o)$, and each class has its own sigmoid score, so a box can carry several labels; YOLOv3 trains the class scores with binary cross-entropy [@yolov3]. Each output stride has three anchors; the released YOLOv4 configuration uses the nine anchors (in pixels) $12 \times 16$, $19 \times 36$, $40 \times 28$ at stride 8, $36 \times 75$, $76 \times 55$, $72 \times 146$ at stride 16 and $142 \times 110$, $192 \times 243$, $459 \times 401$ at stride 32 [@darknet].

**The grid-sensitivity problem.** Because $\sigma(t_x) \in (0, 1)$ never reaches 0 or 1, a centre that lies exactly on a cell border needs $t_x \to \pm\infty$, and centres near borders need very large $|t_x|$. Section 4.3 of the paper calls this "grid sensitivity": the network must output extreme values to place a centre near $c_x$ or $c_x + 1$ [@yolov4]. The fix is to multiply the sigmoid by a factor greater than 1. The paper states only that; the released code writes it as

$$
b_x = s\,\sigma(t_x) - \frac{s - 1}{2} + c_x, \qquad b_y = s\,\sigma(t_y) - \frac{s - 1}{2} + c_y,
$$

where $s \ge 1$ is the scale factor (`scale_x_y` in the configuration) and the shift $-(s - 1)/2$ keeps the range centred on the cell, so the centre can now cover $(c_x - (s-1)/2,\; c_x + 1 + (s-1)/2)$, slightly beyond the cell [@darknet]. The released configuration uses $s = 1.2$, 1.1 and 1.05 at strides 8, 16 and 32 [@darknet]. The snippet decodes one prediction and shows how much less extreme $t_x$ has to be.

```python
import numpy as np

sigmoid = lambda t: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-t))
logit = lambda p: np.log(p / (1 - p))

def decode(t, cell, anchor, stride, s=1.0):
    """One YOLOv3/YOLOv4 prediction t = (tx, ty, tw, th) -> box centre and size in pixels.
    s = 1 is the YOLOv3 formula; s > 1 is YOLOv4's grid-sensitivity fix."""
    tx, ty, tw, th = t
    bx = s * sigmoid(tx) - (s - 1) / 2 + cell[0]        # centre in grid units
    by = s * sigmoid(ty) - (s - 1) / 2 + cell[1]
    return bx * stride, by * stride, anchor[0] * np.exp(tw), anchor[1] * np.exp(th)

# cell (10, 7) of the stride-8 grid, anchor 36 x 75 pixels, raw outputs all zero
print("box (cx, cy, w, h):", np.round(decode((0, 0, 0, 0), (10, 7), (36, 75), 8), 1))

# which raw output tx puts the centre at a given position inside its cell?
for pos in (0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 1.0):
    row = []
    for s in (1.0, 1.2):
        p = (pos + (s - 1) / 2) / s                     # the sigmoid value that is needed
        row.append(f"s={s}: " + (f"{logit(p):5.2f}" if p < 1 else "  inf"))
    print(f"centre at {pos:4.2f} of the cell -> tx = " + " | ".join(row))
```

```text
box (cx, cy, w, h): [84. 60. 36. 75.]
centre at 0.50 of the cell -> tx = s=1.0:  0.00 | s=1.2:  0.00
centre at 0.90 of the cell -> tx = s=1.0:  2.20 | s=1.2:  1.61
centre at 0.99 of the cell -> tx = s=1.0:  4.60 | s=1.2:  2.29
centre at 1.00 of the cell -> tx = s=1.0:   inf | s=1.2:  2.40
```

With $s = 1.2$ a centre on the border needs $t_x = 2.40$ instead of infinity, so the targets the network must learn stay in a comfortable range. In the ablation table this is the column "S" (see [Detector bag of freebies](#detector-bag-of-freebies)).

**Training targets.** The head is trained with three terms: a box loss (CIoU in YOLOv4, see [Box loss](#box-loss-from-iou-to-ciou)), a binary cross-entropy objectness loss, and binary cross-entropy class losses on the positive predictions. In YOLOv3, a prediction whose box already overlaps a ground-truth box well but is not assigned to it is ignored in the objectness loss rather than pushed to "background"; YOLOv3 used an IoU of 0.5 for this rule [@yolov3], and the released YOLOv4 configuration sets it to 0.7 (`ignore_thresh`) [@darknet]. Which predictions count as positives is the subject of the next section.

### Label assignment: several anchors per object

**Plain version.** During training, every object must be "given" to some predictions, which are then taught to fire on it. YOLOv3 gave each object to exactly one anchor, the best-shaped one. YOLOv4 gives it to every anchor whose shape is similar enough, so each object trains several predictions.

In YOLOv3, an object is assigned to the single anchor whose shape has the highest IoU with the object's box, at the cell containing the object's centre [@yolov3]. YOLOv4's bag of freebies includes "using multiple anchors for a single ground truth": every anchor with

$$
\text{IoU}(\text{truth}, \text{anchor}) \gt \tau
$$

becomes a positive, where the IoU compares only widths and heights (both boxes placed on the same centre) and $\tau$ is the "IoU threshold" hyper-parameter [@yolov4]. The genetic search (below) selected $\tau = 0.213$, which is also the value in the released configuration (`iou_thresh`) [@darknet]. Because the nine anchors are split across three strides, one object can now be learned on two output levels. The snippet applies the rule to three object shapes.

```python
import numpy as np

# the nine anchors (width, height in pixels) of yolov4.cfg; three per output stride
anchors = np.array([[12, 16], [19, 36], [40, 28], [36, 75], [76, 55], [72, 146],
                    [142, 110], [192, 243], [459, 401]], float)
stride = np.repeat([8, 16, 32], 3)

def shape_iou(wh, anchors):
    """IoU of boxes that share a centre: only width and height matter."""
    inter = np.minimum(wh[0], anchors[:, 0]) * np.minimum(wh[1], anchors[:, 1])
    return inter / (wh[0] * wh[1] + anchors.prod(1) - inter)

for wh in [(30, 60), (100, 90), (60, 120)]:
    iou = shape_iou(np.array(wh, float), anchors)
    pos = np.flatnonzero(iou > 0.213)
    print(f"object {wh[0]:3d}x{wh[1]:<3d} | best anchor {iou.argmax()} (IoU {iou.max():.2f}) | "
          f"IoU > 0.213: anchors {pos.tolist()} on strides {sorted(set(stride[pos].tolist()))}")
```

```text
object  30x60  | best anchor 3 (IoU 0.67) | IoU > 0.213: anchors [1, 2, 3, 4] on strides [8, 16]
object 100x90  | best anchor 6 (IoU 0.58) | IoU > 0.213: anchors [3, 4, 5, 6] on strides [16, 32]
object  60x120 | best anchor 5 (IoU 0.68) | IoU > 0.213: anchors [3, 4, 5, 6] on strides [16, 32]
```

Each object now trains four predictions instead of one. More positives give more training signal, especially for objects whose shape falls between two anchors, but they also teach several predictions to fire on the same object, which makes NMS indispensable. In the ablation table this is the column "IT". Later detectors replaced fixed shape rules with assignment based on the network's own predictions ([YOLOX](#yolox-anchor-free-yolo-with-simota), [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector)) and eventually with one-to-one assignment that removes NMS ([YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments)). [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#anchors-and-label-assignment) explains the IoU-based rules of two-stage detectors and of RetinaNet for comparison.

### Activation: Mish

**Plain version.** An activation function decides how a neuron responds to its input. YOLOv3 used the leaky rectifier, which has a sharp corner at zero. Mish is a smooth curve that behaves like the identity for large inputs and lets small negative values through, which the paper found to help both the classifier and the detector.

Mish is defined as [@mish]

$$
\text{Mish}(x) = x \tanh\!\big(\text{softplus}(x)\big) = x \tanh\!\big(\ln(1 + e^{x})\big),
$$

where $\text{softplus}(x) = \ln(1 + e^x)$. For large positive $x$, $\tanh(\text{softplus}(x)) \to 1$ and $\text{Mish}(x) \approx x$; for large negative $x$, it tends to 0; in between it dips slightly below zero, so it is not monotonic. The leaky rectifier of YOLO v1 to v3 is $\phi(x) = x$ for $x \gt 0$ and $0.1x$ otherwise [@yolov1]. The paper's activation candidates were ReLU, leaky ReLU, parametric ReLU, ReLU6, SELU, Swish [@swish] and Mish; it removed parametric ReLU and SELU as hard to train and ReLU6 as designed for quantized networks, and notes that Swish and Mish are both continuously differentiable [@yolov4].

```python
import numpy as np

mish = lambda x: x * np.tanh(np.log1p(np.exp(x)))   # x * tanh(softplus(x))
leaky = lambda x: np.where(x > 0, x, 0.1 * x)         # the leaky rectifier of YOLO v1-v3

for v in (-3.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 3.0):
    print(f"x = {v:4.1f} | Mish {mish(v):7.4f} | leaky {float(leaky(v)):5.2f}")
x = np.linspace(-6, 6, 120001)
i = mish(x).argmin()
print(f"Mish minimum {mish(x[i]):.4f} at x = {x[i]:.3f}")
h = 1e-6
print(f"slope at 0: Mish {(mish(h) - mish(-h)) / (2 * h):.4f}; leaky jumps from 0.1 to 1")
```
