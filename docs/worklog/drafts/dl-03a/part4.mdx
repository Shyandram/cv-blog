### YOLO v1: detection as a single regression

*Paper:* J. Redmon, S. Divvala, R. Girshick and A. Farhadi, "You only look once: Unified, real-time object detection," CVPR 2016 [@yolov1]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the detector anatomy](#the-detector-anatomy-input-backbone-neck-and-head).

#### Challenge

In 2015 the accurate detectors were pipelines: a proposal method suggested about 2,000 candidate regions, a convolutional network classified each, and further stages refined boxes and removed duplicates. Each stage had to be tuned separately, and the systems were slow; the paper notes that R-CNN took more than 40 seconds per image at test time, and that among the GPU detectors of the time only a fast deformable-parts implementation ran at 30 FPS or more [@yolov1]. Fast R-CNN [@fastrcnn] and Faster R-CNN [@fasterrcnn] made the pipeline faster but not real-time. The challenge was a detector that is real-time by design, trained end to end on the detection objective.

#### Method

**One grid, one tensor.** The image is resized to $448 \times 448$ and divided into an $S \times S$ grid. The cell that contains an object's centre is responsible for that object. Each cell predicts $B$ boxes, each with five numbers $(x, y, w, h, \text{confidence})$, and one set of $C$ conditional class probabilities $\Pr(\text{Class}_i \mid \text{Object})$, so the whole output is a tensor of shape

$$
S \times S \times (5B + C),
$$

which for PASCAL VOC [@voc] ($S = 7$, $B = 2$, $C = 20$) is $7 \times 7 \times 30$ [@yolov1]. The box centre $(x, y)$ is an offset within the cell, between 0 and 1; $w$ and $h$ are relative to the whole image. The confidence is trained toward $\Pr(\text{Object}) \cdot \text{IoU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}}$, zero when no object is present. At test time the class-specific score of each box is

$$
\Pr(\text{Class}_i \mid \text{Object}) \cdot \Pr(\text{Object}) \cdot \text{IoU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}} = \Pr(\text{Class}_i) \cdot \text{IoU}^{\text{truth}}_{\text{pred}},
$$

which encodes both the class probability and how well the box fits [@yolov1]. The network therefore makes only $7 \times 7 \times 2 = 98$ box predictions per image, against about 2,000 proposals for a selective-search pipeline.

**Network.** 24 convolutional layers (inspired by GoogLeNet, with $1 \times 1$ reduction layers followed by $3 \times 3$ convolutions) and 2 fully connected layers; "Fast YOLO" uses 9 convolutional layers with fewer filters [@yolov1]. The first 20 convolutional layers are pre-trained on ImageNet [@imagenet] at $224 \times 224$ (about a week of training, 88% single-crop top-5 accuracy); four convolutional and two fully connected layers are added for detection and the input resolution is doubled to $448 \times 448$. All layers except the last use the leaky rectifier $\phi(x) = x$ for $x \gt 0$ and $0.1x$ otherwise; the last layer is linear [@yolov1].

**Loss.** The paper optimizes a sum of squared errors with two corrections. Most cells contain no object, so their confidence errors would overwhelm the others: the coordinate terms are weighted by $\lambda_{\text{coord}} = 5$ and the no-object confidence terms by $\lambda_{\text{noobj}} = 0.5$. Errors in large boxes should matter less than in small ones, so the network predicts $\sqrt{w}$ and $\sqrt{h}$. The loss is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L} ={}& \lambda_{\text{coord}} \sum_{i=0}^{S^2} \sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij} \Big[ (x_i - \hat{x}_i)^2 + (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \big(\sqrt{w_i} - \sqrt{\hat{w}_i}\big)^2 + \big(\sqrt{h_i} - \sqrt{\hat{h}_i}\big)^2 \Big] \\
&+ \sum_{i=0}^{S^2} \sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij} (C_i - \hat{C}_i)^2 + \lambda_{\text{noobj}} \sum_{i=0}^{S^2} \sum_{j=0}^{B} \mathbb{1}^{\text{noobj}}_{ij} (C_i - \hat{C}_i)^2 + \sum_{i=0}^{S^2} \mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{i} \sum_{c} \big(p_i(c) - \hat{p}_i(c)\big)^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $i$ indexes cells and $j$ the boxes of a cell, $\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{i}$ is 1 if an object appears in cell $i$, $\mathbb{1}^{\text{obj}}_{ij}$ is 1 if box predictor $j$ of cell $i$ is "responsible" for that object (the predictor with the highest current IoU with it), $\mathbb{1}^{\text{noobj}}_{ij}$ is its complement, hatted symbols are predictions and unhatted ones targets, $C$ is the confidence and $p_i(c)$ the class probability [@yolov1]. Because only the responsible predictor is trained on each object, the two predictors of a cell specialize in different sizes and shapes; this is the first YOLO label-assignment rule.

**Training.** About 135 epochs on VOC 2007 and 2012 train and validation data, batch 64, momentum 0.9, weight decay 0.0005; the learning rate is raised from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ over the first epochs, kept for 75 epochs, then $10^{-3}$ for 30 and $10^{-4}$ for 30. Dropout [@dropout] with rate 0.5 after the first fully connected layer, random scaling and translations of up to 20% of the image size, and random exposure and saturation changes of up to a factor 1.5 in HSV space [@yolov1]. Greedy NMS is still applied at test time; the paper reports that it adds 2–3% mAP.

#### Results

On PASCAL VOC 2007 test (trained on 2007+2012), YOLO reaches 63.4% mAP at 45 FPS and Fast YOLO 52.7% mAP at 155 FPS, on a Titan X GPU without batching; the real-time deformable-parts baselines reach 26.1% at 30 FPS and 16.0% at 100 FPS [@yolov1]. Slower detectors are more accurate: Fast R-CNN 70.0% at 0.5 FPS, Faster R-CNN with VGG-16 73.2% at 7 FPS, and a YOLO trained with a VGG-16 backbone 66.4% at 21 FPS [@yolov1]. On VOC 2012 test YOLO scores 57.9% mAP [@yolov1]. The headline is speed: more than twice the mAP of earlier real-time systems.

#### Ablations

The paper has no component-by-component ablation table; its analyses play that role. An error breakdown on VOC 2007 (following a standard diagnosis tool) shows that 19.0% of YOLO's top detections are localization errors and 4.75% background errors, against 8.6% and 13.6% for Fast R-CNN: YOLO localizes worse but mistakes background for objects about three times less often [@yolov1]. Using YOLO to rescore Fast R-CNN's detections raises Fast R-CNN from 71.8% to 75.0% mAP, which the paper attributes to these different error types rather than to simple ensembling [@yolov1]. A person-detection test on artwork shows that YOLO's accuracy drops less than that of R-CNN-style detectors when moving from photographs to paintings [@yolov1].

#### Conclusion

YOLO v1 established detection as a single regression from image to boxes and classes, trained end to end, and showed that such a detector can run at video rate with useful accuracy. Every later YOLO keeps its "look once, predict densely on a grid" structure.

#### Problems

The paper lists its own limits [@yolov1]: each cell predicts only two boxes and one class, so nearby small objects (a flock of birds) compete for one cell; boxes are learned from data with no shape prior, so unusual aspect ratios generalize poorly; coarse features after many downsampling steps limit localization; and the squared-error loss treats small and large boxes alike, despite the square-root correction. Localization was its main source of error. [YOLOv2](#yolov2-and-yolo9000-anchors-priors-and-joint-training) answered with anchors, batch normalization and a fully convolutional design; [SSD](#ssd-multi-scale-default-boxes) answered with predictions on several feature maps.

### YOLOv2 and YOLO9000: anchors, priors and joint training

*Paper:* J. Redmon and A. Farhadi, "YOLO9000: Better, faster, stronger," CVPR 2017 [@yolov2]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the YOLOv3 head and box decoding](#head-the-yolov3-head-and-box-decoding).

#### Challenge

YOLO v1 was fast but made many localization errors and had low recall compared with proposal-based methods [@yolov2]. The paper sets three goals in its title: *better* (more accurate, especially in recall and localization, without losing speed), *faster* (a cheaper backbone than VGG-16 [@vgg]) and *stronger* (detect many more categories than detection datasets label, by also learning from classification data).

#### Method

The "better" part is a sequence of changes, each measured (see Ablations):

- **Batch normalization** [@bn] on all convolutional layers, which also allows dropout to be removed.
- **High-resolution classifier:** the backbone is fine-tuned on ImageNet at $448 \times 448$ for 10 epochs before detection training, so it adapts to the higher detection resolution.
- **Convolutional with anchor boxes:** the fully connected layers are removed, the input is $416 \times 416$ so the $32\times$-downsampled output is a $13 \times 13$ grid with a single centre cell, and boxes are predicted relative to anchors, with class and objectness predicted per anchor.
- **Dimension clusters:** instead of hand-picked anchors, k-means clustering on the training boxes finds the anchor shapes, with the distance $d(\text{box}, \text{centroid}) = 1 - \text{IoU}(\text{box}, \text{centroid})$ so that large boxes do not dominate as they would with Euclidean distance; $k = 5$ is chosen as a trade-off between recall and model complexity [@yolov2]. The k-means loop is worked through in the [DLCV 03 Supplementary notes](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/notes/#anchor-shapes-by-k-means-clustering-with-an-iou-distance).
- **Direct location prediction:** region-proposal-style offsets $x = t_x w_a - x_a$ are unconstrained, so an anchor can drift anywhere and early training is unstable. YOLOv2 instead predicts the centre relative to the cell with a sigmoid, $b_x = \sigma(t_x) + c_x$, $b_y = \sigma(t_y) + c_y$, $b_w = p_w e^{t_w}$, $b_h = p_h e^{t_h}$, and the objectness $\sigma(t_o) = \Pr(\text{object}) \cdot \text{IoU}(b, \text{object})$, where $(c_x, c_y)$ is the cell offset and $(p_w, p_h)$ the prior's size [@yolov2]. This is the formula YOLOv4 inherits and then corrects for grid sensitivity.
- **Fine-grained features:** a passthrough layer brings $26 \times 26$ features to the $13 \times 13$ output by stacking adjacent features into channels, turning a $26 \times 26 \times 512$ map into $13 \times 13 \times 2048$ and concatenating it with the output features [@yolov2].
- **Multi-scale training:** every 10 batches the network picks a new input size from $\{320, 352, \dots, 608\}$ (multiples of 32), so one set of weights works at several resolutions and offers a speed–accuracy trade-off at test time [@yolov2].

The "faster" part is **Darknet-19**, a 19-layer convolutional backbone with 5 max-pooling layers, mostly $3 \times 3$ filters with $1 \times 1$ compressions, batch normalization and global average pooling; it needs 5.58 billion operations per image and reaches 72.9% top-1 and 91.2% top-5 accuracy on ImageNet, while VGG-16's convolutional layers need 30.69 billion and YOLO v1's custom network 8.52 billion [@yolov2].

The "stronger" part is **YOLO9000**. Classification labels (ImageNet) and detection labels (COCO [@coco]) are not mutually exclusive ("Norfolk terrier" is also a "dog"), so a single softmax over all labels is wrong. The paper builds **WordTree**, a hierarchy derived from WordNet, and predicts a conditional probability at every node, $\Pr(\text{child} \mid \text{parent})$, with one softmax per group of siblings; the probability of a label is the product of the conditionals along its path from the root [@yolov2]. Joint training mixes detection and classification images: for a detection image the full loss is back-propagated; for a classification image only the classification loss, at and above the label's level in the tree, using the box that predicts that class with the highest probability [@yolov2]. The combined tree has 9,418 classes [@yolov2].

#### Results

On VOC 2007 test, the same trained YOLOv2 runs at different input sizes on a GTX Titan X (the original, not the Pascal model): 69.0% mAP at 91 FPS ($288 \times 288$), 76.8% at 67 FPS ($416$) and 78.6% at 40 FPS ($544$), against 73.2% at 7 FPS for Faster R-CNN with VGG-16, 76.4% at 5 FPS for Faster R-CNN with ResNet and 76.8% at 19 FPS for SSD500 [@yolov2]. On VOC 2012 test YOLOv2-544 scores 73.4% mAP, and on COCO test-dev 21.6% AP and 44.0% AP50 [@yolov2]. YOLO9000 reaches 19.7% mAP on the ImageNet detection validation set and 16.0% mAP on the 156 of its classes that have no labelled detection data at all [@yolov2].

#### Ablations

Table 2 of the paper, "the path from YOLO to YOLOv2", adds the changes one by one and reports VOC 2007 mAP: YOLO v1 63.4; + batch normalization 65.8; + high-resolution classifier 69.5; + convolutional with anchor boxes 69.2; + the new network 69.6; + dimension priors and direct location prediction 74.4; + passthrough 75.4; + multi-scale training 76.8; + high-resolution detector 78.6 [@yolov2]. Two steps barely change mAP: switching to anchor boxes keeps mAP (69.5 → 69.2) but raises recall from 81% to 88%, giving "more room to improve", and the new network cuts computation by 33% at equal mAP [@yolov2]. Table 1 shows why clustering matters: 5 clustered priors reach an average IoU of 61.0 with the closest box, as good as 9 hand-picked anchors (60.9), and 9 clustered priors reach 67.2 [@yolov2].

#### Conclusion

YOLOv2 turned YOLO into a fully convolutional, anchor-based detector with learned anchor shapes, bounded centre offsets and multi-scale training, and showed with YOLO9000 that a detector can be trained on detection and classification data together. Its decoding formula and its dimension clusters survive, almost unchanged, in YOLOv3 and YOLOv4.

#### Problems

Its single $13 \times 13$ output, even with the passthrough layer, limits small-object detection (21.6% COCO AP is low next to later detectors), the bounded sigmoid creates the grid-sensitivity problem that YOLOv4 later fixes, and YOLO9000's accuracy on classes without detection data (16.0% mAP) is far from that on detected classes. [YOLOv3](#yolov3-three-scales-and-darknet-53) moved to three output scales and a deeper backbone.

### YOLOv3: three scales and Darknet-53

*Paper:* J. Redmon and A. Farhadi, "YOLOv3: An incremental improvement," tech. report, arXiv, 2018 [@yolov3]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the YOLOv3 head and box decoding](#head-the-yolov3-head-and-box-decoding), which YOLOv4 keeps.

#### Challenge

By 2018 YOLOv2 was fast but weaker than newer detectors such as RetinaNet [@retinanet] on COCO, particularly for small objects. YOLOv3 collects "a bunch of little design changes" from other work to improve accuracy while staying fast; the report is short and informal and was written, in its own words, so that other papers could cite the changes [@yolov3].

#### Method

- **Box prediction.** The YOLOv2 decoding is kept ($b_x = \sigma(t_x) + c_x$, $b_w = p_w e^{t_w}$, and so on), with a sum-of-squared-error loss on the raw outputs $t$ [@yolov3]. Objectness is predicted by logistic regression: its target is 1 for the anchor that overlaps a ground-truth object more than any other anchor; anchors that are not the best but overlap an object by more than 0.5 IoU are ignored; each object is assigned to exactly one anchor, and unassigned anchors receive only an objectness loss [@yolov3].
- **Class prediction.** Independent logistic classifiers with binary cross-entropy replace the softmax, so a box can carry overlapping labels (for example "woman" and "person") [@yolov3].
- **Predictions across scales.** Boxes are predicted at three scales with an FPN-like design [@fpn]: after the last backbone features, a few convolutions predict a tensor; then the features from two layers earlier are upsampled by 2 and concatenated with an earlier, finer feature map, and the process is repeated twice. With 3 boxes per scale and COCO's 80 classes, each scale outputs an $N \times N \times [3 \cdot (4 + 1 + 80)]$ tensor [@yolov3]. The nine anchors, from k-means on COCO, are divided evenly across the scales: $10 \times 13$, $16 \times 30$, $33 \times 23$, $30 \times 61$, $62 \times 45$, $59 \times 119$, $116 \times 90$, $156 \times 198$, $373 \times 326$ [@yolov3].
- **Darknet-53.** A new backbone of 53 convolutional layers, successive $3 \times 3$ and $1 \times 1$ convolutions with residual shortcuts [@resnet], and stride-2 convolutions instead of pooling [@yolov3].

#### Results

Darknet-53, tested at $256 \times 256$ on a Titan X, reaches 77.2% top-1 and 93.8% top-5 accuracy on ImageNet with 18.7 billion operations at 78 FPS, against 77.6% / 93.8% at 37 FPS for ResNet-152 and 77.1% / 93.7% at 53 FPS for ResNet-101 [@yolov3]. On COCO, YOLOv3 at $320 \times 320$ reaches 28.2% AP in 22 ms; at $608 \times 608$ it reaches 33.0% AP, 57.9% AP50, 34.4% AP75 and 18.3% / 35.4% / 41.9% AP for small, medium and large objects in 51 ms [@yolov3]. Its AP50 (57.9%) is close to RetinaNet's 57.5% at 198 ms, which the report summarizes as "similar performance but 3.8× faster"; times come from an M40 or Titan X [@yolov3].

#### Ablations

There is no ablation table, but the report lists "things we tried that didn't work" [@yolov3]: predicting $x, y$ offsets as multiples of the anchor size with a linear activation decreased stability; linear instead of logistic $x, y$ predictions dropped mAP by a couple of points; the focal loss dropped mAP by about 2 points, which the authors suspect is because separate objectness and conditional class predictions already handle the imbalance; and Faster R-CNN-style dual IoU thresholds for positives and negatives did not give good results.

#### Conclusion

YOLOv3 added multi-scale prediction, multi-label classification and a strong, fast residual backbone to YOLOv2's design. Its head, decoding and assignment rules are what YOLOv4 keeps as "the YOLOv3 head".

#### Problems

The report itself points out the main weakness: performance drops sharply as the IoU threshold rises from 0.5 to 0.95, so YOLOv3 "struggles to get the boxes perfectly aligned with the object"; it also notes comparatively worse medium- and large-object AP [@yolov3]. One anchor per object gives few positives, the sigmoid creates grid sensitivity, and the box loss is squared error on raw outputs. YOLOv4 addresses each: CIoU loss, several anchors per object, the grid-sensitivity factor and a stronger backbone and neck. This was the last YOLO by its original authors; later numbers came from other teams.

### SSD: multi-scale default boxes

*Paper:* W. Liu, D. Anguelov, D. Erhan, C. Szegedy, S. Reed, C.-Y. Fu and A. C. Berg, "SSD: Single shot multibox detector," ECCV 2016 [@ssd]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [the detector anatomy](#the-detector-anatomy-input-backbone-neck-and-head) (multi-scale dense prediction). [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#one-stage-detectors-yolo-ssd-and-retinanet) gives a short overview.

#### Challenge

YOLO v1 was fast but predicted from one coarse grid with no shape priors, so it was much less accurate than Faster R-CNN. SSD asks whether a single-shot detector can match proposal-based accuracy at real-time speed, by predicting from several feature maps of different resolutions with a set of default boxes at each location [@ssd].

#### Method

**Multi-scale feature maps.** A truncated VGG-16 [@vgg] base network (with its fully connected layers converted to convolutions) is followed by extra convolutional layers that shrink the feature map step by step. Small convolutional predictors are attached to six maps of decreasing resolution, from conv4_3 to the last extra layer, so early maps handle small objects and later maps large ones [@ssd].

**Default boxes.** At each location of a map with $m$ prediction maps in total, default boxes (anchors) of scale

$$
s_k = s_{\min} + \frac{s_{\max} - s_{\min}}{m - 1}(k - 1), \qquad k \in [1, m],
$$

are placed, where $s_{\min} = 0.2$ and $s_{\max} = 0.9$ (as fractions of the image size), with aspect ratios $a_r \in \{1, 2, 3, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3}\}$, width $s_k\sqrt{a_r}$ and height $s_k/\sqrt{a_r}$, plus one extra box of scale $\sqrt{s_k s_{k+1}}$ for ratio 1: six default boxes per location [@ssd]. For each default box the predictor outputs four offsets and $C + 1$ class scores (including background). The $300 \times 300$ model makes 8,732 predictions per class [@ssd].

**Matching.** Each ground-truth box is matched to the default box with the highest Jaccard overlap (IoU), and then to every default box with IoU above 0.5, so several overlapping default boxes become positives [@ssd].

**Loss.** With $x^p_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}$ indicating that default box $i$ is matched to ground truth $j$ of class $p$,

$$
\mathcal{L}(x, c, l, g) = \frac{1}{N}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\text{conf}}(x, c) + \alpha\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}(x, l, g)\big),
$$

where $N$ is the number of matched default boxes, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{conf}}$ is the softmax cross-entropy over class scores $c$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{loc}}$ is a smooth L1 loss between predicted offsets $l$ and ground-truth offsets $g$ (centre offsets normalized by the default box's size, log-ratios of width and height), and $\alpha = 1$ [@ssd].

**Hard negative mining and augmentation.** Most default boxes are negatives; SSD keeps only the negatives with the highest confidence loss, at most three for every positive [@ssd]. Each training image is either used whole, or a patch is sampled so that its minimum IoU with the objects is 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 or 0.9, or a random patch is sampled; patches are resized, flipped and photometrically distorted [@ssd].

#### Results

On VOC 2007 test (trained on 2007+2012), SSD300 reaches 74.3% mAP and SSD512 76.8%, against 73.2% for Faster R-CNN with VGG-16 and 63.4% for YOLO v1 [@ssd]. Speeds on a Titan X: SSD300 at 46 FPS with batch 1 and 59 FPS with batch 8; SSD512 at 19 and 22 FPS [@ssd]. On COCO test-dev, SSD300 reaches 23.2% AP and SSD512 26.8% AP [@ssd]. Note the two batch sizes: the frequently quoted 59 FPS is a batch-8 throughput.

#### Ablations

Table 2 of the paper, on SSD300 and VOC 2007: without the extensive data augmentation mAP is 65.5%; with it, but without the extra aspect ratios, 71.6%; adding the $\{\tfrac{1}{2}, 2\}$ boxes gives 73.7%; with all boxes but without the atrous (dilated) version of VGG-16, 74.2%; with everything, 74.3% [@ssd]. Data augmentation alone is worth 8.8 points, and removing the $\{\tfrac{1}{3}, 3\}$ boxes costs 0.6 points and the $\{\tfrac{1}{2}, 2\}$ boxes another 2.1 [@ssd]. The atrous network gives about the same accuracy and is about 20% faster [@ssd]. Table 3 removes prediction layers while keeping a similar total number of boxes: accuracy drops monotonically from 74.3% to 62.4% as fewer layers are used, so predicting from several resolutions is a main source of SSD's accuracy [@ssd].

#### Conclusion

SSD showed that dense, single-shot prediction from several feature maps with default boxes of several shapes can match two-stage accuracy at real-time speed, and that heavy augmentation is part of the detector. Its matching rule (best box plus all boxes above an IoU) is the template for YOLOv4's "several anchors per object".

#### Problems

The paper notes that SSD is much weaker on small objects than on large ones, and that higher input resolution helps but does not close the gap [@ssd]. The shallow conv4_3 features used for small objects are semantically weak, the default-box design adds hyper-parameters (scales, ratios, which layers), and duplicates are removed by NMS. FPN-style necks [@fpn] and the focal loss of [RetinaNet](#retinanet-and-the-focal-loss) addressed the weak small-object features and the foreground–background imbalance.

### RetinaNet and the focal loss

*Paper:* T.-Y. Lin, P. Goyal, R. Girshick, K. He and P. Dollár, "Focal loss for dense object detection," ICCV 2017 [@retinanet]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [box loss](#box-loss-from-iou-to-ciou) and the classification terms of the head; [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#focal-loss-and-class-imbalance) defines the focal loss.

#### Challenge

In 2017, one-stage detectors such as SSD and YOLO were faster but less accurate than two-stage detectors. The paper argues that the main reason is class imbalance during training: a dense detector evaluates about 100,000 candidate locations per image, almost all easy background, and their many small losses dominate training [@retinanet]. Two-stage detectors avoid this with proposals and sampled mini-batches (for example 1:3 positives to negatives); one-stage detectors used hard example mining [@ohem], which discards most of the data.

#### Method

**Focal loss.** For a binary classification with predicted probability $p$ for the true class and $p_t = p$ if the label is positive and $1 - p$ otherwise,

$$
\text{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t\,(1 - p_t)^{\gamma} \log(p_t),
$$

where $\gamma \ge 0$ is the focusing parameter and $\alpha_t$ a class-balancing weight ($\alpha$ for positives, $1 - \alpha$ for negatives) [@retinanet]. With $\gamma = 0$ this is the balanced cross-entropy. The factor $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$ shrinks the loss of well-classified examples: with $\gamma = 2$, an example with $p_t = 0.9$ gets 100 times less loss than with plain cross-entropy, so training focuses on hard examples. The paper uses $\gamma = 2$ and $\alpha = 0.25$ [@retinanet].

**RetinaNet.** A ResNet [@resnet] backbone with an FPN [@fpn] on levels $P_3$ to $P_7$; at each level, anchors of areas $32^2$ to $512^2$ with three aspect ratios $\{1{:}2, 1{:}1, 2{:}1\}$ and three scales $\{2^0, 2^{1/3}, 2^{2/3}\}$, so $A = 9$ anchors per location [@retinanet]. Anchors with IoU at least 0.5 with a ground-truth box are foreground, those below 0.4 are background, and the rest are ignored. Two small fully convolutional subnets run on every level with shared weights across levels: a classification subnet (four $3 \times 3$ convolutions with 256 channels and ReLU, then a $3 \times 3$ convolution with $KA$ sigmoid outputs) and a class-agnostic box subnet with the same structure and $4A$ outputs [@retinanet]. The focal loss is summed over all anchors and normalized by the number of positive anchors.

**Initialization.** If all anchors start with a probability around 0.5, the many background anchors produce a huge loss at the first iterations. The bias of the final classification layer is therefore initialized to $b = -\log((1 - \pi)/\pi)$ with prior $\pi = 0.01$, so every anchor starts with a foreground probability of about 0.01 [@retinanet].

**Inference.** Per level, at most 1,000 top-scoring predictions above a confidence of 0.05 are decoded; the predictions of all levels are merged and NMS with threshold 0.5 is applied [@retinanet].

#### Results

The best model, ResNet-101-FPN trained with scale jitter and 1.5 times longer, reaches 39.1% AP on COCO test-dev, ahead of all one-stage and two-stage detectors the paper compares with [@retinanet]. Table 1(e) gives the speed–accuracy trade-off on test-dev, timed on an Nvidia M40: ResNet-50 at 500 pixels reaches 32.5% AP in 72 ms, ResNet-101 at 800 pixels 37.8% AP in 198 ms [@retinanet].

#### Ablations

Table 1 of the paper (COCO minival, ResNet-50 at 600 pixels unless noted) [@retinanet]:

- **(a) Balanced cross-entropy:** the best $\alpha$ gives 31.1% AP ($\alpha = 0.75$).
- **(b) Focal loss:** $\gamma = 2$, $\alpha = 0.25$ gives 34.0% AP, a gain of 2.9 points with the same network; results are fairly robust to the exact values ($\gamma = 1$: 33.7%, $\gamma = 5$: 32.2%).
- **(c) Anchors:** one scale and one ratio gives 30.3% AP; three scales and three ratios 34.0%; more saturates.
- **(d) Focal loss against hard example mining** (ResNet-101): the best online hard example mining variant reaches 32.8% AP, the focal loss 36.0%.

What it proves: the loss, not the architecture, closes most of the gap to two-stage detectors.

#### Conclusion

RetinaNet showed that a dense one-stage detector can be the most accurate detector once the training loss handles the foreground–background imbalance, and it fixed the modern one-stage layout: an FPN neck and separate, shared classification and box subnets.

#### Problems

It is accurate but not real-time (198 ms for the most accurate model), its anchors and IoU thresholds are hand-designed, and NMS is still required. YOLOv3 reported that the focal loss did not help its design (see [YOLOv3](#yolov3-three-scales-and-darknet-53)), and YOLOv4 mentions the focal loss only as a way to address imbalance in its survey; quality-aware variants of the focal loss, such as the varifocal loss [@vfl], returned in [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector) and [DEIM](#deim-dense-one-to-one-matching). [FCOS](#fcos-per-pixel-anchor-free-detection) removed the anchors.

### FCOS: per-pixel anchor-free detection

*Paper:* Z. Tian, C. Shen, H. Chen and T. He, "FCOS: Fully convolutional one-stage object detection," ICCV 2019 [@fcos]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [label assignment](#label-assignment-several-anchors-per-object). [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#anchor-free-detectors-points-instead-of-boxes) introduces the regression targets and centre-ness.

#### Challenge

Anchor-based detectors such as RetinaNet depend on anchor hyper-parameters (sizes, aspect ratios, IoU thresholds) that must be tuned per dataset, produce a large number of anchors, most of them negative, and compute many IoUs during training [@fcos]. FCOS asks whether a detector can work per pixel, like semantic segmentation, with no anchors at all, and still match anchor-based accuracy.

#### Method

**Per-location regression.** Every location $(x, y)$ on a feature map (mapped back to image coordinates) that falls inside a ground-truth box is a positive and regresses the four distances to the box sides,

$$
l^* = x - x_1, \qquad t^* = y - y_1, \qquad r^* = x_2 - x, \qquad b^* = y_2 - y,
$$

where $(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2)$ is the box [@fcos]. The network predicts these distances through $\exp(s_i x)$ with a trainable scalar $s_i$ per level, so that one shared head can serve levels of different scales [@fcos].

**Multi-level assignment.** Five levels $P_3$ to $P_7$ (strides 8 to 128) come from an FPN [@fpn]. Instead of assigning anchors of different sizes to levels, FCOS limits the regression range per level: a location on level $i$ is a negative if $\max(l^*, t^*, r^*, b^*) \gt m_i$ or $\lt m_{i-1}$, with $m_2, \dots, m_7 = 0, 64, 128, 256, 512, \infty$ [@fcos]. Most overlaps are between objects of very different sizes, which then land on different levels; if a location still belongs to several boxes, the box with the smallest area is chosen [@fcos].

**Centre-ness.** Locations far from an object's centre produce poor boxes. A one-layer branch predicts

$$
\text{centerness}^* = \sqrt{\frac{\min(l^*, r^*)}{\max(l^*, r^*)} \times \frac{\min(t^*, b^*)}{\max(t^*, b^*)}},
$$

which is 1 at the centre and decays to 0 at the box edges; it is trained with binary cross-entropy and multiplied into the class score at test time, so off-centre boxes are ranked lower before NMS [@fcos].

**Loss.** $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{pos}}} \sum \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} + \frac{\lambda}{N_{\text{pos}}} \sum \mathbb{1}_{c^* \gt 0}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$, where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$ is the focal loss [@retinanet], $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$ the IoU loss of UnitBox [@unitbox], $N_{\text{pos}}$ the number of positives, $\mathbb{1}_{c^* \gt 0}$ selects positive locations and $\lambda = 1$ [@fcos].

#### Results

On COCO test-dev with single-model, single-scale testing, FCOS with ResNet-101-FPN reaches 41.5% AP against 39.1% for RetinaNet with the same backbone, and the version with ResNeXt-64x4d-101-FPN and the post-submission improvements reaches 44.7% AP [@fcos].

#### Ablations

On COCO minival with ResNet-50-FPN [@fcos]: with RetinaNet's training and testing settings, FCOS already reaches 36.3% AP against RetinaNet's 35.9%; with group normalization in the heads and an NMS threshold of 0.6 it reaches 37.1%. The **centre-ness** ablation (Table 4) is decisive: without it 33.5% AP, with centre-ness computed from the predicted regression vector also 33.5%, with the separate centre-ness branch 37.1% [@fcos]. Further "improvements" added after submission (centre-ness on the regression branch, sampling only the central part of boxes as positives, GIoU loss [@giou], normalizing targets by the stride) bring the minival result to 38.6% [@fcos]. On recall, FCOS without FPN already reaches a best possible recall of 95.55%, and with FPN it is close to the best anchor-based setting; with FPN, ambiguous locations (inside several boxes) fall to a small fraction [@fcos].

#### Conclusion

FCOS showed that anchors are not necessary: per-location distance regression, multi-level range assignment and a centre-ness score match or beat anchor-based RetinaNet with fewer hyper-parameters. Its "centre sampling" and distance parameterization reappear in YOLOX's anchor-free YOLO.

#### Problems

Its assignment is still a fixed geometric rule (inside the box, inside the level's range, optionally near the centre), it still needs NMS, and the paper's improvements list shows that much of the gain depends on training details. Prediction-aware assignment ([YOLOX](#yolox-anchor-free-yolo-with-simota), [YOLOv6](#yolov6-a-re-parameterized-industrial-detector)) and one-to-one heads ([YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments)) went further.

### CenterNet: objects as points

*Paper:* X. Zhou, D. Wang and P. Krähenbühl, "Objects as points," arXiv, 2019 [@centernet]. *Related part of YOLOv4:* [post-processing](#post-processing-nms-and-diou-nms). [DLCV 03](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-03-object-detection/#anchor-free-detectors-points-instead-of-boxes) introduces the heatmap formulation. Note the name clash: the "CenterNet" in YOLOv4's comparison tables is a different detector, based on keypoint triplets, by Duan et al. [@centernetduan].

#### Challenge

Most detectors enumerate many potential boxes and then remove duplicates with NMS, a post-processing step that is not differentiable and is hard to train end to end [@centernet]. The paper asks whether an object can be represented by a single point, its centre, so that detection becomes keypoint estimation and duplicates disappear without IoU-based NMS.

#### Method

**Heatmap of centres.** For an input of size $W \times H$ and output stride $R = 4$, the network predicts a keypoint heatmap $\hat{Y} \in [0, 1]^{\frac{W}{R} \times \frac{H}{R} \times C}$, one channel per class [@centernet]. Each ground-truth centre is splatted onto the heatmap with a Gaussian whose standard deviation adapts to the object size; overlapping Gaussians of the same class take the element-wise maximum.

**Focal loss on the heatmap.** With $Y_{xyc}$ the target heatmap,

$$
\mathcal{L}_k = \frac{-1}{N} \sum_{xyc} \begin{cases} (1 - \hat{Y}_{xyc})^{\alpha} \log(\hat{Y}_{xyc}), & Y_{xyc} = 1, \\ (1 - Y_{xyc})^{\beta}\, (\hat{Y}_{xyc})^{\alpha} \log(1 - \hat{Y}_{xyc}), & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases}
$$

where $N$ is the number of objects, $\alpha = 2$ and $\beta = 4$, and the factor $(1 - Y_{xyc})^\beta$ reduces the penalty for locations near a true centre [@centernet].

**Size and offset.** At each location the network also predicts the box size $(w, h)$ in raw pixels, trained with an L1 loss at the centre points only, and a sub-pixel offset that recovers the discretization error of the output stride, also with an L1 loss. The total loss is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{det}} = \mathcal{L}_k + \lambda_{\text{size}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{size}} + \lambda_{\text{off}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{off}}$ with $\lambda_{\text{size}} = 0.1$ and $\lambda_{\text{off}} = 1$ [@centernet]. The network outputs $C + 4$ channels per location; each output has its own $3 \times 3$ convolution, ReLU and $1 \times 1$ convolution on a shared backbone.

**Decoding without NMS.** Peaks are locations whose value is greater than or equal to their 8 neighbours, which a $3 \times 3$ max pooling finds; the top 100 peaks are kept and turned into boxes from the predicted size and offset [@centernet].

#### Results

On COCO validation, timed on a Titan Xp, CenterNet with a ResNet-18 backbone reaches 28.1% AP at 142 FPS, a mid-sized backbone 37.4% AP at 52 FPS, and the largest (a 104-layer hourglass network) 40.3% AP at 14 FPS, 42.2% with flip testing at 7.8 FPS and 45.1% with multi-scale testing at 1.4 FPS [@centernet]. The same design extends to 3D boxes and human pose by regressing more quantities at the centre.

#### Ablations

The paper checks that IoU-based NMS is unnecessary by applying it anyway: AP rises from 39.2% to 39.7% for the mid-sized backbone with flip testing and stays at 42.2% for the largest one, so NMS is not used [@centernet]. It also counts **centre collisions**: in COCO training data, 614 pairs of objects fall on the same centre point at stride 4, out of 860,001 objects, so fewer than 0.1% of objects cannot be represented [@centernet]. Further ablations cover the size-loss weight ($\lambda_{\text{size}} \le 0.1$ works well) and longer training schedules.

#### Conclusion

CenterNet showed that a detector can be a keypoint estimator: one positive per object (its centre), no anchors and no IoU-based NMS, with a simple, fast and general design. It is the clearest one-stage example of one-to-one assignment before the NMS-free YOLOs.

#### Problems

It remains an arXiv paper; objects whose centres coincide on the stride-4 grid cannot both be represented (fewer than 0.1% of COCO objects, as counted above); its size regression in raw pixels needs a hand-set loss weight; and its best accuracy uses multi-scale testing, which merges the results with NMS again [@centernet]. Training one prediction per object directly, with a learned matching rather than a geometric one, became the route of [YOLOv10](#yolov10-nms-free-training-with-dual-assignments) and the real-time DETRs.
