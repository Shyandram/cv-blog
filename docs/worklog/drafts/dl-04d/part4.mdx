### The high-order degradation pipeline, step by step

This note lists exactly what one Real-ESRGAN training input goes through, with the parameter ranges printed in the paper [@realesrgan], and contrasts it with BSRGAN's shuffled model [@bsrgan].

**Notation.** Let $y$ be an HR training patch (Real-ESRGAN uses $256 \times 256$ HR patches) and $\mathcal D_j$ one "classical" degradation round, $\mathcal D_j(z) = \mathcal C_{q_j}\bigl(\mathcal R_j(z \circledast k_j) + n_j\bigr)$, where $k_j$ is a blur kernel, $\mathcal R_j$ a random resize, $n_j$ noise and $\mathcal C_{q_j}$ JPEG at quality $q_j$. An $n$-order model applies $x = (\mathcal D_n \circ \dots \circ \mathcal D_1)(y)$; Real-ESRGAN uses $n = 2$ and describes "order" as the number of times the same operation is repeated, not a mathematical order.

**Round 1 and round 2** (the second round uses the narrower ranges in brackets):

1. **Blur.** With probabilities 0.7, 0.15 and 0.15, draw a Gaussian, a generalized Gaussian or a plateau-shaped kernel; the kernel size is drawn from $\{7, 9, \dots, 21\}$, the standard deviation from $[0.2, 3]$ ($[0.2, 1.5]$), and the shape parameter $\beta$ from $[0.5, 4]$ (generalized Gaussian) or $[1, 2]$ (plateau). The Gaussian density is $k(i, j) \propto \exp\bigl(-\tfrac12 C^\top \Sigma^{-1} C\bigr)$ with $C = [i, j]^\top$ and $\Sigma = R_\theta \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2) R_\theta^\top$, where $R_\theta$ rotates by $\theta$ and $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ are the widths along the principal axes; the generalized Gaussian raises $C^\top \Sigma^{-1} C$ to the power $\beta$, which gives flatter or more peaked kernels. With probability 0.1 the kernel is a sinc filter instead (next note). The second blur is skipped with probability 0.2.
2. **Resize.** A random up- or downscale with area, bilinear or bicubic interpolation; nearest-neighbor is excluded because it shifts the image.
3. **Noise.** Gaussian or Poisson with probability 0.5 each; Gaussian $\sigma \in [1, 30]$ ($[1, 25]$) in 8-bit units, Poisson scale in $[0.05, 3]$ ($[0.05, 2.5]$); with probability 0.4 the same noise is applied to all three channels ("gray noise").
4. **JPEG** with quality $q \in [30, 95]$, implemented with a differentiable JPEG in PyTorch.

**Final step.** Resize to the LR size, then apply a final sinc filter (probability 0.8) and a final JPEG compression in random order, because images are sometimes sharpened and then compressed, and sometimes the other way round.

**Training-pair pool.** All steps run on the GPU on whole batches, which forces one resize factor per batch; a pool of 180 pairs mixes batches to restore diversity.

**BSRGAN's model** differs in three ways [@bsrgan]. (1) **Order**: the sequence $\{B_{\text{iso}}, B_{\text{aniso}}, D_s, N_G, N_{\text{JPEG}}, N_S\}$ (isotropic and anisotropic blur, downsampling, Gaussian noise, JPEG noise, processed camera sensor noise) is randomly permuted, then a final JPEG saves the image; in a permutation the LR image can be "noisy, then downsampled, then blurred". (2) **Downsampling**: nearest (with a half-pixel-corrected Gaussian prefilter), bilinear, bicubic, or a down-up resize with an intermediate factor $a$. (3) **Camera noise**: an RGB image is mapped to RAW with an inverse camera pipeline, noise is added in RAW, and the forward pipeline (demosaicking, exposure, white balance, color matrices, tone curve, gamma) is reapplied, with parameters pooled from five real cameras; this happens with probability 0.25. Real-ESRGAN argues that "not all shuffled degradations are necessary" and that repeating the plausible order is closer to how real images are produced [@realesrgan]. Neither paper reports a direct numerical comparison of the two pipelines under the same network and training budget.

### Sinc filters and ringing

This note explains why an ideal low-pass filter produces the ringing that Real-ESRGAN teaches its network to remove.

An ideal 2-D low-pass filter keeps all spatial frequencies inside a disk of radius $\omega_c$ (radians per pixel) and removes the rest. Its impulse response, the inverse Fourier transform of that disk, is the radially symmetric "jinc" function

$$
k(i, j) = \frac{\omega_c}{2\pi \sqrt{i^2 + j^2}}\, J_1\!\left(\omega_c \sqrt{i^2 + j^2}\right), \qquad k(0, 0) = \frac{\omega_c^2}{4\pi},
$$

where $(i, j)$ is the pixel offset from the kernel center, $J_1$ is the Bessel function of the first kind of order 1, and the center value is the limit as the radius goes to zero; Real-ESRGAN calls it a sinc filter and uses this formula [@realesrgan]. The kernel has a positive center surrounded by alternating negative and positive rings (Figure 4, left). Convolving a step edge with it produces an overshoot on the bright side and an undershoot on the dark side, followed by decaying ripples: the 2-D version of the Gibbs phenomenon of truncated Fourier series. In our run with $\omega_c = \pi/3$ and a $21 \times 21$ kernel, the edge overshoots by about 0.10 above 1 and undershoots by about 0.10 below 0 (our numbers; a Gaussian blur of the same edge has no overshoot at all).

Right: profiles across a step edge; the black sharp step, a blue Gaussian-blurred ramp without overshoot, and a red sinc-filtered curve that dips below zero before the edge, overshoots above one after it, and ripples." caption="Figure 4 — Sinc (jinc) kernels and their effect on a step edge. Why this matters for SR: a network trained only on Gaussian-blurred inputs has never seen halos and treats them as real edges, so it sharpens them further (Real-ESRGAN shows this in its sinc ablation). Lower $\omega_c$ gives wider rings; Real-ESRGAN draws the cutoff at random so the network sees halos of many widths. The pipeline snippet in the main text draws $\omega_c \in [\pi/3, \pi]$, our own choice.

### Why the kernel is hard to identify

This note shows that the kernel and the image cannot be separated from one LR image without assumptions, which is why every blind method needs a prior on one or both.

**Two exact ambiguities.** With $y = (x \circledast k)\downarrow_s$:

1. **Shift.** Shifting the scene by one pixel and the kernel by one pixel in the opposite direction leaves $x \circledast k$, and so $y$, unchanged: $\bigl(x(\cdot - \delta)\bigr) \circledast \bigl(k(\cdot + \delta)\bigr) = x \circledast k$ for any offset $\delta$. SRMD uses exactly this argument to explain why a blind network trained on many kernels averages over shifted HR candidates and over-smooths [@srmd]. KernelGAN's regularizer that keeps the kernel's center of mass at the center removes this ambiguity by convention [@kernelgan].
2. **Blur trade.** Convolution is associative, so a soft scene $x \circledast g$ blurred by $k$ equals a sharp scene $x$ blurred by $g \circledast k$. For Gaussians, $g_{\sigma_1} \circledast g_{\sigma_2} = g_{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}}$, so "a defocused photo of a sharp poster" and "a sharp photo of a soft painting" can produce the same LR image.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi
from skimage import data, img_as_float64

x = img_as_float64(data.camera())
s = 4

def lr(img, sigma, shift=0):
    """Blur with a Gaussian kernel centred at `shift` pixels, then keep every s-th pixel."""
    k = ndi.gaussian_filter(img, sigma, mode="wrap")
    return np.roll(k, shift, axis=1)[::s, ::s]

# 1) Shift ambiguity: move the scene one pixel right and the kernel one pixel left.
a = lr(x, 1.5)
b = lr(np.roll(x, 1, axis=1), 1.5, shift=-1)
print("shifted scene + shifted kernel, max |difference|:", f"{np.abs(a - b).max():.1e}")

# 2) Blur ambiguity: a soft scene with a sharp kernel vs a sharp scene with a wide kernel.
soft_scene = ndi.gaussian_filter(x, 1.0, mode="wrap")
c = lr(soft_scene, 1.2)
d = lr(x, np.hypot(1.0, 1.2))                     # Gaussian widths add in quadrature
print("soft scene + sigma 1.2 vs sharp scene + sigma 1.56, max |difference|:", f"{np.abs(c - d).max():.1e}")
```

```text
shifted scene + shifted kernel, max |difference|: 0.0e+00
soft scene + sigma 1.2 vs sharp scene + sigma 1.56, max |difference|: 2.2e-05
```

The first difference is exactly zero; the second is at the level of the discretization error of sampled Gaussians.

**What breaks the tie.** Each family resolves the ambiguity with a different assumption. Explicit methods restrict $k$ (Gaussian-shaped, centered, summing to 1) and rely on the network's learned image prior to prefer sharp, natural $x$. KernelGAN assumes that natural images are self-similar across scales, so the right kernel is the one that preserves the patch distribution when the image is shrunk [@kernelgan], [@michaeli]. Implicit methods never estimate $k$ at all; they learn a direct mapping whose output is, in effect, the most plausible sharp image for the training distribution of kernels. The ambiguity is the deep reason why blind SR cannot be solved from the data alone, and why a method's assumptions decide which answer it gives.
