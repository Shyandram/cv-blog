## Real-world SR with generative priors

**Plain version.** The networks above learn what photos look like from a few thousand training images. A large text-to-image diffusion model has seen billions. Since about 2023, the strongest real-world SR methods keep such a model (almost) frozen and add a small network that shows it the LR image, so that it "paints in" detail that fits.

### Why a generative prior

When the input is badly damaged, most of the HR detail is simply not in the LR image, and a regression network can only return the blurry average of all plausible answers. A generative model instead returns one plausible answer. [DLCV 04C](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04c-generative-restoration/#the-perceptiondistortion-trade-off) explains why this costs PSNR, how diffusion sampling works ([diffusion in one page](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04c-generative-restoration/#diffusion-in-one-page)), and why a strong prior can invent content ([the hallucination problem](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04c-generative-restoration/#the-hallucination-problem)). Here we concentrate on what is specific to SR: how each method feeds the LR image to the prior, what extra condition it uses, and what knob it offers between fidelity and realism.

All methods in this section train on synthetic pairs from the Real-ESRGAN pipeline or a similar one, and most build on Stable Diffusion, a latent diffusion model: an autoencoder compresses a $512 \times 512$ image into a $64 \times 64$ latent (a factor of 8 per side), and the denoising network works in that latent space [@ldm]. Many adapt ControlNet, which attaches a trainable copy of the denoiser's encoder to a frozen model through zero-initialized convolutions [@controlnet].

### What each method conditions on

| Method | Prior | How the LR image enters | Extra condition | Fidelity knob | Steps (as reported) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| StableSR [@stablesr] | Stable Diffusion 2.1, frozen | Time-aware encoder + spatial feature transform (SFT) layers in every residual block | none (null prompt) | Controllable feature wrapping weight $w$ in the decoder | 200 (DDPM) |
| DiffBIR [@diffbir] | Stable Diffusion 2.1, frozen | First removed by a restoration network, then the *cleaned* image enters an IRControlNet | negative prompt | Region-adaptive restoration guidance scale | 50 |
| PASD [@pasd] | Stable Diffusion | Degradation-removal module and pixel-aware cross-attention | high-level semantic features and captions | Adjustable noise schedule | 20 |
| SeeSR [@seesr] | Stable Diffusion 2, frozen | ControlNet on the LR latent; LR latent also mixed into the starting noise | Degradation-aware tags (hard prompts) and tagger embeddings (soft prompts) | — | 50 |
| SUPIR [@supir] | SDXL (2.6 billion parameters), frozen | Degradation-robust encoder, trimmed ControlNet with ZeroSFT connectors | Captions from a multimodal LLM; negative-quality prompts | Restoration-guided sampling $\tau_r$ | 100 (EDM) |
| ResShift [@resshift] | Trained from scratch (latent space of a VQGAN) | The chain *starts* at the LR image and shifts the residual | none | Noise schedule $\kappa$, $p$ | 15 |

Step counts are the defaults reported in each paper; the SeeSR supplementary material times them side by side on one V100 GPU for a $128 \to 512$ task: 18.70 s for StableSR (200 steps), 5.85 s for DiffBIR (50), 6.07 s for PASD (20), 7.24 s for SeeSR (50) and 1.12 s for ResShift (15), against 0.09 s for Real-ESRGAN [@seesr].

Three design ideas recur:

- **Conditioning strength must vary with the noise level.** StableSR adds a time embedding to its condition encoder and finds that the encoder supplies its strongest guidance around a signal-to-noise ratio of about $5 \times 10^{-2}$, where image content is formed [@stablesr].
- **Do not show the prior the raw degradation.** DiffBIR observes that a diffusion model conditioned directly on noisy or compressed inputs turns the artifacts into content, so it removes the degradation first with an ordinary restoration network and only then regenerates detail [@diffbir]. SUPIR fine-tunes its autoencoder's encoder to be robust to degradations for the same reason [@supir].
- **Semantics help where pixels are gone.** PASD and SeeSR extract text from the LR image and feed it to the prior; SeeSR trains its tagger to give the same tags for degraded and clean images, so that a blurry ship is not "recognized" and then painted as a bird [@pasd], [@seesr]. SUPIR uses a multimodal large language model to caption the input and lets users steer restoration with text [@supir].

### Diffusion transformers and newer priors

**Plain version.** The denoiser inside the prior is changing from a U-Net to a transformer, and SR methods follow.

DreamClear builds a restorer on a diffusion transformer (DiT), curates one million synthetic high-quality training images with a generative pipeline, and mixes restoration experts with token-wise degradation priors [@dreamclear]. DiT4SR feeds the LR latent into the attention of a large DiT as its own stream, so that the LR guidance evolves with the diffusion process instead of being injected once, plus a cross-stream convolution for local detail [@dit4sr]. The trend is the same as in text-to-image generation: bigger and better priors, with the conditioning moved deeper inside the model.

### Fidelity, hallucination and the knob

**Plain version.** A stronger prior paints more convincingly, and paints more that was not there. Every method therefore exposes a knob between "stay close to the input" and "look real".

The knobs differ in form but not in purpose: StableSR's $w \in [0, 1]$ blends encoder features of the LR image into the decoder [@stablesr]; DiffBIR's guidance scale $s$ pulls each intermediate estimate toward the cleaned image, more strongly in flat regions [@diffbir]; SUPIR's $\tau_r$ interpolates each step's prediction toward the LR latent with a weight that decays during sampling [@supir]; ResShift's $p$ controls how fast the residual is shifted [@resshift]. In each paper, moving the knob toward fidelity raises PSNR and SSIM and lowers no-reference scores such as CLIPIQA and MUSIQ, which is the perception–distortion trade-off in practice. Two hallucination patterns are specific to SR: **semantic errors**, where a degraded object is reconstructed as a different object (SeeSR's examples of a ship turned into a bird or a fish), and **texture invention in flat regions**, where the random starting noise of a sampler is turned into detail on sky or walls (the reason for SeeSR's LR-embedding start and for SUPIR's restoration-guided sampling) [@seesr], [@supir]. Small text and small faces remain difficult for all of them; StableSR, SeeSR and OSEDiff each name these as limitations [@stablesr], [@seesr], [@osediff].

### Fewer steps

**Plain version.** Fifty or two hundred network evaluations per image is too slow for a phone or for video. For SR, the LR image already fixes most of the content, so a diffusion model does not need to start from pure noise, and a few steps, or one, can be enough.

ResShift's 15-step chain is the bridge: it starts from the LR image instead of noise [@resshift]. Distilled and one-step methods go further: SinSR distils ResShift into a single step [@sinsr], and OSEDiff fine-tunes Stable Diffusion with low-rank adapters to map the LR latent to the HR latent in one step, regularized by variational score distillation [@osediff]. These one-step and few-step methods, and how their speed is measured, have their own chapter: [DLCV 04E](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04e-one-step-diffusion-super-resolution/).

## Evaluation in the blind and real-world setting

**Plain version.** On real photos there is no ground truth, so you cannot compute an error. You can test on synthetic damage where the truth is known, score real outputs with a model of human judgement, or ask people. Each answer is partial, and they often disagree.

### Full-reference metrics on synthetic test sets

Blind-SR papers in the explicit family use fixed synthetic protocols so that kernels are known: **Gaussian8** (eight isotropic Gaussian kernels per scale, applied before bicubic downsampling, defined in IKC [@ikc]) and **DIV2KRK** (the 100 DIV2K validation images [@div2k], each with its own random anisotropic, noise-perturbed kernel, defined in KernelGAN [@kernelgan]). Real-world methods use synthetic test sets made with their own training pipeline, for example 3,000 DIV2K-validation crops degraded with the Real-ESRGAN pipeline in StableSR, SeeSR and OSEDiff [@stablesr], [@seesr], [@osediff], or BSRGAN's DIV2K4D with four degradation types [@bsrgan]. On these sets PSNR and SSIM measure distortion, and LPIPS [@lpips] and DISTS [@dists] measure perceptual distance to the reference. A caveat: a test set made with a method's own training pipeline favors that method.

For captured pairs (RealSR, DRealSR) the same metrics apply, but misalignment between the two zoom levels lowers every method's PSNR and rewards blur; KernelGAN gives the same reason for not using the NTIRE 2018 blind-SR data [@kernelgan].

### No-reference metrics

**Plain version.** A no-reference (NR) metric predicts how good an image looks without seeing the original. It is a learned or hand-made model of human opinion, and it can be fooled.

The common NR metrics in real-world SR papers are **NIQE** (distance of an image's natural-scene statistics from those of pristine images; lower is better) [@niqe], **MUSIQ** (a multi-scale transformer trained on human ratings; higher is better) [@musiq], **MANIQA** (an attention-based model that won an NTIRE 2022 IQA challenge track; higher is better) [@maniqa] and **CLIPIQA** (similarity of the image's CLIP embedding to the prompts "Good photo." and "Bad photo."; higher is better) [@clipiqa]. Their definitions and validation are in [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04-image-restoration/#no-reference-metrics). FID, a distance between feature distributions of a set of outputs and a set of real images, is also reported on paired sets [@fid].

The snippet shows in miniature why such scores must be read with care. It compares a blurry output, an unsharp-masked output and a blurry output with added fine noise, and scores each with PSNR and with a deliberately crude NR "crispness" score, the mean gradient magnitude. Real NR metrics are far better than this, but they share its basic weakness: they score the look of an image, not its correctness.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi
from skimage import data, img_as_float64
from skimage.color import rgb2gray

hr = img_as_float64(rgb2gray(data.astronaut()))
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def psnr(a, b):
    return 10 * np.log10(1.0 / np.mean((a - np.clip(b, 0, 1)) ** 2))

def crispness(img):
    """A crude no-reference score: mean gradient magnitude (higher = 'sharper looking')."""
    return np.mean(np.hypot(ndi.sobel(img, 0), ndi.sobel(img, 1)))

smooth = ndi.gaussian_filter(hr, 1.5)                               # a cautious, blurry restoration
candidates = {
    "smooth (posterior-mean-like)": smooth,
    "unsharp-masked x2": smooth + 2.0 * (smooth - ndi.gaussian_filter(smooth, 2)),
    "smooth + fake fine texture": smooth + 0.04 * rng.standard_normal(hr.shape),
}
for name, img in candidates.items():
    img = np.clip(img, 0, 1)
    print(f"{name:28s} PSNR {psnr(hr, img):6.2f} dB | crispness {crispness(img):.3f}")
print(f"{'ground truth':28s} PSNR    -     | crispness {crispness(hr):.3f}")
```

```text
smooth (posterior-mean-like) PSNR  26.82 dB | crispness 0.170
unsharp-masked x2            PSNR  28.42 dB | crispness 0.259
smooth + fake fine texture   PSNR  24.48 dB | crispness 0.262
ground truth                 PSNR    -     | crispness 0.246
```

Both "sharpened" outputs score *crisper than the ground truth* (our toy numbers), and the one with fake texture is the crispest of all while having the lowest PSNR. PSNR, in turn, prefers the unsharp-masked output only because sharpening partly undid the blur; it would also prefer a slightly blurry correct image to a sharp image with shifted texture. ### Why rankings disagree

The published tables show the same tension at full scale:

- **NR metrics versus visual quality.** On BSRGAN's 20 real images (RealSRSet), BSRGAN has the worst NIQE of the methods it compares (5.60, against 3.99 to 4.95 for the others), although its authors find its results visually better; they conclude that these NR metrics "do not always match perceptual visual quality" and should be updated for new degradation types [@bsrgan].
- **Fidelity versus realism.** In StableSR's comparison on DIV2K validation crops, StableSR has the best FID, CLIPIQA and MUSIQ but a lower PSNR (23.26 dB) than several GAN methods (up to 24.62 dB) [@stablesr]. In SUPIR's synthetic tests SUPIR leads every NR metric and trails in PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS, and its authors show cases where the metrics prefer a visibly worse result [@supir].
- **NR metrics versus each other.** In OSEDiff's tables, multi-step methods (SeeSR, PASD) score better on NIQE, MUSIQ and MANIQA, while OSEDiff is better on LPIPS, DISTS, FID and CLIPIQA [@osediff]. Which method "wins" depends on the metric chosen.
- **Prompts move NR scores.** StableSR shows that classifier-free guidance with negative prompts raises CLIPIQA and MUSIQ, and that too strong guidance over-sharpens [@stablesr]. A score that a sampling trick can raise is not an independent judge.

Rule of thumb: report at least one full-reference metric on a synthetic or captured set, two NR metrics from different families on real images, and a user study for headline claims; state the test resolution and crop protocol, because methods trained at $512 \times 512$ behave differently on small crops (StableSR reports that SUPIR does poorly on its small crops but better at large resolutions [@stablesr]).

### User studies and challenges

User studies remain the final arbiter: StableSR collected 1,400 votes from 35 participants on 40 real images [@stablesr]; SeeSR measured how often 20 participants confused its output with the ground truth (a confusion rate of 38.6%) and how often they chose it as the best real-world result (57.1%) [@seesr]. Good practice (hidden method names, randomized order, enough images and raters) is described in [DLCV 04](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04-image-restoration/#user-studies-and-paired-comparisons).

The challenges trace the field's shift from known to unknown degradations:

- **NTIRE 2018** SR had one bicubic track and three tracks with realistic unknown downgrading operators that simulated a camera pipeline, learnable from provided training pairs [@ntire2018].
- **NTIRE 2019** Real Image SR was the first track on captured pairs, introducing RealSR (DSLR images at two focal lengths) [@ntire2019].
- **AIM 2019** and **NTIRE 2020** Real-World SR provided no pairs at all, only source-domain LR images (and in one track unpaired high-quality targets), and ranked entries by perceptual quality, in 2020 through a human study [@aim2019], [@ntire2020].
- **AIM 2020** Real Image SR used captured pairs at ×2, ×3 and ×4 and ranked by PSNR and SSIM [@aim2020].
- **NTIRE 2025** ×4 SR returned to bicubic inputs but added a perceptual track ranked by a perceptual score next to the PSNR-ranked restoration track [@ntire2025].

## Failure modes

**Plain version.** Real-world SR fails in two directions: it misreads the damage (and so removes too little or too much), or it trusts its prior too much (and so invents).

- **Out-of-distribution degradations.** A synthesis-trained model can only remove damage its pipeline could produce; Real-ESRGAN lists unknown, out-of-distribution degradations among its limitations and notes that it may even amplify them [@realesrgan].
- **Kernel misestimation.** In explicit methods a wrong kernel gives the blur or ringing of Figure 1; spatially varying blur breaks the assumption of a single kernel.
- **GAN textures and artifacts.** Real-ESRGAN reports twisted lines from aliasing and unpleasant GAN artifacts [@realesrgan]; BSRGAN reports "bubble" artifacts in texture regions [@bsrgan]; LDL was designed to suppress such artifacts [@ldl].
- **Semantic hallucination.** Diffusion-prior methods can reconstruct the wrong object or invent texture in flat regions; degraded small text and faces are the hardest cases [@seesr], [@stablesr].
- **Color shifts.** Diffusion models can shift colors; StableSR corrects them by matching each output channel's mean and variance to the input, or by replacing the output's low-frequency wavelet band with the input's [@stablesr].
- **Tiling seams and resolution effects.** Models trained on $512 \times 512$ crops need tiling for large images; StableSR shows block inconsistency without its aggregation sampling, and performance depends on the test resolution [@stablesr].
- **Metric gaming.** Sharpening, added grain or prompt tricks can raise NR scores without improving fidelity (Figure 3).

A cheap sanity check works for every method: degrade the SR output back to the input size with a plausible model and compare it with the input. Large differences in flat regions or on text mean that the method changed content the input did measure. As [DLCV 04C](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04c-generative-restoration/#the-hallucination-problem) explains, passing this check is necessary but not sufficient.

## Modern view

### Survey papers

**Plain version.** Two surveys define the vocabulary of this chapter, one for blind SR and one for real-world SR; a diffusion-SR survey and a broad recent SR survey extend them.

- **Liu et al., "Blind Image Super-Resolution: A Survey and Beyond" (TPAMI)** [@liu_survey]. *Covers*: blind SR methods, datasets and earlier challenges, with a comparison on synthetic and real images. *Taxonomy*: explicit modelling with external data (image-specific adaptation without kernel estimation, such as SRMD, UDVD and USRNet, and with kernel estimation, such as IKC and DAN), explicit modelling with a single image (internal statistics: KernelGAN, ZSSR), and implicit modelling with external data (data-distribution learning with GANs: CinCGAN, FSSR and others). *Takeaways*: the taxonomy reveals a gap, implicit modelling from a single image; data-distribution methods are data-hungry and produce GAN artifacts; fair comparison is hard because methods assume different degradations.
- **Chen et al., "Real-World Single Image Super-Resolution: A Brief Review" (Information Fusion 2022)** [@chen_review]. *Covers*: real-world datasets (RealSR, City100, DRealSR and others) and metrics, with comparisons of quality and speed. *Taxonomy*: degradation-modelling-based (IKC, DAN, KernelGAN), image-pairs-based, domain-translation-based (CinCGAN) and self-learning-based (ZSSR). *Takeaway*: results on synthetic data overestimate how well methods super-resolve real images.
- **Moser et al., "Diffusion Models, Image Super-Resolution, and Everything" (TNNLS 2025)** [@moser]. *Covers*: diffusion foundations and SR methods by input domain, conditioning, guidance and corruption space. *Takeaways*: realism has improved greatly; compute, comparability across papers and color shifts remain open.
- **Zhang et al., "Deep-Learning-Empowered Super Resolution: A Comprehensive Survey and Future Prospects" (Proceedings of the IEEE 2025)** [@zhang_survey]. *Covers*: single-image, video, stereo and light-field SR, with a taxonomy by backbone (CNN, transformer, GAN, diffusion), datasets, evaluation protocols and complexity. *Use*: a wide index into the literature, including the real-world and diffusion methods of this chapter.

### State of the art, 2023–2026

1. **Generative priors are the default for real-world SR.** StableSR, DiffBIR, PASD, SeeSR and SUPIR attach a frozen text-to-image model through encoders, ControlNet variants and SFT-style connectors [@stablesr], [@diffbir], [@pasd], [@seesr], [@supir]; DiT-based restorers such as DreamClear and DiT4SR follow the move of generators to transformers [@dreamclear], [@dit4sr].
2. **Semantics enter as conditions**: degradation-aware tags, captions from multimodal models, and text control [@seesr], [@supir].
3. **Synthetic pipelines are still the data source**: almost every method trains on BSRGAN- or Real-ESRGAN-style degradations, with the training images scaled up (SUPIR reports 20 million images with text; DreamClear one million curated synthetic images) [@supir], [@dreamclear].
4. **Speed is the main engineering effort**: from 200 steps to 15 (ResShift) and to one step (SinSR, OSEDiff and later methods; see [DLCV 04E](/en/learn/deep-learning/dl-04e-one-step-diffusion-super-resolution/)) [@resshift], [@sinsr], [@osediff].
5. **Explicit blind SR continues in the classical setting** with better estimators and representations (DCLS, KDSR), benchmarked on Gaussian8 and DIV2KRK [@dcls], [@kdsr].

### Open problems

- **Faithfulness.** No metric reliably separates "sharp and correct" from "sharp and invented"; first attempts score hallucination directly with multimodal models [@hallucination].
- **Degradations beyond the pipeline.** Spatially varying blur, camera processing, repeated social-media compression and video codecs are only partly covered by synthetic pipelines.
- **Evaluation without ground truth.** NR metrics disagree with each other and with people, can be raised by sampling tricks, and were not designed for diffusion outputs [@bsrgan], [@stablesr].
- **Cost.** Large priors (billions of parameters) and multi-step sampling remain far from phone budgets.
- **Implicit modelling from a single image**, the gap identified by Liu et al., is still largely open [@liu_survey].

## Key takeaways

- **Blind** SR has unknown parameters of a known degradation family; **real-world** SR has an unknown family too, usually with no ground truth.
- Methods differ in whether they model the degradation **explicitly** (a kernel and noise level, given, estimated or learned per image) or **implicitly** (a network absorbs it from data), and in whether they use external data or only the test image.
- A wrong kernel is punished asymmetrically: too wide gives ringing and a large PSNR loss, too narrow gives blur. IKC and DAN exploit this signature to correct their estimates; KernelGAN estimates the kernel from the image's own patch recurrence.
- **Degradation synthesis** (BSRGAN's shuffle, Real-ESRGAN's second-order pipeline with sinc filters) is the most widely used data source: one network trained on a very wide damage distribution works on many real photos but not on damage the pipeline cannot produce.
- **Generative priors** (StableSR, DiffBIR, PASD, SeeSR, SUPIR, ResShift) give the most realistic results; they differ in how the LR image and semantic information enter, and each exposes a fidelity knob. They lose PSNR and can hallucinate.
- Evaluate with full-reference metrics on synthetic or captured sets, two kinds of NR metrics on real images, and a user study; expect the rankings to disagree.
